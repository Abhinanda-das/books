# Astrology: Language of the Cosmos

## Astrology: Language of the Cosmos

by James Braha

In a 1947 edition of *Astrological Magazine,* India’s most famous astrologer, B.V. Raman, noted an ominous planetary conjunction occurring around November of that year. Accordingly, he predicted: “The loss, by assassination...of a respected mass leader of this country.” Only two months later, on January 30th, 1948, the prediction came true, and tragically, marked the day of Mahatma Gandhi’s assassination.

In a book titled *Rolling Thunder: the Coming Earth Changes* by J. E. Jochmans (not to be confused with the book on the American Indian), another interesting astrological forecast was made. It concerned the “break” of the United States “Presidential death cycle.” The cycle, for those who do not know, is a series of Presidential deaths affecting every U.S. leader elected during the Jupiter-Saturn conjunction. The seven deaths began with William Harrison in 1840 and have continued every 20 years until 1980. It is important to note that out of the remaining 27 Presidents not elected during the conjunction, only one died in office.

Jochmans states in his book, published in October 1980, before the Presidential election, “Only since 1840 has the conjunction been taking place in an earth sign. . . and will continue to do so until the year 2080. But there is one important exception: in 1980, Jupiter and Saturn will be meeting in an air sign, in Libra. For this reason, astrologers are predicting that the man elected in 1980 will not die in office this time. In fact, they go on to forecast that the Presidential winner is destined to become one of the most universally popular leaders in our nation’s history.” An especially accurate assessment of our “polls topper” Ronald Reagan.

Regarding the authenticity of astrology, such examples are very likely superfluous for those who have actually sat down with an experienced astrologer and had their *jyotish* (Hindu astrology) birthchart analyzed. Such individuals have, no doubt, been struck with the power and profundity of the heavenly language. Accurate personal predictions received from an astrologer have a way of piercing straight to the core of a person’s consciousness and impacting his or her perception of the subject forever. In 1982, I sat in a tiny, dark room in Benares, India, and listened to the first *jyotish* interpretation of my life. After hearing Shastri, the astrologer, tell me much I already knew about myself, about my life as a metaphysician and teacher, he confidently made a very startling prediction.

He said, “You are going to write some books.” “What?” I replied, both shocked and flattered at the same time. “You are going to write some books,” he said again. At this point, my mind felt jarred as I considered the matter. The fact is I was then 31 years old, had only one semester of college English, and had even been refused admittance to one university because of poor high school English grades! The rest of our conversation went as follows: “Are you sure? I mean, are you absolutely positive?” “My dear friend, astrology is not an absolute science.” “Ahhhhh,” I sighed. “But,” asserted Shastri, “you WILL write books!”

I had no idea at the time that my first book, a text on Hindu predictive astrology for Westerners, would be sitting on bookstore shelves even in Delhi and Bombay some three years later. Or, that at the age of 38, I would be halfway through the writing of my third book. So as time passed, Shastri’s clear-cut forecast made a rather deep impression on my psyche as a demonstration of the veracity of astrology.

And yet, despite the three astrological examples given so far—two objectively verifiable and one provable only to myself—is it truly wise to “believe” in astrology? Is this the domain to which astrology belongs—belief or disbelief? It may seem, at this point, a subtlety or play of semantics, but the language of the heavens is a practical discipline: something to experience,

not “believe” in. It is not a conceptual, ritualistic, or devotional subject. Putting astrology into the context of “belief” and “disbelief” has done more damage to this great system of wisdom and guidance than perhaps any other process, as it has served to give credence to the opinions of anyone and everyone, especially to those who have virtually no experience at all with the subject. Sir Isaac Newton, who studied astrology, is reported to have replied to the chiding of the astronomer nonbeliever Edmund Halley (of Halley’s Comet fame), “I have studied the subject, Mr. Halley, and you have not.” But the worst effect of this belief-system business is to obscure the real nature and profundity of the star language and lump it in with static, objectively verifiable, “closed system” sciences. Astrology, for better or worse, is a mystical discipline which, though certainly comprising scientific elements, goes far beyond such limitations. The cosmology of the star language is as complex and intricate as life itself, and contains the key to the philosophically plaguing, paradoxical issue of free will versus predestiny. But more of this later.

Certainly, anyone who has visited a competent professional astrologer knows that the star language works. It is not without cause that the discipline has existed in virtually every culture since the beginning of time. Millions, over the centuries, have experienced its authenticity. Moreover, the practice has to its credit some of the greatest individuals in history: Galileo, Kepler, Carl Jung, and from the Bible, Abraham, and the three wise men who heralded the coming of Christ, to name but a few.

However, the real meaning and significance of astrology is more complex than most people are aware. The term *jyotish* means “light,” and in our context refers to the planets and heavenly bodies. Knowledge of the stars was cognized or perceived through meditation by ancient enlightened seers, and forms one of the chapters of the Vedas. But what exactly is astrology and how does it work? The answer is plain to astrologers: it is a language. A language of nature, of life, of the cosmos which uses stars and planets as words or symbols.

At the exact instant of each person’s entry into the world, the destiny, potential, and direction of the soul are mapped out in detail and openly proclaimed to anyone and everyone capable of listening. The proclamation is given through the position of the planets and their interrelationships. This of course forms one’s astrological birthchart or blueprint. Those who know the language will find in each discourse (birthchart) nothing less than a “mirror” of a particular human destiny.

One may *experience* the veracity of all this firsthand by actually decoding and interpreting the language, or by visiting an experienced, professional *jyotishi* (astrologer). But as far as *belief* in it is concerned, Swami Sri Yukteswar said to Yogananda, who as a youth did not believe in astrology: “It is not a question of *belief;*  the scientific attitude one should take on the subject is whether it is *true.* The law of gravitation worked as efficiently before Newton as after him. The cosmos would be fairly chaotic if its laws could not operate without the sanction of human belief.”

The essential problem with whether or not to “believe” in astrology is that the question is inappropriate. Astrology is a language, nothing more and nothing less. People do not believe or disbelieve in languages. In 38 years of living, I have never heard anyone ask, “Do you believe in French?” The question is, “Do you *understand* French?”! And so it is, or should be, with astrology. What may be considered is that since the beginning of time, there has existed a miraculous universal language which certain individuals have devoted their lives to understanding and interpreting. Interestingly, most people, whether admirers of astrology or not, believe in some form of destiny; that certain aspects of their lives are fated or “meant to be.”

But in grasping the real meaning of astrology, it is crucial to dispel some misunderstandings. First of all, planets and stars do not, as far as we know, *cause* effects. If they did, astrologers would have labeled astrology an empirical mundane science long ago, and proved it by demonstrating that each particular planetary configuration causes the *exact same* effect in all humans with the aspect in his or her birthchart. Fortunately, for those of us who enjoy the experience of choice in our lives, it does not work quite that way. Although each planetary aspect or birthchart feature is consistent and reliable in its meaning, there are clearly environmental and free will factors to be taken into account.

For example, let us take the case of John Lennon, whose threat of death could have been seen by the most elementary of Hindu astrologers. Lennon’s birthchart is so blatantly clear in describing the time frame of his death that even without attempting an intricate astronomical discourse, the reader can gain some insight into the functioning of astrology. One of the greatest features of *jyotish* is what is called the *dasha*  system of prediction. A *dasha* is a period of time during which one’s life is said to be influenced by a particular planet. *Dashas* are also divided into *bhuktis* or subperiods of other planets which “color” or further define a period.

John Lennon began a 16-year Jupiter *dasha* in April of 1978. Unfortunately, Jupiter is the worst or most “afflicted” planet in his chart. The problem is that Jupiter, even though it is by nature a greatly benefic planet, was conjunct *within one degree* (out of 360!) with the greatly malefic planet Saturn. As if this were not bad enough, both planets were posited in the eighth “house,” the space representing death in the star language Further, out of the entire 360-degree zodiac, Saturn was in its absolutely worst position (the 20th degree of Aries). Therefore, during his Jupiter period, great misery was easily predictable.

However, the most ominous and positively frightening time was the two-and-a-half-year period of Jupiter *dasha* and Saturn *bhukti,* the Jupiter-Saturn period and subperiod. This occurred between May, 1980 and December, 1982, and gave the results of two afflicted planets in the space representing death. Lennon died in late 1980, as any respectable *jyotishi* would expect. But does this mean that every person born with a chart like John Lennon died in that two-and-a-half-year period? Or that everyone with such a chart became world famous in his or her twenties? No, of course not. For the simple reason that astrology, despite its great efficiency and predictive accuracy, is a language; a language conveying a schedule of likelihoods, potentials, and probabilities. It is neither a cause-and-effect system nor an empirical science in the conventional sense of the word.

Individuals born at a similar time and place to John Lennon could have, and probably did, have certain similar experiences. But free will and environmental factors would also play their part. Lennon, being a public figure, was obviously at greater risk than others with birthcharts similar to his. As an astrologer, I have seen several people live through astrological periods which blatantly indicated potential death. One was a relative of mine who, during the year-or-so-long period, was continually being harassed by a businessman threatening to kill him. Another was a spiritual seeker who arrived at my door with bandages on his head, having taken a terrible fall while jogging. Yet another was a man who lived through a fire in his home. So free will and environment do play their part.

Even ancient astrological scriptures infer the possibilty of free will when they advise *upayes* or antidotes to planetary afflictions in order to ameliorate negative influences revealed through one’s birthchart. John Lennon, had he visited a *jyotish* astrologer before 1980, would have been advised to secure a fine quality yellow sapphire, two carats or more. (Lennon is said to have been living with a psychic and astrologer at the time he was assassinated, but this was a Western astrologer, not a practitioner of *jyotish.* Western astrology excels in personality, not events and circumstances.) He would also have been told to chant a specific mantra for the planet Jupiter 16,000 times, and to arrange for the performance of a *yajna* (pronounced jug-ya), which is a Vedic spiritual ceremony where Hindu brahmans chant mantras and perform devotional rituals. This would have been the most powerful and significant of remedies. Had Lennon taken these precautions, I dare say he might still be alive today.

At any rate, if we are to come to a significant understanding of the star language, we must now consider just how and why the process works. In doing so, it will become clear how astrology can work perfectly, even absolutely, and yet not fit into the category of an empirical, objectively verifiable science—a cosmic or intuitive science, yes; but a science in the technical sense, never. What follows demands a bit of rigorous thinking and openness to the concept of paradox, for in analyzing the star language, we are about to dissect the mechanics of how free will and predestiny exist perfectly, side by side.

I might add that much of the reason for my first journey to the Far East was precisely to deal with the issue of fate. I had, at the time, been studying Western astrology for two or three years, and needed some definitive answers in the matter. How was I to decide whether or not to spend my life astrologizing for society without knowing whether or not the destinies I would be describing were fated, likely, or merely possibilities? My hope was that in my travels to Israel, India, and Nepal, I would meet enough gurus and religious authorities to come to an understanding. As it turned out, every religious proponent I encountered said virtually the same thing: Free will exists, absolutely and without question. Paradoxically however, when asked about the omniscience of God, everyone said that the Supreme Being knows everything that has, does, and ever will occur in creation. In other words, free will and predestiny both exist, with no infringement upon each other.

Incidentally, this does not mean that certain aspects of our lives are predestined while others are somehow not. The truth is closer to: we choose with our free will, and that choice, once made, is predestined, *even though our free will is indeed real*. Being paradoxical, this of course does not make sense to the mortal mind. That is to be expected. But the matter will become clearer as we continue. Bear in mind that the star language, in describing and foretelling human destinies, plays out the role of fate. Humankind, in our subjective experience of choice, represents the agent of free will.

Within existence there is, necessarily, language. Within language there is, always, existence. The two actually cannot be separated. What is meant here by language is not merely words or symbols, but also the underlying purpose—the ability to distinguish. For those familiar with Sanskrit, the language of the Vedas, the intertwining and interdependence of language and existence is no surprise. Sanskrit is known to be a “name and form” language, where there is literally no difference between the symbol (word or vibration) and the object it describes. It is even said sometimes that the supernatural powers of perfected yogis to materialize objects at will is connected to the ability to *purely* speak a Sanskrit word and thus invoke its actual form. Also, according to Vedic philosophy, there is the special mantra *Om*, the primordial sound which continually reverberates throughout the universe, and is considered to be the vibratory power behind all energies.

However, since most of us may not be able to detect the cosmic *Om* or to speak Sanskrit purely, there is another way to show just how integral language, or the ability to distinguish, is to our being. Consider, if you will, “picture puzzles,” those interesting drawings nearly all of us have seen, which are created in such a way as to present two images—one obvious and one hidden. One well-known example is the picture that looks initially like a vase, but upon closer examination reveals two identical human silhouettes facing each other. One generally notices the silhouettes only after being told to look more carefully. So from the viewer’s perception, the human silhouettes did not exist until he or she could distinguish them *in language.* The silhouettes only become present to the viewer who is aware of the concept of a “silhouette.”

So the existence of a thing is based largely on language—the ability to distinguish. There are cases of less civilized tribes who have been shown photographs but see nothing other than a piece of paper because they are as yet uneducated to the concept of the photograph. The same natives cannot see themselves in a mirror because there is no word (distinction) for “mirror” in their language. Perhaps the best example is the case of Helen Keller who, in describing the moment she awoke to language, gives us some excellent insight. She writes, “Suddenly, I knew not how or where or when, I awoke to language, to knowledge of love, to the usual concepts of nature, of good and evil.” Of her life before languaging ability she says, “ For nearly six years I had no concepts whatever of nature or mind or death or God. *There was not one spark of emotion or rational thought....I was like an unconscious clod of earth.”*

Now if it is so that humans depend largely, if not entirely, on language, and humans are but a microcosm of the universe, then what, logically, must the universe also require for its existence? What we now see is a reason or purpose for the cosmos to speak, to distinguish—and thus a reason for astrology, its inherent language. However, it is not enough for nature to simply speak; what kind of existence could the universe enjoy if it could speak (distinguish) but there was no one to listen (acknowledge)? Therefore, when the creation speaks, when it relays its moment-to-moment messages via planets and stars—who must it necessarily speak to? Clearly, to beings capable of listening; to beings also capable of language, the ability to distinguish, and thereby the ability to answer back.

How do we humans respond back to the universe? By either accepting and living the dictates and prophecies the cosmos has made about our lives, or by using our free will, our language, and our creative ability to live as we choose. Essentially, what is occurring is a kind of continual dance or interplay between nature, or the cosmos, and the individual. Most fascinating is the fact that the communications from the universe come to us in the form of fate or predestiny while we recipients must necessarily possess free will, or the universe would lose its enjoyment of existence. This is how free will and predestiny exist side by side without infringement upon each other. Indeed, one could not even exist without the other.

Now that we know why the star language exists, the question still remains as to how it works. How is it that nature could indicate, through the planets, that John Lennon was to die during a particular period and the act came to pass? Why do so many astrological predictions, forecasts, and time frames come true? Do the planets cause these occurrences? Not in the sense of heavenly bodies being physically connected to our lives. Although it is possible that astrology works that way, the greater reality is that both the universe and the individual possess language: the ability to distinguish and therefore create. It then comes down to who “speaks” louder, more clearly, and more precisely.

In Lennon’s case, every Hindu astrologer would agree that nature spoke with the most acute precision possible. The birthchart indications were incredibly blatant. It would have been Lennon’s job, had he been aware of nature’s proclamation, to speak or will things differently. For those who are not astrologers, the easiest way to grasp the concept of two speakers, two “creators” both distinguishing an experience, is to consider how human behavior changes in the presence of certain people. Brilliant individuals may become dullards around relatives who perceive them as such; capable children become truly inept around overly critical parents. This is simply the interplay of two beings simultaneously engaged in creating a type of reality. So it is with the individual and the cosmos. Nature distinguishes who and what we are, and in a relative sense, we are free to choose and create our own reality.

Interestingly, we call astrology “the language of the stars,” but the more appropriate term is “the language of God,” for the stars and planets are not the speaker; they are but the lips and tongue of nature, the material energy of God. If we know that nature is speaking, we would only be wise to listen. If a highly experienced astrologer warns of disaster, we should consider the forecast seriously and take every available precaution. We should not, however, get carried away with fear; it is simply like carrying an umbrella when the weatherman forecasts rain.

If our astrologer predicts fame, fortune, or success, we must summon up all our power and creativity, keep the faith, and strive to prove the astrologer correct. In 1983, astrologer Shastri said I would write books. In 1984, I took up his challenge, spent a month of eight-hour writing days, and failed miserably. A year later, with his prediction still reverberating in my ears, I tried again. Nine months of seven-day work weeks followed. It was no picnic. But in the end, his forecast, which I dearly wanted to see realized, had come to fruition. If there is a proper way to “believe” in astrology, it is to take the messages of the stars and planets seriously, maintain faith in predictions, but realize our own choice, responsibility, and free will in the matter. That is of utmost importance.

There is a story about His Divine Grace A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, the founder of the Hare Krishna Movement. One day while sitting on a New York park bench, he was approached by a subway conductor. This occurred during his early days in America when he was as yet unknown and financially impoverished. The conductor inquired as to who he was and what he was doing. Swami Prabhupada explained that he was very successful and had temples all over the world filled with spiritual seekers, devotees of God. The man looked puzzled at what appeared an obvious fib, whereupon Prabhupada replied, *“It is only sepa ra ted by time!”*

In accord with the Indian Bengali tradition, Prabhupada’s parents had consulted a Hindu astrologer immediately following the child’s birth. They were told that their son would cross the ocean at the age of 70 as a proponent of a particular religon, he would minister to followers worldwide, and would construct 108 temples.

Swami Prabhupada obviously had unshakeable faith in God—whom he lovingly addressed as Krishna—and the dictates of his birthchart. Who knows what his destiny might have been had he so “believed” in his fate that he neglected to take action? But in conjunction with his faith he also had the blessed “common sense” to be responsible, to use his free will, and thereby play his part in the drama of life. He paid for a ticket, boarded a boat for the West, and did whatever he deemed necessary to carry out his divine “mission.” The way it happened, the Swami left India at the age of 69, and the rest, as they say, is history.
