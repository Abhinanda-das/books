# Caitanya Vaisnava Philosophy

## Caitanya vaisnavism on trial: continuity and transformation in the eighteenth century\[1\]

*Kiyokazu Okita*

In the history of Caitanya Vaisnavism, the sixteenth century, the period of the Gosvamis, was the time of its formation. Since Krsna Caitanya, the charismatic inaugurator of the tradition, taught orally and did not compose his own works, it was his disciples who articulated Caitanya’s teachings and systematically laid the tradition’s foundation in the form of their writings.

Thanks to the solid foundation laid by the founding fathers, Caitanya Vaisnavism was well established by the eighteenth century. The tradition, however, was faced with new challenges. To escape from the effect of aggressive anti-Hindu policies adopted by the Mughal regime since the middle of the seventeenth century,many of the sacred images worshipped in the Caitanya Vaisnava temples in Vrndavana moved to Rajasthan, especially to the land of the Kachvaha dynasty, which supported the Caitanya Vaisnavas since the time of the Gosvamis.

Firmly embracing the long-standing connection between his royal family and the Caitanya Vaisnava tradition, Jaisingh II, the celebrated Kachvaha king who ruled in the first half of the eighteenth century, supported and promoted the Caitanya Vaisnavas who recently moved to his kingdom to continue their worship of sacred images. From the king’s viewpoint, however, the Caitanya Vaisnava tradition lacked two important elements for it to be accepted as a legitimate Vaisnava school. They were the tradition’s lack of its own Brahmasutra commentary, and the lack of affiliation with any of the four accepted Vaisnava traditions.

It was Baladeva Vidyabhusana who tackled these challenges and successfully defended the legitimacy of the Caitanya Vaisnava school. In this chapter, we shall first examine the life of Baladeva and the historical context in which he composed his works. Then, based on his writings, we shall examine Baladeva’s thought in relation to earlier thinkers.

## The life of Baladeva

As for the life of Baladeva, we can gather its rough outline based on hagiographical accounts as well as on existent historical documents such as his letters and official records mentioning his name. Haridasa Dasa, a Gaudia author in the twentieth century, gives the following hagiographical summary:

Baladeva was bom in the Balesvara area of Orissa, in a village near Ramuna in approximately the 18thcentury. After studying grammar (vyakarana), rhetoric (alankara), and logic (nyaya) in some place of learning on the bank of the Cilka lake, he went to Mahisura (Mysore) to study the Vedas. At this time, having accepted discipleship in the Madhva sampradaya, he became a member of that sampradaya (tatsampradayi). Later, having accepted the sannyasa order of life, and after becoming victorious over Purusottamaksetra’s learned community in scriptural debate, he took up residence in the monastery for the Tattvavadins (i. e. the followers of Madhva). Some days later, he studied Jiva Gosvami’s Satsandarbha with Radhadamodara, a resident of Kanyakubja, who was a disciple of a disciple of Rasikananda. Being drawn at the core of his heart to the teachings of Gaudiya Vaisnavism, Baladeva became Radhadamodara’s disciple. It is said that he then studied the scriptures concerning bhakti with Pitambara Dasa and studied the Bhagavata Purana with Visvanatha Cakravarti. He accepted the dress of a Vaisnava renouncer, and became known as Baladeva “Ekanti Govinda Dasa”. He installed the sacred image of Syamasundara in Vrndavana. Uddhavadasa and Nandamisra were his two main disciples. He is the master of Gaudiya Vedanta, the author of Govindabhasya. When, in Srila Visvanatha Cakravarti's final years, news reached Vrndavana that the Bengalis performing service at the temples in Jaipur, being considered “without legitimate lineage” (asampradayi), were removed from their service, then Baladeva, by the order of Visvanatha, went to Jaipur with Krsnadeva Sarvabhauma, and defeated the opposing groups in debate. Then he re-established the seat of the Bengalis in the mountainous region called Galta and established the sacred image of Vijaya Gopala. Even today, this sacred image is shining in the temple there. At that time, by the merciful command of Govindadeva, he composed Govindabhasya, and brought glory to the Gaudiya Vaisnavas.

For our current purpose, important points to remember in this account are: (1) Baladeva was first trained in the Madhva tradition, but later he converted to the Caitanya Vaisnava tradition; (2) he was involved in some sort of religious disputes in Jaipur.

Thanks to Burton’s archival research,in addition to the above hagiographical accounts we also know that the year of Baladeva’s death is documented as 1793.Given this timeline, it is not likely that he was bom before 1700. As the author of the Brahmasutrakarikabhasya,\[6\]a brief commentary on the Brahmasutra, commissioned under Jaisingh II’s rule (1700-43), Baladeva must have established himself as a leader of the Caitanya Vaisnavas in Vrndavana, visited Jaipur and interacted with the king sometime before 1743. A letter sent to Jaisingh II in 1742 describes Baladeva as being responsible for the service of the Govindadevaji temple, one of the most influential places of worship in Vrndavana. Mansingh (r. 1589-1614), Jaisingh’s Kachvaha ancestor who ruled Amber, constructed this temple for Rupa Gosvami. Given this historical background, Baladeva must have been an important pipeline between the Caitanya Vaisnavas in Vrndavana and the Kachvaha family in Jaipur. A letter sent in 1755 to Madhavasingh, who succeeded Jaisingh, still describes Baladeva as the representative of the temple. Thus it appears that he was an influential leader of the Gaudiya community in the mid eighteenth century.

As for the class (varna) from which Baladeva hailed, I have encountered two opinions. Aksaya Kumara Sharma, a Gaudiya author in the twentieth century, argues that Baladeva was a Brahmana. The view that he was a non-Brahmana is proposed by Bhaktivinoda Thakura, a nineteenth-century Gaudiya author. There has been a discussion on this point by two contemporary academics, Delmonico who supports Sharma,and Buchta who defends the plausibility of Bhaktivinoda’s position.In his journal Sajjanatosani Bhaktivinoda says that Baladeva took his birth in the lineage of Khandaitas.In the secondary literature on Baladeva the

Khandaitas are understood to belong to the Vaisya class.However, the name Khandaita seems to suggest some affinity with the Ksatriya class, although, according to Kulke, Khandaita or Khandayata are more like tribal chiefs rather than Hindu kings.According to Tanabe, Khandayatas dominate the population of the coastal area in contemporary Orissa, and they exercise considerable political and economic influence.All these points suggest that the Khandayats are non- Brahmanas who possess considerable local political power. In addition, in Fakir Mohan Das’s view the distinction between Khandayats and Brahmanas in Orissa is not substantial.

As we shall see below, Baladeva wrote extensively on Vedanta. The issue at stake, then, is whether a non-Brahmana (either Ksatriya or Vaisya) could have written on the subject. The Manusmrti 1. 88-90, for example, describes the duties of the three upper classes, stating that although all of them are allowed to study the Vedas (adhyayana), only the Brahmanas are assigned to teach them (adhyapana).Accordingly, Madhva emphatically forbade open access to learning

Vedanta.As a result, traditionally only male Brahmanas were allowed to receive the kind of training which would eventually enable them to take up the position of teaching. According to anthropological research on contemporary Madhvas, all the pontiffs of the Asta Mathas (eight monasteries) of Udupi, which are the centers of the Madhva tradition, are male Brahmanas, and only young Madhvas who hail from the same class can succeed them.All the Madhva gurus in Udupi are also Brahmanas, be they renouncers (samnyasi) or householders (grhastha).\[18\]Baladeva was thoroughly familiar with Madhva Vedanta, and as a sannyasi he was a representative of the Madhva tradition. Given these points, I find it more likely that Baladeva was a Brahmana. However, if indeed the Khandayats in Orissa possessed an influential social status comparable to that of the Brahmanas, it might have been possible that the Madhvas accepted Baladeva as an exception. We should also remember that the state of the matter at the present time is only indicative but not conclusive of how these issues were handled at the time of Madhva or of Baladeva.

Baladeva was a prolific writer who composed both commentaries and independent works. Haridasa Dasa attributes the following works to Baladeva:commentaries on the Bhagavata Purana (Vaisnavanandini), on the Gopalatapani Upanisad, on the Bhagavad Gita (Gitabhusana), on the Brahmasutras (Govindabhasya), on Rupa Gosvami’s Laghubhagavatamrta (Sarangarangada), and Stavamala (Stavamalabhusana), on Jiva Gosvami’s Tattvasandarbha, on Rasikananda’s Syamanandasataka, and on the Karika portion of Mammata’s Kavyaprakasa (Sahityakaumudi). As for the independent works, they are the Siddhantaratna, Prameyaratnavali, Siddhantadarpana, Kavyakaustubha, and Aisvaryakadambini.

Although Dasa attributes the Chandahkaustubha\[22\]and the Vedantasyamantaka to Baladeva, I maintain that it is a work of Baladeva’s Caitanya Vaisnava teacher Radhadamodara.Dasa also says that Baladeva wrote a commentary on Rupa’s Natakacandrika. However, this work is nowhere to be found.

Besides these, Baladeva wrote the Brahmasutrakarikabhasya\[25\]mentioned above, and another work titled Tattvadipika.\[26\]These were commissioned by Jaisingh II, and are usually not listed in the works of Baladeva. Therefore it seems that they were not widely circulated among the Gaudiya followers but were kept in the king’s collection.

## Historical background

The Kachvaha royal family maintained a long-standing relation with Caitanya Vaisnavas since the time of Mansingh, who was the Raja of Amber from 1589 to 1614. Under the influence of his father Bhagavan Dasa as well as Raghunatha Bhatta, who was one of the six Gosvamis of Vrndavana, Mansingh constructed a temple in Vrndavana so that Rupa Gosvami could worship his sacred image (murti) of Govindadeva there.

This sacred image of Govindadeva, alongside with his Caitanya Vaisnava pujaris, moved to Jaipur during the reign of Jaisingh II to escape the iconoclasm instigated by Aurangzeb. This arrival of the sacred image strengthened the royal family’s tie to the Caitanya tradition. Eventually, Jaisingh II made Govindadeva one of the tutelary deities of the royal family. Due to this prominent position Govindadeva received in Jaipur, the social status of Caitanya Vaisnavas attending the sacred image improved as well.

This change in social standing was a blessing as well as a curse for Caitanya Vaisnavas who arrived from Vrndavana. It was a curse since the social prominence they acquired prompted other Vaisnava groups to challenge their status. Until their arrival, Rama was the only tutelary deity of the Kachvahas (whose attendants were the Ramanandis).The Caitanya Vaisnavas’ sudden rise to prominence must have created a considerable threat to the position of the Ramanandis as the religious custodians of the royal family. In addition, other Vaisnava groups such as the Pustimargis (i. e. the followers of Vallabha) also arrived in Jaipur from Vrndavana to seek royal support.

For the Vaisnava groups competing for the king’s favor one way to challenge the Caitanya Vaisnavas at the court was to question their religious legitimacy.The idea of four legitimate Vaisnava traditions (catuhsampradaya) became vital in this context. By Jaisingh’s time, there were only four legitimate Vaisnava traditions— those initiated by Ramanuja (the Sri Sampradaya), Madhva (the Brahma Sampradaya), Visnusvami / Vallabha(the Rudra Sampradaya), and Nimbarka (the Sanaka Sampradaya).\[31\]Traditions that did not belong to any of these lineages were considered illegitimate and thus not worthy of royal support. The pedigree of Caitanya Vaisnavas was questioned since they essentially considered Caitanya to be the founder of their tradition. Although Kavi Karnapura’s Gauraganoddesadipika written in the sixteenth century already points out Caitanya’s connection with the Madhvas, it appears that the Caitanya Vaisnavas prior to Baladeva did not emphasize their connection with any of the four “legitimate” traditions.

Given the lack of clear connection with the Brahma-Madhva tradition in the pre-Baladeva period, the Caitanya Vaisnavas were forced to clarify their sampradaya identity. It seems, however, that the ways in which they conceived of their relation to the Madhvas were not unanimous. For example, in the royal library of the Man Singh II Museum in Jaipur, there are two manuscripts under the title

Caitanyaguruparampara, “The Lineage of Caitanya’s Gurus. ”Although they are catalogued under the same title, and although both of them list the teachers’ names in Caitanya’s lineage starting from Brahma, the names found in them are completely different from each other.

Manuscript #5172 describes the teachers in Caitanya’s lineage in verses, and these verses are more or less identical with those found in Kavi Karnapura’s Gauraganoddesadipika. Manuscript #5170 in contrast is simply a list of names, which, unlike the other manuscript, does not contain any of the names of the Madhva gurus, expect for Padmanabha.Although undated, the list contains the names of seven teachers after Caitanya. If we count 25 to 30 years for each generation, we can assume that the list was composed in the first half of the eighteenth century, which is exactly the period during which Jaisingh II was active. Therefore, although the Caitanya Vaisnavas’ self-perception as part of the Brahma-Madhva tradition might have existed since the sixteenth century, it seems that in the eighteenth century there were competing claims about this.

As a representative of the Caitanya Vaisnavas, Baladeva provides his own list of Caitanya’s lineage in his Prameyaratnavali. In this list, Baladeva traces Caitanya’s lineage back to Brahma via Madhva.Although his list mostly follows that of Karnapura,there is an important difference between the two: according to Baladeva there is a Madhva teacher called Brahmanya between Purusottama and Vyasatirtha. This further supports the view that the idea of Caitanya’s lineage was not absolutely fixed in eighteenth-century northern India.

Another challenge the Caitanya Vaisnavas faced was their lack of any commentary on the Brahmasutras. It has been the practice of the “legitimate” Vaisnava schools to develop their distinct ideologies based on the Brahmasutras,However, exclusively focusing on the Bhagavata Purana, Caitanya Vaisnavas up to the eighteenth century did not write any full commentary on the sutras?\[38\]Thus the absence of a Caitanya Vaisnava commentary on the sutras threatened the legitimacy of their teachings.

To deal with this challenge, Baladeva Vidyabhusana, the most prominent Caitanya Vaisnava author of the eighteenth century, under the king’s order composed the Brahmasutrakarikabhasya. Many hagiographies claim that Baladeva wrote his magnum opus the Govindabhasya, a full commentary on the Brahmasutras, in a very short period of time, in order to answer the challenge posed by other Vaisnava groups. As Burton points out,however, given its brevity, it is more likely that the Brahmasutrakarikabhasya was the one which Baladeva submitted to the king, and he wrote the more elaborate Govindabhasya later.

When we examine the hagiographical, historical, and textual information, Baladeva’s strong association with the Madhva sampradaya is evident: he was trained in the Madhva sampradaya before his conversion to Caitanya Vaisnavism; he presents Caitanya as a part of the Brahma-Madhva lineage, which was necessary in the historical context; and he employs the distinctly Madhva concepts in his Vedantic works. Consequently, some scholars argue that Baladeva was actually writing as a Madhva follower rather than as a Caitanya Vaisnava. B. N. K. Sharma, himself a fervent Madhva adherent, voices this view most clearly:

These facts are sufficient to establish that Baladeva is virtually in agreement with Madhva on all the fundamental points of his system. That is why we find him proclaiming his loyalty to Madhva and affirming the historical and spiritual descent of the Caitanya Sampradaya from Madhva, without any hesitation or mental reservation, not only in the Prameyaratnavali, but also in his Siddhantaratna \[... \] which is an introduction to his Govinda-Bhasya \[... \] This should suffice to place his allegiance to the Madhva Sampradaya beyond question.\[41\]

Baladeva certainly had a strong connection with the Madhva tradition. At the same time, however, we should also remember that at Jai Singh’s court as well as in Vrndavana, Baladeva was recognized as a prominent leader of the Caitanya tradition. This historical fact seems to refute the suggestion that Baladeva was simply a Madhva follower, given the fundamental philosophical differences between the Madhva and the Caitanya Vaisnava traditions. Baladeva was in fact “walking a theological tightrope”,as he, a representative of the Caitanya Vaisnava tradition, had to convince a learned audience at the royal court that the tradition was somehow part of the Madhva sampradaya. Buchta contemplates Baladeva’s affiliation:

Does Baladeva truly follow Madhva’s doctrine, even where it might counter Gaudiya doctrines, or does he simply utilize Madhva’s terminology in presenting Gaudiya doctrines, out of respect for Madhva, and to appropriate Madhva’s authority for the Gaudiya sampradaya?

He then concludes:

I propose that it would be more accurate to see this deference to Madhva not so much as the sustained prominent influence of Baladeva’s early Madhva affiliation, but as the influence of the religious climate of Jaipur and the reforms of religious society coming from Jai Singh.

Following Buchta’s lead,I shall argue below that Baladeva, with his deep knowledge of both traditions, was employing various elements of Madhva Vedanta in the service of Caitanya Vaisnava Vedanta. In what follows, I analyze Baladeva’s epistemology, ontology, and soteriology to observe how he was responding to the challenges the Caitanya Vaisnava tradition faced in eighteenth- century northern India.

## Epistemology: the Upanisads and the Bhagavata Purana

Caitanya taught the Bhagavata Purana to be the highest revelation. Following this teaching, Jiva in his Tattvasandarbha places the Purana even above the Vedic Samhitas.The Bhagavata itself claims its status as the essence of the Vedas (e. g. 1. 1. 3, 1. 3. 40, 1. 4. 20).The Bhagavata was popular not only among the followers of Caitanya but throughout all northern India in the early modem period. Vallabha, a contemporary of Caitanya, for example, also gave the Bhagavata a decisive role in determining the meanings of the prasthana texts (the Upanisads, the Brahmasutras, and the Bhagavad Gita).

The issue of scriptural authority has a long history in South Asia. How does one recognize the validity of scriptural revelation (sabda)? While the Mimamsakas argue that the authority of the Vedas is self-evident (svatah-pramanya), the Naiyayikas claim that it has to be proven (paratah-pramanya) by inference. A somewhat similar tension seems to apply to the Bhagavata. To put it simply, while the followers of Caitanya accepted the supreme status of the Bhagavata as self-evident, Jaisingh II wanted to see its validity proven in relation to the more established texts such as the Brahmasutras.

As a follower of Caitanya Vaisnavism, Baladeva accepted the scriptural authority of the Bhagavata. The king, however, was very keen to study the Brahmasutras—he amassed the commentaries on the sutras from various traditions for his personal study, and seems to have even written his own work on Vedanta called the Brahmabodhini,Since the king emphasized the importance of the Brahmasutras, and by extension, of the Upanisads and the Bhagavad Gita, a part of Baladeva’s task in representing the Caitanya school at the royal court was to explain Caitanya’s philosophy in relation to the prasthanatrayi more explicitly than Jiva did.

So far as his discussion on the means of knowledge (pramana) is concerned, Baladeva mostly follows Jiva and other earlier Vedantic authors. Therefore, his contribution on this topic is not particularly original, even less original than Jiva’s. Although Baladeva amply cites from the Bhagavata in his Vedantic works, he, unlike Jiva, does not seem to emphasize the Bhagavata's supreme status.Therefore, Baladeva’s contribution in epistemology is not so much about the content, but the very fact that he is engaged in the Vedantic discourse from the viewpoint of Caitanya’s teaching. In this section, I shall examine Baladeva’s view on the Vedas based on the Govindabhasya, which is his commentary on the Brahmasutras, and its introduction, the Siddhantaratna.

## The vedas and Brahman

In his discussion on epistemology, Baladeva emphasizes the supra-mundane nature of Brahman, and teaches that the revealed scriptures (sruti) are the only means to understand it. This is a standard argument among the Vedantists. It is this focus on the scriptures which makes them the exegetes (the Mimamsakas). For example, in Siddhantaratna 1. 13, Baladeva comments: “As for the fact that it \[i. e. Brahman\] has a body consisting of knowledge and bliss, we can understand it only through the srutis but not through reasoning (tarka), since \[Brahman\] is a supra-mundane entity. ”Then he cites verses from the Brahmasutras (3. 2. 14-17),which explain the inconceivability of Brahman’s form.

In Siddhantaratna 1. 14, Baladeva gives two more textual passages to support his argument. The first is from the Katha Upanisad: “My dear \[Naciketas\], you cannot reach this understanding through reasoning. Only when another person teaches is it easily understood. ”In his auto-commentary, Baladeva explains that the “other person” in the passage refers to a Vedic teacher,which presumably means someone who can teach the Vedas. The second citation is from the Mahabharata: “As is well known, through reasoning one would not reach those states which are inconceivable. ”It is typical of Baladeva that he cites both sruti (e. g. Katha Upanisad) and smrti (e. g. the Mahabharata) passages to support his argument.

Does Baladeva reject reasoning all at once? In Siddhantaratna 1. 14, an imaginary opponent argues that reasoning must have some place in our understanding of the ultimate reality since the Brhadaranyaka Upanisad says that we should “think” about the self.In response, Baladeva declares that we should not reject reasoning which is in accordance with the Upanisads.He then cites an anonymous smrti passage: “‘Without any contradiction between an earlier \[sentence\] and a later one, what would be the desired meaning here? ’ Reasoning such as this is \[called\] reasoning (tarka). However, groundless reasoning (suskatarka) should be abandoned. ”

Baladeva is not rejecting the reasoning process per se. What he rejects is reasoning without any scriptural basis. However, he does accept what might be called scriptural reasoning. To know Brahman, one needs to understand the scriptures. To do so, it is necessary to exercise reasoning, and this scriptural reasoning is at the heart of Baladeva’s epistemology. The same discussion is found in his commentary on Brahmasutra 1. 1. 3 (sastrayonitvat). This is an important sutra which most Vedantists understand as indicating that the scriptures (sastra) are the source (yoni) for understanding Brahman.

The citation of the above-mentioned smrti passage condemning groundless reasoning is also indicative of Baladeva’s relation to Madhva and Jiva. The anonymous passage is first found in Madhva’s commentary on Brahmasutra 1. 3, where it is attributed to the Kurma Purana. This, however, is a so-called “untraceable quotation, ” meaning that we do not find this passage in the Purana, at least not in the version which is available to us now.The topic of untraceable quotes in Madhva’s works has been hotly debated among modem scholars. Here it suffices to say that citing the untraceable quotes in Madhva’s work suggests that Baladeva considers the citations in Madhva’s writing to be reliable.

This, however, does not mean that Baladeva was deviating from the Caitanya school. The smrti passage is also cited by Jiva in his Sarvasamvadini,\[60\]which is the auto-commentary on his Tattvasandarbha. The Tattvasandarbha is the first of Jiva’s six-volume magnum opus Bhagavatasandarbha, and Jiva lays down his epistemology in this first Sandarbha. In general, Baladeva cites Madhva’s untraceable quotes if they are already accepted by Jiva.

## The eternality of the Vedas

Following earlier Vedantists, Baladeva claims in his Govindabhasya as well as in his Siddhantaratna that the srutis, or the Vedas are the only means of knowing the ultimate reality, Brahman. Why are the Vedas so special? Buddhists and Jains do not accept the authority of the Vedas, and the Naiyayikas do not think that the special status of the Vedas is self-evident. For both the Mimamsakas and the Vedantists, therefore, establishing a special epistemological status for the Vedas was a major concern.

One of the unique arguments they put forward in this regard is that the Vedas are eternal, that is, they do not have any kind of author, be it human or divine. Any author would inevitably make mistakes, and in order to avoid such a risk, the Mimamsakas and the Vedantists reject the idea of authorship in relation to the Vedas. Instead, they argue that the Vedas are eternal and unchanging. If there is no author, however, it follows that there is no authorial intention. If there is no authorial intention, then how do we know what is intended by the Vedas?

The Mimamsasutra attributed to Jaimini responds to this question by saying that the relationship between a verbal signifier and the signified entity is innate.

In his commentary on the sutra, Sabara (fifth century CE) explains that the relation between a word and the object denoted by the word is not man-made (apauruseya),\[63\]and when the word is known, its object is known.In other words, the relation between a word and what it denotes is eternally fixed, and we can comprehend its meaning without any intermediary. Therefore, according to the Mimamsakas, the Vedas are meaningful to us even though there is no authorial intention in them.

Following Sankara, Ramanuja, and Madhva, Baladeva discusses the eternality of the Vedas in his commentary on the Brahmasutras 1. 3. 28and 29.The topic comes up in the context of discussing whether gods such as Indra are able to attain the knowledge of Brahman. Pointing out the temporary existence of the gods, an imaginary opponent (purvapaksi) argues that the Mimamsaka view on the relation between a word and its referendum is not tenable:

Before the creation of them \[i. e. the forms of the gods\] and after the destruction of them, there arises an incongruity with regard to the words which denote them \[i. e. the forms of the gods\], \[the incongruity\] in the form of having no means of being known like the expression “the son of a barren woman”. The eternality (1) of the words, (2) of their objects, and (3) of the connections between them, which is mentioned by the Purvamimamsa system in the statement, “The connection of an object and a word is innate”, that \[i. e. the eternality of the words, and so on\] is also contradicted.

As we saw above, the Mimamsakas argue that the Vedas are eternal, and that the connection between the words in the Vedas and their objects is also eternal. The opponent, however, points out that since the forms of the gods are not eternal, the connections between the words denoting the form of the gods and the objects expressed by such words cannot be eternal either.

Baladeva answers this challenge by arguing that the objects denoted by the words of the Vedas do not have individual existence but rather they are configurations (akrti):

Even with regard to the words in the Vedas, such a contradiction characterized \[by the opponents\] does not arise. Why? Because \[the forms of the gods and so on\] arise from it \[i. e. from the words in the Vedas\]. The meaning is this: \[First, \] based on the various words of the Vedas, which express the varieties of the eternal configurations (akrti) \[of the gods\], \[Brahma\] recollects the varieties of the eternal configurations \[of the gods\]. \[Then, based on these recollections, Brahma\] creates a variety of forms of the gods. The configurations of the gods are eternal because they exist before \[the physical manifestations of\] all individual forms. \[These configurations\], which are mentioned in Visvakarman’s own scripture for the sake of accomplishing various activities, draw yama as one who has a rod in his hand but Varuna as one who has a noose in his hand. In this way, from their very nature the words in the Vedas, which signify the gods and so on, conventionally denote the configurations \[of them\], like words such as “cow”. But \[the words of the Vedas\] do not denote the mere individuals like a word \[i. e. a name\] such as “Caitra”.

Baladeva explains that when the Vedas mention gods such as Indra, these words do not refer to any particular individual objects of temporary existence. What they do denote, however, is a kind of blueprint based on which individual objects are created. Therefore, the noun yama, the name of the god of death in the Vedas, does not denote any individual but the specific nature of divine beings who are characterized by their possession of the rod (danda). This is different from the nouns such as “Caitra, ” which are proper names for individuals. Since these blueprints are eternal, Baladeva says, the relation between the words and the blueprints denoted by the words can be eternal.

This discussion on configuration (akrti) is already found in commentary on Mimamsasutra 1. 1. 5, where he says, “Now, what is the object of this word ‘cow’? We say that it is the configuration (akrti) which is characterized by a dewlap and so on. ”In the example of the word “cow, ” a configuration, which is denoted by the word, is a set of specific characteristics of an entity, such as having a dewlap, which makes the entity a cow. From this discussion of configuration by Sabara it is clear that Baladeva follows Sabara’s idea closely.

In fact, Baladeva is not the first one to follow Sabara. Sankara and Ramanuja also offer the same discussion on configuration in their commentaries on Brahmasutra 1. 3. 28.One may think therefore, that all Vedantists accept the Mimamsaka notion of configuration. Madhva, however, does not seem to follow Sankara and Ramanuja on this point. He does discuss the same topic, namely the words’ eternal relation to their objects, in his commentary on the same sutra. Like his predecessors, Madhva argues that there is no problem in accepting the eternal relation between the words and the denoted objects. However, Madhva does not use the specific term “configuration (akrti)” to make his point. Instead, he argues that even when gods such as Indra die, a new living entity, who has the same nature as the previous Indra, will succeed the post immediately. Therefore, the eternal relation between the words and their objects is still maintained, even though the objects denoted by the words are themselves not eternal. Therefore, the conclusion Madhva arrives at is the same as those of Sabara, Sankara, and Ramanuja. However, Madhva does so without postulating the concept of configuration.

What is Jiva’s position on this topic? Does he accept the concept of configuration? It is difficult to conclude his position decisively. In this Tattvasandarbha Anuccheda 10, Jiva accepts the eternality of the Vedas, and says that they are the only means of knowing Brahman.In his Sarvasamvadini commentary on the Anuccheda, Jiva discusses the nature of the words in the Vedas and their objects. After referring to the Brahmasutra commentaries of Sankara, Ramanuja, and Madhva on the same topic, Jiva concludes, “When the innate connection of a word with \[its\] object is accepted, the credibility of the Vedas is considered to be indeed not dependent \[on other means of knowledge\]. ”Jiva, therefore, accepts the words’ eternal relation with their objects, like his predecessors. Since he refers to the commentaries of Sankara and Ramanuja, he must have been aware of the concept of configuration (akrti). However, he does not use the term, unlike Sabara, Sankara, and Ramanuja. Does this mean Jiva did not accept the concept? It is hard to conclude decisively due to lack of evidence. Since Jiva cites Sankara and Ramanuja without explicitly rejecting the notion of configuration, one could also argue that he did approve of it.

What we know for certain is that Baladeva wrote a commentary on Jiva’s Tattvasandarbha, and therefore he was aware of the views of his Mimamsaka and Vedantist predecessors, including those of Jiva. Baladeva’s contribution is perhaps that he clarifies the Caitanya Vaisnava view on the word-object relationship, by explicitly embracing the concept of configuration.

## Summary

As far as epistemology is concerned, Baladeva seems to strictly follow his Mimamsaka and Vedantist predecessors. Baladeva maintains that the Vedas are the only means of understanding Brahman and reasoning (tarka) should have a scriptural basis. These are standard ideas in Vedanta. We also observed that Baladeva cites an untraceable quote, which is first found in Madhva’s work, and later it is also cited by Jiva. This is an example in Baladeva’s writing where Madhva’s teaching is mediated through Jiva. As for the eternal relation between the words in the Vedas and their object, Baladeva uses the concept of configuration, which is accepted by Sabara, Sankara, and Ramanuja, but not by Madhva. Since Jiva’s stance on this point is somewhat unclear, perhaps Baladeva tried to articulate the Caitanya Vaisnava view by explicitly embracing the concept. Based on these observations, we may conclude that Baladeva’s contribution in epistemology is not so much about the content of his ideas but the very fact that he emphasized the importance of the Vedas and clearly articulated the Caitanya Vaisnava position in the context of a broader Vedantic discourse.

## Ontology: the concept of differentiating capacity (visesa)

In contrast to Baladeva’s view on the Vedas, which is fairly orthodox, his acceptance and the application of the concept of differentiating capacity (visesa) is both unique and controversial. This concept was first proposed by Madhva, to explain the relation between attributes and their substrate. Baladeva uses the same concept in his writings. As Dasgupta points out, this influence of Madhva on Baladeva is controversial partly because Jiva did not use the concept:

The concept of visesa in this sense was first introduced by Madhva; Baladeva borrowed the idea from him in interpreting the relation of God to His powers and qualities. This interpretation is entirely different from the view of Jiva and others who preceded Baladeva.

Baladeva’s application of the concept is unique because he uses it to explain the unity of Radha and Krsna, a doctrine central to Caitanya Vaisnava theology, which, however, is rejected by Madhva. In short, Baladeva takes a concept from Madhva Vedanta which no Caitanya Vaisnava authors used before and applies it to a doctrine which no Madhva followers would approve of. It is here, in his use of the concept, where we can observe Baladeva’s creative genius at its best.

In order to appreciate Baladeva’s creativity, however, we first need to understand the context in which Madhva invented the concept of differentiating capacity. Madhva taught the concept to reject the Vaisesika idea of inherence (samavaya), which was used as an explanation of the relation between attributes and their substrate. Madhva was not the first one to reject the concept of inherence; Sankara and Ramanuja did so as well. In what follows, I shall discuss the Vaisesika concept of inherence, the Vedantic authors’ refutations of the concept, and finally Baladeva’s use of the concept of differentiating capacity.

**Inherence in Vaisesika**

The issue concerning the relation between attributes and their substrate troubled the mind of South Asian philosophers for many centuries. When you see a blue lotus, what is the exact relation between the blue color (an attribute / guna / dharma) and the lotus (its substrate / dravya / dharmin)? Are they the same or different? Evidently, the adjective “blue” and the noun “lotus” do not mean the same. If they did, the expression “the lotus is blue” would be a tautology. At the same time, the referent for the adjective and that for the noun cannot be completely distinct. If they were, they would refer to two separate entities, and the subject-predicate relation would not work. Therefore, we need to explain the relation between attributes and their substrate in such a way that they are related to each other without losing their distinct natures. In a way, the issue at hand is linguistic rather than ontological. However, it became an ontological issue ever since Vaisesika understood the referent of an adjective to be an entity, which is distinct from the referent of the noun qualified by the adjective.

The Vaisesika thinkers proposed samavaya, or the concept of inherence, as a solution to this conundrum. Prasastapada (c. fourth century) explains it in his Padarthadharmasangraha: “Inherence is a connection between the located and the locus, which is established to be inseparable. It is the cause of the idea ‘in this place’. ”In this definition, the located (adharya) refers to attributes, and the locus (adhara) to their substrate. According to Prasastapada, because of inherence we recognize that attributes are located in their substrate (iha-pratyaya). Furthermore, these attributes and the substrate are inseparable (ayutasiddha). The term “inseparable” is used here in order to distinguish inherence from conjunction (samyoga), which is another concept to express a connection in the Vaisesika system. In the case of a blue lotus, the blue color, which is the located, cannot be separated from the lotus, which is the locus. In the case of a pot on a table, however, the pot (the located) can be removed from its locus, i. e. the table. The first is the case of inherence, the second of conjunction.

The relation between attributes and their substrate, therefore, are inseparable according to the Vaisesika system. Somewhat counter-intuitively, however, the system also teaches that this category of inherence is distinct both from substrate and quality. Therefore, in explaining the substrate-attribute relation, the Vaisesika system employs three categories, namely, the substrate, the attributes, and inherence, which are ontologically distinct from each other. Prasastapada says: “Since we perceive the notion ‘in this place’ with regard to the five categories, inherence is a different category than these \[five categories\]. ”Here the five categories refer to substance (dravya), quality (guna), action (karma), generality (samanya), and particularity (visesa). According to this explanation, attributes or qualities and their substrate are two separate entities, which are inseparably connected through the relation of inherence:

It is this ontological separation between a substrate and its attributes which is rejected by the Vedantists starting with Sankara.

**The Vedantic Rejection of Inherence**

In his commentary on Brahmasutra 2. 2. 13 Sankara rejects the Vaisesika notion of inherence on account of a logical fallacy. The discussion is in the context of rejecting atomism in the Vaisesika school. According to the school, all material entities are made of atoms (paramanu). When two atoms come together, they form a dyad. The Vaisesika school teaches that this dyad is distinct from the two atoms, and the dyad and the atoms are connected through the relation of inherence. This dyad in tum is combined to other dyads to form a larger entity.

*(Picture)*

Sankara rejects this Vaisesika theory of creation by arguing that the notion of inherence leads to an infinite regress, and is therefore untenable:

Precisely in the way a dyad, which is completely distinct from two atoms, is connected to them \[i. e. to the atoms\] through the relation characterised by inherence, in the same way inherence too, which is completely distinct from the objects of inherence, would be connected to the objects of inherence through the relation characterised by inherence, which is different \[from the initial inherence\]. And therefore, we need to imagine another connection for each. Thus it would undesirably result in nothing but infinite regress.

As we saw, the Vaisesika system explains that inherence is in a different category than the objects it connects. Sankara’s objection is that if that were the case, the initial inherence, which connects two atoms with the dyad, would itself require another inherence, which connects the first inherence to the atoms and to the dyad. By the same logic the second inherence then would require a third inherence. In this way there is no end to this process.

Ramanuja and Madhva follow Sankara on their commentaries on Brahmasutra 2.2.13, and reject the Vaisesika concept of inherence similarly arguing that it leads to infinite regress. This discussion on the relationship between attributes and their substrate becomes important for the Vaisnava Vedantists such as Ramanuja and Madhva, who argue that the ultimate reality, Brahman, is positively characterized by attributes (savisesa).

For Sankara, who holds the view that Brahman is without attributes (nirvisesa), a sentence such as satyam jnanam anantam brahma (“Brahman is reality, consciousness, and is infinite”)does not ascribe any attributes to Brahman. Rather, it excludes what Brahman is not. Therefore, according to Sankara, the sentence means “Brahman is not unreal, not unconscious and without limits. ”Sankara in this way rejects any possibility that the Upanisadic sentence might suggest internal division (svagata-bheda) in Brahman.

Ramanuja and Madhva do not approve of Sankara’s apophaticism. For them, the above-mentioned Upanisadic sentence defines Brahman by attributing three qualities to it. In fact, for them Brahman is none other than the deity Visnu, who has unlimited qualities. As soon as they ascribe attributes to Brahman, however, they face the same conundrum as the Vaisesika philosophers do. Are these attributes one with, or different from Brahman? In addition, they also have to deal with one of the fundamental principles of any Vedanta, according to which Brahman is without any internal division (akhanditatva).

Ramanuja answers this challenge by suggesting a new concept of inseparability (aprthak-siddhi). According to this concept, attributes and their substrate are inseparably connected, and ontologically speaking, attributes are both one (abheda) with and different (bheda) from Brahman.In this explanation, the concept of inseparability is relational but not ontological. Unlike the idea of inherence in Vaisesika, inseparability is not something that exists separately from attributes and their substrate.

Madhva finds Ramanuja’s answer unsatisfactory, and provides an alternative. This is where his idea of differentiating capacity (visesa) comes in. According to Madhva, attributes and their substrate are ontologically nondifferent. However, on account of the power of a differentiating capacity, attributes appear to be different from their substrate so as to render a phrase such as “the lotus is blue” non-tautological. Madhva defines the idea of differentiating capacity as follows: “That which regulates \[the use of\] other non-synonymous words in relation to the entity which is devoid of division is taught by the name visesa. It exists entirely within things. ”In the expression “the lotus is blue, ” “lotus” and "blue” are not synonyms. The former is a substrate and the latter is an attribute. Nevertheless, these two words do not denote two separate entities. Rather, they refer to the same entity. This capacity to enable coreferentiality (samanadhikaranya) of more than one word is what Madhva calls differentiating capacity (visesa). In addition, by saying “It (i. e. visesa) exists entirely in the things, ” Madhva rejects the Vaisesika view that attributes, their substrate, and inherence are all ontologically distinct from each other. For him, they are ontologically one. Thus Madhva also avoids any possibility of damaging the unity of Brahman.

Concerning the relation between Brahman and Brahman’s qualities, Jiva’s position seems closer to Ramanuja’s than Madhva’s. In explaining Visnu’s relation to his energy (visnusakti), Jiva says they are both one (abheda) and different (bheda):

Therefore, \[visnusakti and Visnu Himself\] are distinct since it is not possible to consider \[visnusakti\] as non-distinct from \[Visnu’s\] own nature. \[However, they\] are also not distinct since it is not possible to consider \[visnusakti\] as distinct \[from Visnu’s own nature\]. Thus we accept only \[simultaneous\] oneness and difference \[bhedabheda\] of potency and the processor of potency. They are paradoxical \[acintya\] as well.

Technically speaking, Brahman in Jiva’s system denotes a lower aspect of Visnu. However, that differentiation is not relevant in the current discussion. Therefore, for the sake of simplification we can equate Visnu with Brahman in the above quote. Then Jiva is close to Ramanuja since both accept oneness and difference \[bhedabheda\] between Brahman and Brahman’s attributes. What is new in Jiva’s formulation, in comparison to Ramanuja’s, is that he adds that these simultaneous oneness and difference are paradoxical (acintya).

Jiva does not follow Madhva in this regard. For Jiva, both oneness and difference are real. According to Madhva’s understanding of differentiating capacity, only oneness between Brahman and its qualities is real. Their difference is merely a semblance.Various understandings concerning the relation between Brahman and its qualities may be summarized as follows:

Baladeva disagrees with Jiva by adopting the Madhva concept of differentiating capacity. In the following section, however, I shall point out that Baladeva’s application of the concept is uniquely in accordance with the Caitanya Vaisnava teachings.

**Baladeva and the concept of differentiating capacity**

In his analysis of the relation between attributes and their substrate, Baladeva accepts Madhva’s idea of differentiating capacity. As discussed above, Jiva does not use this concept in his works. Therefore, Baladeva actively diverges from Jiva by accepting this concept. Moreover, what is unique in Baladeva’s writing is that he uses the concept to argue for the ontological oneness of Krsna and his beloved Radha. This Caitanya Vaisnava doctrine concerning the unity of Radha and Krsna is not accepted by the Madhvas. From this, it is clear that Baladeva uses the Madhva concept in the service of Caitanya Vaisnava philosophy.

In his Siddhantaratna, Baladeva defines the differentiating capacity as follows:

As for the differentiating capacity, we must accept \[it\]. And it \[i. e. the differentiating capacity\] is a semblance of difference, which, even though there is no difference, manifests the effect of difference, namely, linguistic usage concerning the state of qualities and the quality possessor. \[The differentiating capacity\] also manifests the non-synonimity of the words \[in a sentence/ expression\] such as “existent” \[in the phrase “Existence is existent”\]. Otherwise \[i. e. if we do not accept the differentiating capacity\], uncontradicted linguistic usages such as “Existence is existent”, “Distinction is different”, “Time always exists”, “Place is everywhere” would not be possible.

In agreement with Madhva, Baladeva explains that attributes and their substrate appear to be different on account of the differentiating capacity, although they are ontologically one. This semblance of difference makes the subject-predicate relation meaningful. He then gives examples of the effect of this concept. In the first example, “Existence is existent, ” “existent” (sati) is the attribute which qualifies the substrate, “existence” (satta). Although the quality of “existent” is not something ontologically distinct from its substrate “existence, ” they have different semantic fields. According to Baladeva, this is possible because of the differentiating capacity, which is innate to the substrate. Consequently, the sentence “Existence is existent” is not tautological.

This linguistic discussion is important for Baladeva, who, like other Vaisnava authors, holds the view that Brahman has multiple qualities:

Otherwise \[i. e. if you do not accept the concept of differentiating capacity\], in the sentences such as “Brahman is consciousness and joy”, “Brahman is reality, consciousness, and infinite”, the words such as consciousness, which make known only the nature \[of Brahman\], would become synonymous \[with each other\].

As we have pointed out, for Sankara the words such as “consciousness” in these Upanisadic sentences do not describe Brahman positively but they rather state what Brahman is not. Baladeva rejects this hermeneutical strategy, and claims that these words describe nothing but Brahman’s nature. According to Baladeva, by accepting the concept of a differentiating capacity, we can ascribe attributes to Brahman without jeopardizing the concept of the indivisibility of Brahman. This is because, as Madhva explains, qualities and their substrate are ontologically one, and only appear to be distinct on account of a differentiating capacity. Baladeva diverges from Jiva by accepting the concept of the differentiating capacity. He is, however, logically consistent with two general principles of Vaisnava Vedanta, namely, that Brahman has many qualities, and that Brahman is without any division. In addition, one notable difference between Madhva and Baladeva is Baladeva seems to apply the concept of differentiating capacity to explain the attribute-substrate relation only of eternal entities such as time, place, and Brahman. In contrast, Madhva applies the concept to any entity.

Furthermore, Baladeva’s application of the concept of the differentiating capacity is unique since he uses the concept to argue that Krsna and Radha, his most beloved cowherd lady, are ontologically one. In his Prameyaratnavali 1. 12, Baldeva first states that Visnu has three potencies, among which the highest is none other than the goddess Sri, and she is nondifferent from Visnu.Baladeva’s logic is that just like a substrate and its attributes, Visnu and his energy (sakti) are ontologically one but we can speak of them as separate due to the differentiating capacity. Then he identifies Visnu’s highest energy (parasakti) with Sri. This identification leads to the conclusion that Visnu and Sri are one. Madhva would accept the unity of energy (sakti) and the energy possessor (Saktimat). However, the identification of Visnu’s energy with Sri, and the resultant oneness of Visnu with Sri is not in accord with Madhva’s teachings. For Madhva, Sri, though special, is still a living entity (jiva), and ontologically distinct from Visnu.

Once the oneness of Visnu and Sri is established, Baladeva replaces Visnu with Krsna, and Sri with Radha. Citing the Bhagavata Purana 1. 3. 28,Baladeva states that Krsna is the source of Visnu. As for Radha, he cites a passage attributed to the Gautamiyatantra, according to which Radha is the highest goddess “consisting of all Laksmi. ”Madhva and his followers do not accept this Caitanya Vaisnava emphasis on the pre-eminence of Radha.

Lastly, Baladeva’s understanding and use of the concept of differentiating capacity needs to be discussed in relation to the same concept found in the Vedantasyamantaka written by Radhadamodara, one of Baladeva’s Caitanya Vaisnava teachers. In the work, Radhadamodara uses the concept as follows:

Moreover, its \[i. e. Brahman’s\] unlimited qualities such as consciousness and joy are not different from it \[i. e. Brahman\] since the \[Brhadaranyaka\] Upanisad says, “We should behold \[this\] as singular only”, “There is no diversity here at all. ” Even then, on account of the differentiating capacity, there exists the linguistic usage that \[Brahman’s qualities\] are different from it \[i. e. Brahman\].

This paragraph confirms the singularity of Brahman, based on the two Upanisadic passages. Many qualities are ascribed to Brahman, even though there is no division in Brahman. This, according to Radhadamodara, is possible because of the differentiating capacity of Brahman. He then defines this capacity as follows:

And, the differentiating capacity is the semblance of difference, causing to perceive its \[i. e. the difference’s\] effect even though there is no difference, as seen in \[the sentences\] such as “Existence is existent”, “Distinction is different”, “Time always exists”. Without \[this differentiating capacity\], the condition of qualifiers and qualified and so on would not be possible.

From this paragraph it is clear that Radhadamodara’s definition of and the examples for the differentiating capacity are more or less the same as Baladeva’s.

As in Baladeva’s Prameyaratnavali, the Vedantasyamantaka also claims that Sri as the highest energy of Visnu is nondifferent from him,and that Radha is highest among the goddesses, just as Krsna is highest among God’s various manifestations.

Since the way in which the concept of differentiating capacity is used in the Vedantasyamantaka is very close to how it is used in Baladeva’s works, we would be naturally curious to know who used the concept first. As far as I am aware, however, there is unfortunately no decisive evidence to decide the direction of influence. Therefore, we have the following two scenarios.

If Radhadamodara used it first, then it means that Baladeva used the same concept since his Caitanya Vaisnava teacher already approved of its use. In this case, the credit for the creative use of the concept goes to Radhadamodara, and Baladeva can be seen as his faithful follower. In contrast, if Baladeva used it first, then this means that Radhadamodara was not only willing to learn from Baladeva an important element of Madhva Vedanta but also apply it in his own writing. This then would make an intriguing case study for an inter-denominational influence between the Madhva and the Caitanya Vaisnava traditions.

**Summary**

In this section, we examined Baladeva’s understanding and application of the concept of differentiating capacity in the context of the discussion on the subject- predicate relation in classical Indian philosophy. The concept of the differentiating capacity as Baladeva uses it is derived from Madhva’s teaching, which Madhva established after rejecting the Vaisesika concept of inherence (samavaya), Sankara’s understanding of language, and Ramanuja’s concept of inseparability (aprthaksiddhi). Since Jiva did not use this concept, Baladeva’s use of it is sometimes regarded as evidence for his divergence from Caitanya Vaisnavism. However, the fact that Baladeva uses the concept to establish oneness of Radha and Krsna suggests that he uses the Madhva concept in the service of Caitanya Vaisnavism. We do not know if Baladeva was the very first one in the Caitanya Vaisnava tradition to use the concept since his teacher Radhadamodara uses the same concept in his work. One thing we know for sure, however, is that at least two Caitanya Vaisnava authors saw it as acceptable to use the Madhva concept of differentiating capacity to explain the doctrine unique in their tradition, namely, the ontological oneness of Radha and Krsna.

## Soteriology: Varnasramadharma vs. Bhagavaddharma

As described, Jaisingh II was eager to establish himself as a dharmic king. Dharma in this context is used in the sense of the social conduct that conforms to the norm of orthodox brahmanical culture. One way to accomplish this aim was to support the Brahmanas and their Vedic ritual performances meant for maintaining the cosmic order. In addition, he tried to implement the varnasrama system for the maintenance of the domestic order.In his Bhagavad Gita commentary and in the Govindabhasya, Baladeva gives a novel typology of devotees, a typology which, as far as I am aware, is not found either in the writings of the Madhvas or of the Caitanya Vaisnavas prior to him. In this typology he discusses the position of social duty (varndsramadharma)\[95\]in relation to devotional service dedicated to Visnu / Krsna (bhagavaddharma).

Inherent in the Gaudiya tradition is the tension between the accepted standards of social behavior and complete dedication to God, which supersedes such standards. Consider the example of the gopis in the Bhagavata Purana. The tradition praises them precisely because they take the risk of crossing the boundaries of accepted social behavior so that they can meet Krsna. Thus the idea of subverting the social norm for the transcendent purpose is deeply rooted in Gaudiya theology. The subordination of social norms to religious values, however, tended to encourage practitioners to completely abandon the moral values prescribed by the varnasrama system, and this was not in accordance with the intentions of the king who was responsible for maintaining the social order.

A historical example of such an issue was the case of Rupa Kaviraja, a Gaudiya Vaisnava author who lived in Radhakunda at the beginning of the eighteenth century.In 1727 Jaisingh II denounced Rupa Kaviraja’s teachings and deprived him and his followers of their rights to act as religious authories in any capacity. Based on his interpretation of Rupa Gosvami's Bhaktirasamrtasindhu 1. 2. 295,Rupa Kaviraja taught that if a male practitioner after his physical death wishes to attain the status of the gopis in Vraja, he should imitate the gopis not only internally but also externally.Thus there “roamed renouncers through both Braj and Jaipur who in the name of god-madness sported a religiously or otherwise female persona thereby imitating the exemplary female companions of Krsna. ”Visvanatha Cakravarti, a contemporary of Rupa Kaviraja and one of the Caitanya Vaisnava teachers of Baladeva, rejected Kaviraja’s idea and taught that the male practitioners of raganugabhakti should imitate the gopis only internally but not externally.

The challenge of Rupa Kaviraja, however, was not simply about religious practitioners living in the garb of women. It addressed a deeper theological issue: he insisted that those who pursue the path of raganugabhakti should give up the rules and regulations followed in conventional society.The king banned Rupa Kaviraja’s teaching with the help of his theological counselor Sarvabhauma Krsnadeva Bhattacarya, a disciple of Visvanatha Cakravarti and a representative of the Caitanya Vaisnavas at the court.At the request of the king, he wrote the Karmavivrtti, in which he claimed that all practitioners engaged in raganugabhakti, including the advanced ones, must perform obligatory rituals and follow the rules of ordinary society. Horstmann summarizes his argument:

All agree that a mature devotee... does not commit sin by not performing rituals. So why should he perform rituals?... \[H\]e must abide by rituals, for it is his duty to contribute to “holding the world together” (lokasamgraha). ... The conclusion drawn from this kind of reasoning is that for the sake of “holding the world together”, even the supreme form of bhakti, raganuga, even in its ultimate perfection (mukhyaraganuga) requires that ritual injunctions be faithfully executed.

The term “holding the world together” (lokasamgraha) occurs in Bhagavad Gita 3. 20: “For, Janaka and others achieved perfection only through action. Just for holding the world together, you should act. ”The verse is spoken by Krsna to Arjuna, when Arjuna was considering to give up his social obligation as a warrior, that is, to fight in the battle against his cousins. Krsna instructs Arjuna not to give up his duty but rather to execute it since it is possible to attain perfection through action (karma). Krsna mentions Janakasince he is seen as an ideal king who achieved perfection by performing his royal duties. Given that the Kachvahas traced their lineage back to Rama, perhaps it is reasonable to speculate that Krsnadeva is consciously using the term lokasamgraha to affirm the king’s concern to implement the varnasramadharma among the Vaisnavas.

The fundamental issue here is that in the vaidhi-raganuga continuum Rupa Gosvami did not specify when a practitioner should give up performing obligatory rituals and following the conventional rules of society, if he should do that at all. Thus Rupa Kaviraja rejected following the rules for the raganuga practitioners. In reaction to this Krsnadeva, who had the king’s concern in mind, emphasized the value of “holding the world together” (lokasamgraha), and argued that conventional rules have to be followed by all practitioners, irrespective of their degree of advancement.

Perhaps Krsnadeva went too far. It seems that Baladeva tried to strike a balance by inventing a novel typology. On the one hand, Baladeva understood the king’s concern and how it was reflected in Krsnadeva’s writing. On the other hand, he wished to reassert that bhagavaddharma is in fact superior to varnasramadharma, and therefore those who are absorbed in the former might be exempt from performing the latter.

In the introduction to his commentary on the Bhagavad Gita, Baladeva discusses levels of qualification (adhikara) for studying the text. He categorizes those who are eligible to study the Gita into three groups: Sanistha, Parinisthita, and Nirapeksa:

He who is faithful, devoted to perform his duty, and who has conquered his senses is qualified \[to study\] this scripture \[i. e. the Bhagavad Gita\]. Furthermore, such a \[person\] is of three types because of the distinction between Sanistha, Parinisthita, and Nirapeksa. Among them, the first \[i. e. Sanistha\] is he who steadily performs his own duty as worship of Hari, and desires to experience \[other\] worlds such as Svarga. The second \[i. e. Parinistha\] delights in devotion to Hari, and performs those \[duties\] because of the desire to hold the world together. Both \[i. e. Sanistha and Parinistha\] perform \[the duties of\] their stage in life. The third \[i. e. Nirapeksa\] is one whose mind is completely purified by being truthful, performing austerity, praying and so on, who delights only in Hari, and who is without \[the duties of\] one’s stage in life.

According to Baladeva, the bottom line for one who is desirous to study the Bhagavad Gita is that he should possess faith, perhaps either in the text or in Krsna, the projected speaker of the text. He should also be self-controlled and dedicated to performing his duty (dharma). What does dharma mean in this context? Baladeva explains that it is worshipping the Lord (haryarcana). Conducting the worship of the Lord is common to the first and the second classes of devotees. Devotees in these two categories are also bound by duties according to their stage in life (sasrama). In his commentary on Brahmasutra 3. 4. 1 Baladeva explicitly states that they need to perform duties not only according to their asrama but also according to their class (varna).\[107\]Thus Baladeva makes it clear that the Sanistha and the Parinisthita devotees should not abandon their social obligations while performing their worship of the Lord.

The difference between the first and the second category of devotees, according to Baladeva, is that the Sanistha devotees perform their duties partly due to their desire to experience the different worlds after the death. In contrast, the Parinisthita devotees execute their social obligation out of desire to hold the world together (lokasamjighrksaya). Thus their motivation to perform action is aimed at serving others by setting an example. As we saw, the idea of “holding the world together” (lokasamgraha) was emphasized by Krsnadeva in his Karmavivrtti. In his definition of the Sanistha and the Parinisthita, therefore, Baladeva acknowledges the concern of the king and Krsnadeva.

In his discussion of devotees in the third category, the Nirapeksa, Baladeva seems to disagree with Krsnadeva. Whereas Krsnadeva argued that the duties prescribed by the varnasrama system should be executed by all practitioners without exception, Baladeva says that those in the Nirapeksa category are without such obligation (nirasrama). According to his description, they are purified by their austerity and other practices to such an extent that they need not execute any conventional social obligations.

Baladeva offers a more detailed discussion on this three-part categorization in his commentary on Brahmasutra 3. 4. 32-37. In this section, however, Baladeva uses the term Svanistha for Sanistha. I have not been able to find any explanation for these differences, but he seems to use both sets of the terms synonymously. According to Baladeva, the topic discussed in these sutras is whether those who achieved knowledge of Brahman (vidya) should still perform the duty according to the varnasrama system.Commenting on the six sutras, Baladeva gives an answer for each category of devotees.

Baladeva first discusses the Svanistha devotees. In his commentary on sutra 3. 4. 32 he claims that even after attaining the knowledge of Brahman they should perform their duties prescribed by the varnasrama system since doing so would increase their knowledge.Proceeding to sutra 3. 4. 33, Baladeva clarifies that he is not advocating the idea of “knowledge and action combined” (jnanakarmasamuccaya).He says that only knowledge of Brahman would lead one to liberation, and action is simply a supporting factor (sahakarin).Performing varnasramadharma, according to Baladeva, is a supporting factor since it helps the Svanisthas to attain the knowledge of Brahman, after which action enhances knowledge. Baladeva thus says that the Svanisthas should follow varnasramadharma even after attaining perfection. This view seems to be the closest to that of Krsnadeva, who insisted that everyone, irrespective of their maturity, should follow the rules of ordinary society.

Moving to the discussion on Parinistha or Parinisthita, in his commentary on sutra 3. 4. 34 Baladeva raises a new question. As discussed earlier, the Parinistha devotees perform varnasramadharma for the sake of “holding the world together” while serving the Lord at the same time. If there is a conflict between the two duties, how should they deal with it? Is it acceptable to drop one for the sake of the other?Baladeva answers that if two duties coincide, one should perform bhagavaddharma first, and then varndsramadharma.\[113\]Then in his commentary on sutra 3. 4. 35 he asserts that in case a Parinistha devotee gives up varnasramadharma for the sake of performing bhagavaddharma, he does not incur any fault. Baladeva cites Brhadaranyaka Upanisad 4. 4. 23: “He subdues all calamities. No calamity subdues him. He destroys all calamities. No calamity destroys him. ”In his commentary on the Bhagavad Gita and on Brahmasutra 3.4.1 Baladeva describes the Parinistha devotees as sasrama, that is, they are expected to perform the conventional social duties for the sake of lokasamgraha. Nevertheless, in his commentary on Brahmasutras 3. 4. 34-35 Baladeva emphasizes the superiority of bhagavaddharma over varnasramadharma.

Finally, in his commentary on sutras 3. 4. 36-37, Baladeva discusses the position of the Nirapeksa devotee in relation to the varnasramadharma. Pointing out Gargi,who challenges Yajnavalkya in the Brhadaranyaka Upanisad, as an example, Baladeva argues that those who are nirasrama (without the duties of the varnasramadharma) can also attain the knowledge of Brahman.Commenting on sutra 36 Baladeva explains that even without performing the conventional social duties, a Nirapeksa devotee is already purified because of the meritorious acts performed in previous lives. Then, when such a devotee comes into contact with saintly people, the knowledge of Brahman arises in him.In his commentary on sutra 37, citing two passages from the Bhagavata Purana, Baladeva further emphasizes the importance of the company of saintly people.

How does Baladeva’s categorization differ from Madhva’s? One of the most succinct discussions on this topic by Madhva can be found in his commentary on the Brahmasutra 1. 1. 1: “Then therefore desire to understand Brahman” (athato brahmajijnasa).According to Madhva, apart from its meaning denoting auspiciousness, the word atha denotes eligibility (adhikara), after which there arises the desire to know Brahman.

Then, citing passages from the Bhagavata Tantra,\[119\]Madhva describes who is eligible to inquire about Brahman. According to the cited text, there are three classes of beings who are eligible. The lowest (manda) among them are the best among men. The middling (madhya) ones are the sages and the Gandharvas. The best (uttama) beings are the gods.This classification is determined according to one’s birth (jati).

The Bhagavata Tantra also gives another classification of eligibility according to one’s quality (guna). According to this second classification, the lowest (adhama) are those who studied the Vedas and who have devotion to Visnu as supreme. The middling (madhyama) ones are those who additionally have qualities such as calmness (sama). The highest (uttama) are those who, in addition to the qualities mentioned above, have realized the transitory nature of the world, the quality of dispassion, and who accept Visnu as the only refuge.

Baladeva and Madhva are both concerned with the question of qualification, that is, who is regarded as eligible to understand ultimate reality. Although both classify eligible beings into three, it is clear that Baladeva does not follow either of the two categorizations which Madhva suggests via the Bhagavata Tantra. Besides the terminological differences, Baladeva does not discuss the celestial beings mentioned in Madhva’s first scheme. Although both Madhva’s second scheme and Baladeva emphasize devotion to Visnu or Hari, Baladeva seems to be more concerned with clarifying the role of the varnasramadharma in relation to the worship of the lord (bhagavaddharma).

## Conclusion

Baladeva was a distinguished thinker of Caivanya Vaisnavism in the eighteenth century. As someone who was initially trained in the Madhva tradition, he possessed a thorough command of many branches of Indian philosophy such as Nyaya, Vaisesika, Mimamsa, and various schools of Vedanta. In addition, as a student of prominent teachers such as Radhadamodara and Visvanatha Cakravarti, he was equipped with in-depth knowledge of Caitanya Vaisnavism as well.

What makes Baladeva’s thought exceptional not only in the history of Caitanya Vaisnavism but also in the history of Indian thought, however, is the unique historical circumstance in which Baladeva worked. He was forced to come up with creative solutions to deal with the challenges at hand. Since Jaisingh II wanted the Caitanya Vaisnavas to legitimate their tradition in relation to the four accepted Sampradayas, Baladeva, through his Vedantic works, attempted to establish the Caitanya Vaisnava connection with the Madhva sampradaya.

The Madhva influence on Baladeva is most visible in the list of Caitanya’s lineage he gives in the Prameyaratnavali, and in his acceptance of the concept of differentiating capacity (visesa). With the list, Baladeva genealogically deals with the issue of the sampradaya affiliation. By accepting the concept of differentiating capacity, he philosophically demonstrates the Caitanya Vaisnava affinity with the Madhvas. This, however, is an ingenious strategy since by this concept he simultaneously demonstrates the oneness of Radha and Krsna, which is a doctrine unique in Caitanya Vaisnavism.

While Baladeva possessed a thorough knowledge of the Madhva tradition, he was not a slave to it. When he judged Madhva’s teaching on certain points was not the best, Baladeva was not hesitant to give him up and follow other predecessors’ view. The case in point is his acceptance of the concept of configuration (akrti), which is used by Sabara, Sankara, and Ramanuja, but not by Madhva.

Finally, Baladeva was a creative author who could invent new ideas. The controversy surrounding Rupa Kaviraja’s teaching, and Jaisingh’s denunciation of it posed a new challenge which the Caitanya Vaisnava authors of the sixteenth century were not faced with. To deal with this new circumstance, therefore, Baladeva employed a novel typology of devotees. Through this typology, Baladeva re-emphasized the superiority of bhagavaddharma over varnasramadharma, while acknowledging the king’s concern for “holding the world together (lokasamgraha). ”

In view of what has been discussed, Baladeva emerges as an independent thinker thoroughly trained in various branches of traditional Sanskrit learning, who at the same time was sensitive and flexible enough to successfully meet the challenges of his time. Certainly, the Caitanya tradition owes him a lot to have survived the eighteenth century, which was one of the most turbulent periods in the history of northern India.

## A pearl necklace of propositions: Baladeva Vidyabhusana’s prameyaratnavali

## Preface

*Translated by Ravi M. Gupta*

Although a relatively short work, Baladeva Vidyabhusana’s *Prameyaratnavali* (“A Pearl Necklace of Propositions”) occupies a place of historical significance in Caitanya Vaisnava philosophy. As discussed by Okita in the preceding essay, the text was likely a product of the same historical circumstances that led to Baladeva’s commentary on the *Brahmasutra* in the eighteenth century. The challenges faced by Caitanya Vaisnavas in their new Rajasthani home led to important changes in how they viewed and asserted their religious identity. The Kachvaha ruler, Maharaja Jaisingh II, although deeply supportive of the Caitanya tradition and its sacred images, insisted that the new arrivals demonstrate their affiliation with older Vaisnava lineages, both by providing lists of disciplic succession and by showing theological continuity, especially in terms of Vedanta commentary. This led Caitanya Vaisnavas to assert an affiliation with the Madhva *sampradaya.* Although this claim was likely not new, it had not been a matter of much significance to early Caitanya Vaisnavas, who were content to trace their lineage to Sri Caitanya, whom they regarded as Krsna himself.

The *Prameyaratnavali* in particular, reveals Baladeva’s attempt to satisfy two competing concerns, namely, to demonstrate substantive affiliation with the Madhva tradition while also maintaining the distinctive theological identity of Caitanya Vaisnavas. He finds a useful tool in Vyasatirtha’s *Navaprameya* (“Nine Propositions”), which would have been a well-known statement of Madhva theology in the eighteenth century. Baladeva begins the *PrameyaratnavalT* by stating the nine propositions in his own words and concludes the work by quoting Vyasatirtha's version verbatim, thus affirming his agreement with the fundamental tenets of Madhva thought. Nevertheless, in his explanation and defense of each proposition, Baladeva offers a theology that is uniquely Caitanya Vaisnava, including such topics as Krsna’s threefold *sakti,* the pre-eminent position of Sri Radha, and the classification of devotion as either *vidhi-bhakti* or *ruci-bhakti.* In a manner typical of Vedantic commentary, much of the work consists of carefully selected quotations from the Upanisads, *Bhagavad Gita,* and other scriptural texts.

Now about Sri Visnu’s supremacy:

Indeed, there is nothing to be known beyond this. ”

And in the Sri Bhagavad Gita (7. 7): “Nothing whatsoever is superior to me, O winner of wealth. ”

Because Krsna is the \[original\] cause; because omnipresence, consciousness, blissfulness, and other qualities are inherent in him; because he eternally possesses Laksmi and other energies; therefore Krsna is considered supreme.

11

As to his being the cause of everything, the Svetasvatara Upanisad (5. 4-5) says, “Thus God, the most excellent Bhagavan, rules over those who have the nature of causality. According to those who are learned in the self, the etymology of the word “atma” implies that God has a form of knowledge and joy, and that he is the goal of those who are liberated.

12

And the Brhadaranyaka Upanisad (3. 9. 28) states, “Brahman is knowledge and bliss, the gift of one who gives, and the ultimate aim of one who, standing firm, knows Brahman.”

In the Gopalatapani Upanisad, “He alone, Govinda, is the form of eternity, consciousness, and bliss. ”

The nature of a body made only of consciousness and joy should be understood like \[the form of\] a musical scale. Because words like vijnanaghana (“a mass of knowledge”) are used to describe Him, God has a form. It certainly follows from this that there is no difference between the body and the embodied\[i. e. between God and his body\].

13

This form has the quality of being omnipresent, as stated in the Mundaka Upanisad:\[126\]“The one stands in heaven, fixed like a tree. That person pervades this whole world. ”

14

And in the tenth book of the Sri Bhagavata Purana (10. 9. 13-14), “He who has no inside and no outside, no front and no back, who is inside and outside the universe, in front of and behind it, and who is the universe—he, the unmanifest Adhoksaja, had a human form and was bound with rope to the wooden mortar—like an ordinary child—by the Gopi (Yasoda), who thought of him as her own offspring. ” And in the Sri Bhagavad Gita (9. 4-5), “I pervade this entire world by my unmanifest form. The word ‘yoga’ \[in the above Gita verse\] indicates that the Lord has inconceivable energy (acintya-sakti) that can resolve all contradictions. This is the view of those who know the truth.

There is no difference between an attribute and its substrate (the possessor of the attribute), but because of differentiating capacity (visesa), there is a perception of difference. For example, even the wise accept such notions as “Time exists every day. ”

## Second Proposition

Now begins the second proposition, that he is to be known by all the scriptures.

1

As the Sri Gopala Upanisad says, “That is the one who is sung by all the Vedas. ” And in the Katha Upanisad (2. 15), “Whose abode all the Vedas declare, and for whom they describe all austerities... ”

The Sri Harivamsa says, “In the Veda, in the Ramayana, in the Purana, and in the Mahabharata, in the beginning, in the middle, and in the end—everywhere, Hari is sung. ”

2

All the Vedas, directly or indirectly, sing about Madhava. The Upanisads sing about him directly, while other Vedic texts do so indirectly.

3

Wherever the Vedas state that he is inexpressible by words, the meaning is that he cannot be fully expressed by words. Otherwise the entire endeavor \[of studying the Vedas\] would be useless. This is my opinion.

4

\[An objection: \] The wise say that the attributeless Brahman is inexpressible by words because categories such as species etc., which provide semantic scope to words, do not apply to him.

5

\[Response: \] But if Brahman cannot be expressed by any words, then he cannot even be indirectly suggested by words (laksana). Thus, the attributeless Brahman could not be the suggested meaning \[of the Vedas\], This is my view.

Thus ends the second proposition of the Prameyaratnavali.

## Third Proposition

Now begins the third proposition, on the reality of the universe.

1

“By his own energy, the omniscient Visnu creates a genuine world. ” According to this statement, the world is real. The statements which assert unreality are to encourage renunciation.

To this end, the Svetasvatara Upanisad (4. 1) says, “Alone, with fixed purpose, he who is without color creates varieties of colors using his energy. ”

And the Visnu Purana says, “Just as a fire, situated in one place, spreads its light everywhere, so also the energy of the Supreme Brahman pervades the entire universe. ”

In the Isavasya Upanisad (8), “That person has understood the luminous, unembodied, flawless, without sinews, pure, unaflicted by vice, self-born, all- encompassing wise sage who has granted all desired objects in perpetuity. ”

2

The Visnu Purana says, “Best of sages! This entire world is imperishable and eternal, alternating between appearance and disappearance, birth and destruction. ”

3

The Mahabharata says, “Brahman is real, austerity is real, and Prajapati is also real. All beings are bom from the real, and real also is the world that consists of them. ”

4

In the statement, “This atman alone \[existed in the beginning\]” the world is compared to a bird completely concealed in a forest \[which does exist, even though hidden\]. Thus the world should be considered real, as confirmed by those who know the Vedas.

Thus ends the third proposition of the Prameyaratnavali.

## Fourth Proposition

Now begins the fourth proposition about the difference between the living beings and Visnu.

1

In this regard, the Svetasvatara Upanisad says (4. 6-7), “Two birds of handsome plumage, companions intimately united as friends, dwell on the same tree. Of the two, one eats the sweet fruit, while the other does not eat, but only watches. Although enveloped by the same tree, one person grieves, bewildered by her \[the sakti\] who is not the Lord. But when he sees the other person, the beloved Lord, and his glory, he becomes free of grief. ”

2

The indicators by which one can decide the meaning \[of a text\] are: the introduction (upakrama), conclusion (upasamhara), repetition (abhyasa), originality (apurvata), objective (phalam), subordinate statements of praise (arthavada), and argument (upapatti).

The wise say that these six indicators of meaning prove difference \[between living beings and God\]. Thus, difference is understood by the \[above\] text.

3

Furthermore, we find in the Mundaka Upanisad (3. 1. 3), “When the seer sees the golden-hued Person, the origin of Brahman, the Lord and creator, then the wise person becomes pure, and shaking off both merit and misfortune, attains the highest equanimity. ”

And the Katha Upanisad (2. 4. 15) says, “Gautama! Just as pure water poured into pure water becomes alike, so also becomes the atman of the wise sage. ”

And in the Sri Bhagavad Gita (14. 2), “After devoting oneself to this knowledge, those who have attained my nature do not take birth again during creation, nor do they suffer during dissolution. ”

From these statements, which affirm difference even after liberation, it follows that difference is an absolute fact.

4

“I am Brahman, and I am the only living being. There are no other living beings besides me, nor is there a God. They are creations of my own ignorance. ” Such thinking is corrupted. Otherwise, the meaning of statements like the following would not make sense.

In this regard, the Katha Upanisad (2. 5. 13) states, “There is an eternal among eternals, a sentient among sentients. That one, who fulfills the desires of the many, the wise perceive within themselves, and thus they achieve everlasting peace. Others do not. ”

5

The many living beings are different from the single, eternal, and sentient Lord, and he is different from them. Therefore, the difference between them is eternal.

6

Because their functions are dependent on prana alone, the speech and other organs are called prana. In the same way, the world is said to be Brahman because its functions depends on Brahman.

In this regard, we read in the Chandogya Upanisad(5. 1. 15), “One does not call them speeches, sights, hearings and minds. One calls them pranas because prana. indeed, becomes all of these. ”

7

Since Brahman is all-pervading, some regard the world as Brahman. As stated in the Visnu Purana, “O Divine Lord! You are this host of gods that has come into your presence, because you are the creator of the world, present everywhere. ”

8

The two theories of pratibimba (reflection) and pariccheda (limitation), which have been adopted by others, are rejected by the learned because Brahman is all- pervading and not a material object \[and therefore cannot be reflected or divided by a limiting adjunct\].

9

The non-duality that you talk about is either different or nondifferent from Brahman. If it is different from Brahman, then you have the problem of duality. If it is nondifferent from Brahman, then you are establishing what has already been established by scripture.

Brahman without qualities is a false idea, due to lack of evidence. The learned should not believe it. Thus spoke those who teach the truth.

Thus ends the fourth proposition of the Prameyaratnavali, on the matter of the reality of difference.

## Fifth Proposition

Now, that the living beings are servants of Bhagavan:

1

About this, the Svetasvatara Upanisad (6. 7) states, “Among lords, he is the highest, great Lord; Among gods, he is the highest God; among masters, he is the highest master. He is higher than the highest. Let us know the praiseworthy God, Lord of the universe. ”

2

And in the smrti, “Brahma, Sambhu, the sun, the moon, Indra, the foremost gods as well as the others—all are endowed by power of Visnu. ”

“The gods, including Brahma, Rudra and Indra, together with the great seers, worship Narayana, the best of gods, Lord Hari. ”

The Padma Purana describes the characteristic of living beings, “He is the servant of Hari, and never of anyone else. ”

Thus ends the fifth proposition of the Prameyaratnavali, on the matter of servitude to Bhagavan.

## Sixth Proposition

Now begins discussion of the gradation among living beings.

1

The living entities are all equal because they all have a form of infinitesimal consciousness, they all possess knowledge, and so on. And yet, there is gradation among them due to \[different\] methods of practice.

2

About their inifinitesimality, the Svetasvatara Upanisad (5. 9) says, “A hundred parts of the tip of the hair, further divided into one hundred parts—one such part should be known as the (size of the) living being (jiva). And yet, it partakes of infinity. ”

About the living being’s having a form of consciousness, possessing knowledge and so on, the Prasna Upanisad (4. 9) says, “This person is the conscious self; he alone is the seer, toucher, hearer, smeller, taster, thinker, knower, and actor. ”

3

The phrase “and so on” refers to the quality of pervading the body, as stated in the Sri Bhagavad Gita, “Bharata! As one sun illuminates this entire wold, so also does the owner of field \[the self\] illuminate the entire field \[the body\]. ”

And the author of the Brahmasutra (2. 3. 24) states, “Or by its quality \[of consciousness, the self illuminates the entire body\], like \[a flame illuminates\] a room. ”

The Brhadaranyaka Upanisad (4. 5. 14) confirms that these qualities are eternal, “My dear, this self (atma) is imperishable and indestructible by nature. ”

4

Thus, although there is equality, the wise say that there is also diversity—arising in this world due to karma, and in the other due to differences in devotion.

As stated in Chandogya Upanisad (3. 14. 1), “As a person’s resolve is in this life, so will it be when he goes to the next. ”

And the smrti says, “Whatever a person’s devotion is like, such is the state he will attain. ”

There are five kinds of emotion through which one can meditate upon God, beginning with placid love and ending with amorous love. Thus considered, there are differences among people.”

Thus ends the sixth proposition of the Prameyaratnavali, on the subject matter of the gradation among living entities.

## Seventh Proposition

Now, that the attainment of Sri Krsna is liberation.

1

As it has been said (Svetasvatara Upanisad 1. 11), “After knowing God, all fetters fall away... ” “The one omnipresent controller is Krsna. He is to be praised... ” (Gopalatapani Upanisad).

In many ways and in many forms does he appear, but Krsna is the master himself. After seeking him, the liberated souls remain happy in his eternal abode.

Thus ends the seventh proposition of the Prameyaratnavali, that the state of liberation is attainment of Sri Krsna.

## Eighth Proposition

Now that the cause of liberation is one-pointed devotion.

1

As stated in the Sri Gopalatapani Upanisad, “Devotion means worshipping him (Krsna). Worship means fixing the mind on him, by alternative goals. This alone is unmotivated (devotion). ”

In the Narada Pancaratra, “Service that is free of all alternative goals, devoid of impurity, and which is done by the senses for the lord of the senses, having him as their highest object, is called ‘bhakti’. ”

2

This bhakti becomes nine-fold. As it is said in the Sri Bhagavata Purana (7. 5. 23- 24), “Hearing, chanting, remembering Visnu, serving his feet, worshiping, praising, service, friendship, and complete dedication of oneself: people offer these nine kinds of bhakti to Visnu. If one acts in this way for Bhagavan, I consider that to be the best thing learned. ”

This devotion to Bhagavan is obtained only if there is service to saints and the guru, treating them as though they were God, and not in any other way.

3

About serving saints as if they were God, the Taittiriya Upanisad (1. 11. 2) says, “Let your guest be like God! ”

Bhakti for him (Krsna) arises by such service, as stated in the Sri Bhagavata Purana (7. 5. 32), “As long as one does not bathe in the dust of the feet of these great souls, who are without possession, one’s mind will not touch the feet of wide- striding Visnu. At that is the means by which all undesirable things are removed. ”

4

The Taittiriya Upanisad (1. 11. 2) also speaks of service to the guru as though he were God: “Let your teacher be like God! ”

And in the Svetasvatara Upanisad(6. 23), “To that great soul who has complete devotion for God—and as for God, so also for the guru—all the expressed meanings \[of the Upanisads\] are revealed. ”

Bhakti for Krsna arises by such service, as stated in the Sri Bhagavata (11. 3. 21-22): “Therefore, one who wants to know the highest good should resort to a guru who is deeply learned in scripture and in the Supreme Brahman, and who is completely tranquil in mind.

Then, treating the guru as his personal deity, the disciple should guilelessly and obediently learn those Vaisnava duties that can satisfy Hari, who gives his own self to the devotee. ”

5

One who has received the five sacred rites (samskaras) and the two kinds of devotion directly sees Lord Hari and delights eternally in His abode.

6

The five samskaras are described in smrti, “Heating, marking the forehead, the name, the mantra, and sacrifice—these are the five samskaras that lead to the highest one-pointedness. ”

In this verse, “heating” indicates wearing the marks created by a heated cakra or other signs. It also refers to marking the names of Hari, etc.

About this, smrti says, “A person should mark his lineage with the letters of Hari’s name, using sandalwood or other such material. That person purifies the world and attains Hari’s world. ”

The most auspicious forehead mark is a straight upwards line on the forehead. The scriptures speak of it in many ways—as signifying the temple of Hari, as the shape of his feet, as a carrier of great auspiciousness, and so on.

Now, saints say that the (mark of the) name shows that one is a servant of Hari. Mantras, like the one with eighteen syllables, are regarded as the body of one’s worshipable Deity.

The word “sacrifice” refers to the worship of Salagrama and other sacred forms. The standards (for this worship) are seen by the sages in the Puranas.

7

Nine-fold bhakti can be of two kinds, vidhi-bhakti (devotion motivated by scriptural injunction) and ruci-bhakti (devotion motivated by relish). Through bhakti, Krsna himself becomes pleased and gives his devotees whatever abodes they desire.

8

In vidhi-bhakti, God is worshipped as having a form with four or more hands, whereas in ruci-bhakti one adores his human form.

9

Residing in a holy place, one should worship the Tulasi, Asvattha, and Dhatri plants. The day of Hari \[Ekadasi\] should be abandoned \[and observed the following day\] if the lunar day begins after dawn. Similarly, Krsna’s birthday and other occasions should be abandoned if the lunar day begins after sunrise.

10

With a view to keeping order in the world, the wise devotee should steadily perform the obligatory and optional duties, but should never disregard the preeminence of bhakti.

11

One should vigilantly avoid the ten offenses to the name (of Krsna).

12

Bhakti that results in the attainment of Krsna is here called “one-pointed. ” When such devotion is accompanied by knowledge and detachment, the result becomes immediately visible.

Thus ends the eighth proposition of Prameyaratnavali, on the matter of pure bhakti as the giver of liberation.

## Ninth Proposition

And now that pratyaksa, anumana, and sabda alone are the means of valid knowledge (pramana).

1

As Sri Bhagavata Purana says, “The four \[pramanas\] are sruti (scriptural revelation), perception (pratyaksa), tradition (aitihya), and inference (anumana)? '

2

Since tradition can be included within perception, the teacher has said that the means of valid knowledge (pramana) are of only three kinds. Among them, sruti is foremost.

3

Both perception and inference can be corrected with the help of revelation. Perception can be misleading, as when one sees an illusory head.

Inference also leads to error, as when one sees dense smoke on a mountain and infers that there must be fire, which in reality has been extinguished by rain. Therefore, perception and inference are not considered independent means of gaining valid knowledge.

4

Argumentation that conforms \[to the Vedas\] is approved, but dry \[groundless\] argument is avoided.

5

About this, the Brhadaranyaka Upanisad (2. 4. 5) states, “Indeed, the self should be seen, heard, contemplated, and meditated upon, O Maitreyi. ”

In the Katha Upanisad (2. 9), “Dear Naciketas, this understanding cannot be reached by argumentation, but it is easily known when it is taught by another. ”

6

In the smrti: ‘“Which meaning would be appropriate here, so that there is consistency with preceding and subsequent passages? ’ This kind of deliberation is \[appropriate\] argument, but dry argumentation should be avoided. ”

7

Because understanding of Brahman does not arise in those who do not know the Vedas, and because Brahman is known through the Upanisads, therefore scriptural revelation (sruti) is considered the foremost \[means of valid knowledge\].

About this, the sruti says, “One who is ignorant of the Vedas does not understand the Great One. ”

“I ask about the person who is known through the Upanisads. ” (Brhadaranyaka Upanisad 3. 9. 26).

Thus ends the ninth proposition the Prameyaratnavali, namely, that there are three means of valid knowledge.

## Conclusion

hus said the ancients:

1

In the opinion of Sriman Madhva,

1\. Hari is the Supreme

2\. The world is real

3\. The differences are real

4\. The hosts of living beings are followers of Hari

5\. They have higher and lower states

6\. Liberation is the experience of one’s own happiness

7\. Spotless devotion is the means of attaining it

8\. Perception, etc., are the three means of valid knowledge

9\. By all the Vedas only Hari is to be known

2

The wise should keep this Prameyaratnavali in their hearts with reverence, as it contains the nine jewel-like propositions composed by Anandatirtha (Madhva).

3

May Murari (Krsna), who is the life of Sri Caitanya, eternally reside within our hearts. By his compassion, the faultless king of elephants (or the king of Odisha, Gajapati Prataparudra) became completely happy.

Thus the Prameyaratnavali, composed by Baladeva Vidyabhusana, is completed.

Sharma points out the advantage of accepting the concept of *visesa* as follows:

"The conception of the relation between substance and attributes is a very difficult one. If they are identical, the distinction of ‘substance’ and ‘attributes’ is meaningless. We would have substance alone or attributes alone, in that case and not both of them. If the two are different, their relation becomes a purely external one. If they are related internally by *Samavaya* this relation itself has to be related to the terms and so on ad infinitum. The only way in which a regress can be avoided, on any one of these alternatives, would be by agreeing to invest the first relation itself with a certain capacity to take care of itself, explain itself, and relate itself to the relata, without waiting to be explained or related to its terms by another relation. Since an appeal has thus ultimately to be made to the self-explicability o f the relation, it will be wiser, more economical and expedient, to invest the substance itself with such an intrinsic capacity of integrating its attributes into a homogeneous whole, with itself, without prejudice to their distinction of references according to exigencies, and without the need for any external relation. This intrinsic, capacity of substances is proposed to be called ‘Visesas’. ” B. N . K. Sharma, *Philosophy of S ri M adhvacarya* (Delhi, 2002 \[1962\]), pp. 74-5.

The five daily duties outlined in Pancaratra texts are *abhigamana* (approaching the Lord), *upadana* (collecting materials for worship), *ijya* (conducting worship), *adhyayana* (study of scripture), and *yoga* (meditation).

The preceding verses summarize a complex debate between Advaitins and Vaisnava Vedantins regarding the function and use of scripture. The former hold that the Vedas are able to only indirectly point to Brahman who is attributeless and therefore inexpressible by words. Baladeva rejects the idea that Brahman is without qualities (see first proposition) and that he cannot be directly described by revelatory scripture.
