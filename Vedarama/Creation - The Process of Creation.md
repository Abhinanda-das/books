# Creation - The Process of Creation

## The process of creation

*By Steven Rosen*

Speculations about the creation of the universe abound. They always have. For example, Hesiod's “Theogony,” written in the eighth century B.C., is supposed to explain what came before the gods. There were the Titans, six female and six male; one of them, Cronus, successfully devoured all but one of his offspring. Where did the Titans come from? From Uranus, the sky and the stars, and from Gaea, the deep-breasted earth. And before Uranus and Gaea, in the dark beginning, there was only chaos.

Physicists, on the other hand, have their own account of creation. Until a few years ago it was the Big Bang. According to that theory, the universe has been leisurely expanding and cooking for 15 billion years, after a beginning in which all matter was crammed together at unimaginable temperature and density. There is little doubt about the expansion: astronomers have known since the 1920's that the galaxies are coasting away from each other. Tracing the story backwards reveals increasing heat and disruption. Before galaxies and stars and planets there was a vast unstructured soup of atoms. Before that, when the universe was only a few hundred thousand years old, the heat was so great that atoms could not hold together. Rather, they disintegrated into nuclei and electrons. Earlier still, in the first minute after creation, even the nuclei of atoms could not form in the high heat. And so on back to the beginning.

Although generally accepted, the Big Bang theory had serious problems, like the many creation theories before it. Also, it was dissapointingly vague about what happened in the first hundredth of a second. The Inflationary Universe, on the other hand, a brilliant new cosmology that not only seems to rescue the Big Bang, but, in the process, carries scientists back to the very first moments of time, is now considered the most accurate model of creation.

This new theory is developed in James Trefil's book, Moment of Creation. Fragments of the new theory have been leaking out from scientific journals and conferences for several years, but this is the first comprehensive and readable account for the layman. It is a rare glimpse of scientific revolution in progress.

The Inflationary Universe theory hinges on the idea that just after creation, the universe went through a brief and rapid expansion before and after which everything expanded at the relatively slow rate of the Big Bang theory. The rush, it is said, was over in one-billionth of a trillionth of a second after creation.

When he stumbled on this unorthodox idea, Alan Guth, a young theoretical physicist now at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, was studying some other recent concepts in physics; the so-called grand unified theories (GUTS to aficionados). These theories propose that the four forces of nature - gravity, electromagnetism, the strong nuclear force that is responsible for holding subatomic particles within the atomic nucleus, and the weak nuclear force that is responsible for certain kinds of radioactivity - are actually manifestations of one or two more fundamental forces.

For the last 30 years of his life, Einstein struggled in vain to unify two of these forces, gravity and electromagnetism. Most physicists believe Eienstein had the right idea, if not the right details. Now it is said that physicist Stephen Hawking will accomplish what Eienstein set out to do. However, it is recognized that as early as the 1960's, Sheldon Glashow, Abdus Salam and Steven Weinberg had suggested how to unify electromagnetism and the weak nuclear force, a union which is now experimentally confirmed. In the mid-1970's, physicists predicted the approximate the approximate temperature above which three of the forces would merge. Such heat could only be found in an infant universe. And thus came the marriage of GUTS and creation: it was just at the moment when the once-joined forces began to differentiate, as Mr. Guth calculated with pencil and paper in 1980, that space launched into rapid inflation.

Miraculously, then, the problems of the Big Bang theory are now either solved or deflated. One troubling concern, for example, is why the universe today appears so uniform on the large scale. Traditionally, there have been two possible explanations: either it could have just happened that way, or any initial anomolies could have smoothed themselves out, much as hot and cold water in a bathtub will come to the same temperature by exchanging heat. Heat exchange, however, takes time, and we observe equal temperature regions of the universe so far apart that they could not have had time since creation to transfer heat.

The first explanation runs counter to the typical physicist's passion to explain everything. In the Inflationary Universe, the time period of rapid expansion could have taken an initial patch of space so tiny as to be much smaller than the nucleus of an atom, and stretched it into what would become the entire observable universe, maintaining some sort of similarity all the while. The Big Bang theory would not have allowed enough time to fill so large a volume with one such initial patch and would have needed many adjacent patches simultaneously.

In an attempt to prepare us for the inflationary model, Trefil has organized his book into three parts: the first part summarizes the Big Bang theory (most of which still holds) and indicates its problems, the second discusses the physics of elementary particles, forces and the ideas behind GUTS, and the third returns to cosmology with the Inflationary Universe. Trefil, a physicist at the University of Virginia, has taken on a truly ambitious task and has done it mostly well, considering the speculative nature of this whole affair.

Inveitably, there are some slightly erroneous statments, some vague explanations, some perhaps unwarranted emphasis on the author's own research interests. The book does not have the tightness and authority of Steven Weinberg's The First Three Minutes, a clear exposition of the Big Bang theory published in 1977. Trefil does not have the obvious assets of Lewis Thomas and Roger Swain, the clarity of Steven Jay Gould or the elegence of Freeman Dyson.

In at least one respect, however, Trefil surpasses almost all the other scientists writing about science for the public: in “The Moment of Creation”, as in his previous books and articles, one senses a real person talking about the physical world. Trefil says its hard to believe gravity is the weakest of forces after you've helped a friend move a sofa up three flights of stairs. Such humor and charm is very welcome in the dry arena of science communication. Trefil's book trembles with the vigor of physicists and astronomers who believe that they have just caught a view of creation. Certainly, not all has been solved, and fresh problems tend to arise. But it looks like a good bet that the beginning was not chaos. It was one of unified force, rich with potential - to be uncovered and contemplated 15 billion years later in the minds of rather small creatures on a small planet.

Many scientists, in fact, are dissatisfied with their colleagues' puny theories about universal origins because these tend to eliminate even the possibility of God. Albert Einstein said in his last days, “I want to know how God created the universe. Everything else is merely detail.” Obviously, to get the overall picture, we need more scientists like Einstein.

Modern scientific theory presents just that - theory. And after every theory is revised, we find a new theory. The only thing that is definite is that the scientists don't know anything for sure. Knowledge of the creation of the universe can be had from another source, a spiritual source. As soon as we hear this, however, we are taken aback. Our thoughts flood with the inadaquacies of religous dogma. We remember that the Bible presents two distinct and divergent accounts of creation. The first, Genesis 1:1-2:4 , is similar to the Babylonian creation myth, the chief difference being that, in the Babylonian story, the creation of the heavenly bodies precede the formation of the earth, thus avoiding the absurdity of having three "evenings and mornings" - the division of day from night - before there was a Sun in the sky.

The second creation story found in Genesis is an obvious adaption of a second Babylonian legend which was inscribed in Sumerian characters and is therefore much older than the first version.

But the very fact that Genesis includes two stories of creation indicates that both are dubious. Which one is correct? Are either of them correct? Why are there two stories, both divergent and both based on earlier Babylonian accounts, in the Bible? Obviously, the writers of the Bible were in a quandary as to the real story of creation. Thus, modern man is naturally skeptical when told of spiritual solutions.

However, before the Biblical and Babylonian stories even took their birth, there was an account, so detailed and so scientific, and yet also the product of a scriptural tradition, that could be accepted as reasonable and noteworthy. This is the Vedic concept of creation. It is by far the earliest and the most detailed.

Dr. Carl Sagan, in a show filmed in India for the Cosmos television series, praised the Vedic conception of creation, noting that of all religious thought, the ancient Vedic teachings about the origin of the universe “are the most acceptable in terms of modern science”.

Sagan explained that the Vedic science of creation is not very different from some of the modern scientific theories about universal origins, for it suggests that creation and dissolution of the universe occur at regular intervals lasting billions of years.

Briefly stated, the Vedic principle of creation can be described as "emanationism," the concept that matter is not suddenly created out of nothing. Matter is one of the external energies of God. Under His supervision material universes are generated and undergo predetermined, measured cycles of creation, maintenance, and destruction. The periods of manifestation and non-manifestation, according to the Vedas, endure for a period of 4.3 billion years.

According to Bhagavad-gita, a section of the Mahabharata, the essential creative principle behind universal creation is the implantation of spirtual energy in material matter. Matter is endowed with the ingredients for growth and development, but spirit provides the energy that initiates the growth and development process. An analogy of this process can be observed within the womb of a human female, which provides all the ingredients for the production of a human body. The new body cannot begin to develop, however, without the introduction of the male spermatozoa - which provides the initial impetus for human growth.

The Vedas contain the earliest known records of the creation process and were written thousands of years before Darwin wrote his theory of evolution. They elaborately describe a complex process of evolution beginning from the most subtle (conciousness) to atual physical manifestation. It could be called inverse evolution. This theory reflects the original meaning of the word evolution, which refers to an unfolding of something that already exists in an undeveloped form. The modern day scientific theory of evolution on the other hand describes the random production of something entirely new by a physical process.

The Vedic theory begins with a universal controller who is a super intelligent entity. This ultimate integer generates a principle subordinate controller who in turn generates secondary controllers. All of this is done by an asexual process. The secondary controllers engage in sexual reproduction of not only their own kind but of other species as well. Thier bodies contain design information for varieties of organisms. This information is originally present in a seed-like subtle form in the universal controller. He transmits this information to the secondary controllers known as devas who manifest the design information in varieties of species who then sexually reproduce themselves.

There are similarities between the Darwinian and the Vedic conception of evolution. Both involve physical decent from a common ancestor and the appearence of new species by sexual reoproduction. They differ principally inasmuch as the common ancestor in the Vedic theory is a super intelligent being while in Darwin's theory it is a one-celled entity—thus the term inverse evolution which has its begining beyond Earth.

The concept of design information being transmitted from a higher source is not unknown to modern science. Scientist Robert Broom, who discovered some of the early australopathecus remains in Africa, wrote, "The origin of species and much of evolution appears to be due to some organising and partly imntelligent spiritual agency associated with the animal or plant, which controlls its life process and tends to keep the being more or less adapted to its enviornmrnt. But in addition to this there seem to be other spiritual agencies of a much higher type which have been responsible for what may be called greater evolution.... These spiritual agencies appear to have worked by directing from time to time the inferior agencies which are associated with the animals and plants." Broom at least shares with the Vedic conception the idea of higher directing intelligence.

Similarly Alfred Russel Wallace, the cofounder of the theory of evolution, wrote in The World of Life, "If there is such an Infinite Being, and if... his will and purpose is the increase of conscious beings, then we can hardly be the first result of this purpose. We conclude, therefore, that there are now in the universe infinite grades of power, infinite grades og knowledge and wisdom, infinite grades of influence of higher beings upon lower. I have suggested that the vast and wonderful universe, with its almost infiite variety of forms, motions, and reactions of part upon part, from suns and systems up to plant life, animal life, and the human living soul, has ever required and stil requires the continuous coordinated agency of myriads of such intelligences." Unlike many of todays's scientists Wallace at least accepted that the universe has a purpose.

Those seeking greater meaning and purpose in their lives may find light in the Vedic conception. Although we may be less aquainted with it, the Vedic literature has much to share with us regarding cosmology. A careful study will reveal the logic and reasonableness of its conclusions. The sincere and scientifically-minded people would do well to research the Vedas, especially in these days when for many neither the Biblical account of creation or the scientific world view are very satisfying.
