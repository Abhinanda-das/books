# Dhvani in Grammar

## Dhvani in grammar: The Kashmiris’ burden of proof

The Dhvanikara introduced the notion of poetical suggestiveness: *ukty-antarenasakyam yat tac carutvam prakasayan* \| *sabdo vyanjakatam bibhrad dhvany-ukter visayi-bhavet* \|\| (*Dhvany-aloka* 1.15). In addition, he started the usage of the word *dhvani* as “implied sense”, and this, he said, was substantiated by scholars: *yatrarthah sabdo va tam artham upasarjani-krta-svarthau \|* *vyanktah kavya-visesah sa dhvanir iti suribhih kathitah* \|\| (*Dhvany-aloka* 1.13).

Abhinavagupta explains that the poetical term *dhvani* (implied meaning) is sourced in the grammarians’ usage of the term *dhvani* which has the sense of *vaikrta-dhvani* (echo): *sruyamanesv iti, srotra- saskulim santanenagata antyah sabdah sruyanta iti prakriyayam sabda-jah sabdah sruyamana ity uktam. tesam ghantanuranana- rupatvam tavad asti, te ca dhvani-sabdenoktah. yathaha bhagavan bhartrharih,* “*yah samyoga-vibhagabhyam karanair upajanyate, sa sphotah sabda-jah sabda dhvanayo ’nyair udahrtah*.” *evam ghantadi-nirhlada-sthaniyo ’nunaranatmopalaksito vyangyo ’py artho dhvanir iti vyavahrtah.*

“Regarding *sruyamanesu* (in the *vrtti*), the last sounds arrive in the ears as a continuity and are heard. In this manner it is said that sounds originating from sound are being heard. Those sounds are simply the form of the echo of a bell, and they are expressed with the word *dhvani*. For example, the highly venerable Bhartrhari said: “The propagators of another school say: What is produced by the organ of speech by means of contact and disjunction \[of the tongue with the places of pronunciation in the mouth\] is called Sphota. The *dhvanis* (*vaikrta-dhvanis*) are sounds originating from sound” (*Vakya-padiya* 1.102). *Dhvani* is thus used in the sense of being a meaning, an implied one, which is partially indicated, which has the nature of an echo and which is akin to the resonant sound of a bell” (*Locana* 1.13).

The Dhvanikara used the word *anuranana* (echo) as a synonym of *dhvani* (implied sense): *avivaksita-vacyasya pada-vakya- prakasata* \| *tad-anyasyanuranana-rupa-vyangyasya ca dhvaneh \|\|* (3.1). All the major poetical theorists after him did so too.

Abhinavagupta was referring to Anandavardhana’s *vrtti* (elaboration). Ananda sought to prove that the usage of Suggestiveness (*vyanjakatva, or vyanjana*) is sourced in the grammarians’ methodology because, he said, the grammarians are foremost scholars and because the Dhvanikara alluded to scholars: “*suribhih kathitah*” (1.13) *iti vidvad-upajneyam uktih, na tu yatha-kathancit pravrtteti pratipadyate. prathame hi vidvamso vaiyakaranah, vyakarana-mulatvat sarva-vidyanam. te ca* ***sruyamanesu varnesu dhvanir*** *iti vyavaharanti. tathaivanyais tan-matanusaribhih suribhih kavya-tattvartha-darsibhir vacya- vacaka-sammisrah sabdatma kavyam iti vyapadesyo* ***vyanjakatva- samyad*** *dhvanir ity uktah.* (*Dhvany-aloka* 1.13 *vrtti*).

But here Anandavardhana says the word dhvani in the Grammarians’ system is a revealer (*vyanjaka*) (i.e. the *prakrta-dhvanis* reveal the *sphota*). There is a similarity in name only: There is no suggestiveness proper in grammar, because the grammarians do not acknowledge the function called Suggestiveness.

Abhinavagupta too said an absurdity: He deied all logic by stating that in Patanjali’s theory of Sphota, the word *dhvani* signiies not only *vyanjaka-sabda* (*prakrta-dhvani*) but also *vyanjaka artha* (a meaning that reveals).

According to Nagesa Bhatta, the concept of Sphota originates from a sage called Sphotayana, a grammarian mentioned by Panini: *avan sphotayanasya* (*Astadhyayi* 6.1.123). There are two main theories of Sphota: external (Patanjali’s theory) and internal (Bhartrhari’s theory). They have very little in common. Dr. Subrahmania Iyer writes: “The word *sphota* occurs already in the *Mahabhasya* of Patanjali on P. I.1.70; but there it is not applied to the meaning-bearing element.” In *Vakya-padiya*, Bhartrhari mentioned many Sphota theories and propounded his own. Since the Kashmiri Shaivites disagreed with Bhartrhari’s philosophy at large, they only referred to Patanjali’s theory.

## Patanjali’s theory of sphota

In this theory, the term Dhvani has two meanings: *prakrta-dhvani* (phoneme) and *vaikrta-dhvani* (prolongation, echo). The *prakrta-dhvanis* are the audible form of the *sphota*, i.e. the word that the speaker has in mind and utters. And if those *prakrta-dhvanis* are prolonged, they become *vaikrta-dhvanis.*

Patanjali propounded his Sphota theory in his commentary on a sutra by Panini: *tat-paras tat-kalasya* (*Astadhyayi* 1.1.70). That aphorism only refers to the length of the sounds of vowels (*hrasva, dirgha, pluta*). Patanjali comments: *evam tarhi sphotah sabdah, dhvanih sabda-gunah. katham, bhery-aghata-vat, tadyatha bhery- aghato bherim ahatya kascid vimsati padani gacchati kascit trimsat kascic catvarimsat, sphotah ca tavan eva bhavati, dhvani-krta vrddhih, dhvanih sphotas ca sabdanam dhvanis tu khalu laksyate, alpo mahams ca kesancid ubhayam tat-svabhavatah.*

“Thus, *sphota* is *sabda* (sound, word). *Dhvani* is the quality of sound. How is it so? It is like beating a kettledrum. That is as follows: Some drummer beats a kettledrum and goes for twenty steps. Some other drummer beats a kettledrum and goes for thirty steps, and some other drummer beats a kettledrum and goes for forty steps. The sound (*sphota*) is just to that extent (beating the drum one time) (it remains unchanged); the increase was done by the echo (*dhvani*, i.e. *vaikrta-dhvani*). *Dhvani* and sphota take place, but only the *dhvani* of the sounds that is noticed is little and great. In intelligible sounds, both *sphota* and *dhvani* occur, owing to their nature” (*Mahabhasya* 1.1.70).

Commenting on this, Nagesa Bhatta explains that Patanjali used the term *dhvani* in two ways: the phonemes and their prolongation: *dhvanih sabda-guna iti bhasye dhvani-sabdena varna vaikrta- dhvanis ca* (*Mahabhasya-pradipoddyota* 1.1.70). Kaiyata says the phonemes are the revealer and the *sphota* is the revealed: *dhvanih sphotas ceti, vyanjako vyangyas cety arthah* (*Mahabhasya-pradipa* 1.1.70).The difference between Patanjali’s theory and Bhartrhari’s theory can be illustrated as follows: The word *kamala* (lotus) occurs after hearing the six phonemes *k-a-m-a-l-a*. The *sphota* is revealed after the pronunciation of the last letter (although sometimes a person gets the *sphota* before hearing the last letter, especially when a word in the Vedas is incomplete, as in the word *srnga* in the *Rg-Veda* verse that begins catvari *srnga*, cited ahead, where *srnga* stands for *srngani*). In Patanjali’s theory, *sphota* means *sabda* (the word in the speaker’s mind which becomes audible). In Bhartrhari’s theory, however, the last letter activates—in the mind—an ethereal thing (which he calls *sphota*) from which the meaning of the sound *kamala* comes forth. In Bhartrhari’s theory, the *sphota* of *kamala* is called a *pada-sphota* (the *sphota* of a word). Still, Bhartrhari specialized in expounding the *vakya-sphota* (the *sphota*, “lash of insight”, of a sentence). Sometimes, both *sphotas* occur at the same time: When we speak, we know what we are going to say, obviously, but on occasion we grasp another sense of our words upon hearing them. That second sense is a *khanda-vakya-sphota*, whereas Bhartrhari’s *sphota* is the *akhanda-vakya-sphota*.

Dr. Korada Subrahmanyam explains Patanjali’s theory:

**Dhvanis manifest the Sphota. There are ten dhvanis in the sentence ‘*****ghatam anaya*****’ (fetch the pot).**

**In truth the Sphota-sabda is partless and has no sequence whatsoever.**

**Since Sphota-sabda is eternal, the qualities of time won’t / cannot create any distinctions in it. It is manifested by the dhvanis. The qualities of dhvanis relect in Sphota-sabda. Prakrta-dhvanis manifest the Sphota. Since the Sabda is mixed with them it attains the time distinctions, i.e. ekamatra etc.. Since Vaikrta-dhvanis are external their qualities won’t relect in Sphota. Rather, they can cause the existence of Sphota for some time.**

**The Prakrta-dhvani is the cause of the manifestation of Sphota-sabda. Prakrta-dhvani and Sphota-sabda are mixed like milk and water. It is impossible to separate them. Since both are inseparably mixed, the qualities of the Dhvani are relected in the Sphota-sabda. It is due to the Dhvani that the Sphota-sabda appears in the form of** ***ka, ga,*** **etc. The form of Prakrta-dhvani itself looks like the form of Sphota-sabda. It is due to the hypothetical attribution (aropa) that we notice the difference caused by time as ekamatra, dvimatra and trimatra in a Sphota-sabda. That is how the Sphota-sabda differs.**

Patanjali’s Sphota theory only accounts for the usage of the term *dhvani* in the sense of echo, which the Kashmiri rhetoricians stretched to mean “implied sense”.

The poetical function called *vyanjana* (Suggestiveness) was in fact invented by Abhinavagupta: *ekayabhidha-vyaparam karoti dvitlyaya vyanjana-vyaparam* (*Locana* 3.33). Anandavardhana used the term *vyanjakatvam*.

In the Dhvanikara’s methodology, however, Suggestiveness is nothing but the Abhihitanvaya-vadins’ *tatparya* (the Drift) (the meaning of a sentence is more than the sum total of the meanings of the words). The Abhihitanvaya-vadins are Kumarila Bhatta and his followers. Their *tatparya* is the equivalent of Bhartrhari’s Sphota, although the latter has a wider scope. The Dhvanikara only hinted at the origin of the usage of the term *dhvani*: He did not source the origin of what Anandavardhana and Abhinavagupta call Suggestiveness, because he implied that the Abhihitanvaya-vadins’ *tatparya* is a form of Suggestiveness. Anandavardhana followed the Dhvanikara’s methodology on the whole, but in one instance he showed that he was not happy with it: He indicated that the *tatparya* cannot account for an implied sense: *na ca padartha-vakyartha-nyayo vacya-vyangyayoh*, “The methodology (the Drift) between the meanings of the words and the meaning of the sentence does not apply to the methodology between a literal sense and an implied sense” (3.33 vrtti). Abhinava comments: *nanv evam ma bhud vacaka-saktis tathapi tatparya-saktir bhavisyatity asankyaha na ceti*, “He makes this statement suspecting that someone might reply: “Fine, the power of literally expressive words is insuficient to accomplish an implied sense, but the power of the Drift will be suficient”” (*Locana* 3.33)..

In that line of thought, Anandavardhana pointed out karika 1.10 of the Dhvanikara and politely stated that he is wrong in equating Suggestiveness with the Drift. Anandavardhana said that Suggestiveness and the Drift are merely similar in some way. It was Abhinavagupta who clearly established the theory that Suggestiveness is distinct from the *tatparya* and occurs after the *tatparya* has been applied: *asmabhir api prasiddhebhyo sabda- vyaparebhyo bhidha-tatpaya-lakarii-rupebhyo ’tirikto dhvanir ity uktah*, “We say *dhvani* is entirely distinct from the well-known rhetorical functions called *abhidha*, *tatparya*, and *laksana*” (*Locana* 1.13). Abhinavagupta even corrected the Dhvanikara by saying that in an analysis of the derivation of an implied sense, ultimately only Suggestiveness is referred to, not the Drift: *svatas tatparyenety abhidha-vyapara-nirakarana-param idam padam dhvanana-vyaparam aha na tu tatparya-saktim* (*Locana* 2.22). Abhinava seemingly reproached Anandavardhana for the same reason: *vacyeti, tatparyena visranti-dhamataya. prayojanatveveti yavat. prakasanam dyotanam ity arthah* (*Locana* 1.14).

Abhinavagupta explains that the term *sva-samarthya* in Dhvany-aloka 1.11 (cited in the footnote) denotes the Drift: *sva-samarthyam akanksa-yogyata-sannidhayah*, “The term *sva-samarthya* means *akanksa* (mutual requirement of words), *yogyata* (congruity) and *sannidhi* (proximity, i.e. uttered without delay)” (*Locana* 1.11).

Abhinava used the Abhihitanvaya-vadins’ *tatparya* (the Drift) and applied it before using Suggestiveness. According to him, when an implied sense is based on *abhidha*, three functions are involved: *abhidha, tatparya,* and *vyanjana*. And when an implied meaning is based on *laksana*, all four functions are involved, since even then *abhidha* is somewhat taken in consideration insofar as the main meaning is judged incompatible before *laksana* is applied.

In truth, the Dhvanikara’s “Suggestiveness” is the Purport (*tatparya*) based on the Drift (*tatparya*)! It will be shown ahead that Bhatta Nayaka and Dhananjaya agreed with this, according to Dhanika (p. 384). The ‘Purport’ denotes the Anvitabhidhana- vadins’ concept of *tatparya*. It’s the same in English. Bhoja said that what is called the purport in general language is called *dhvani* in poetics: *tatparyam eva vacasi dhvanir eva kavye* (*Srngara-prakasa* 1.5). In essence, the Dhvanikara used Bhartrhari’s Sphofa and changed the term.

According to Nagesa Bhatta, the poetical function called Sugges­tiveness is sourced in Bhartrhari’s methodology in two ways: (1) In grammar, a preposition such as *iva* (like) is called *dyotaka* (suggestive) in the sense that it suggests a similarity (but the Naiyayikas and the Alankarikas call it *vacaka*, expressive), and (2) The Sphota is revealed (*vyangya*) by the phonemes. Bhartrhari mentioned it in his reiteration of the external *sphota* theory (Patanjali’s Sphota), where the *sphota* is the revealed (*vyangya*) and the *prakrta-dhvanis* are the revealer (*vyanjaka*): *grahana- grahyayoh siddha yogyata niyata yatha* \| *vyangya-vyanjaka- bhavena tathaiva sphota-nadayoh* \|\| (*Vakya-padiya* 1.97). But there is no Suggestiveness per se: Nagesa Bhatta clarifies that in grammar *vyangya* stands for *abhivyangya* (manifested): *atredam bodhyam, eka eva sphotas tat-tad-varnais tad-tad-rupenabhivyajyate* (*Mahabhasya-pradipoddyota* 1.1.70).

The poetical concept of suggestion is vaguely sourced in Bhartr- hari’s system: A word ‘suggests’ its meaning, so to speak. Dr. Subrahmania Iyer notes:

**The two,** ***sabda*** **and** ***artha*****, stand in relation to each other, they face each other, and it is then that they are said to be** ***vacaka*** **and** ***vacya*****, the expressive word and the expressed meaning. They are also said to be cause (*****karana*****) and effect (*****karya*****). That is why Bhartrhari has declared that the relation in which they stand towards each other can be looked upon as that of illuminator and illuminated (*****prakasaka-prakasyatvam*****) and as that of cause and effect (*****karya-karana-rupata*****).**

For example: *apoddhara-padarthah ye ye carthah sthita-laksanah\|* *anvakhyeyas ca ye sabda ye capi pratipadakah* \|\| *karya-karana- bhavenayogya-bhavena ca sthitah* \| (*Vakya-padiya* 1.24-25). This is like the aforesaid dynamic of *vyangya* and *vyanjaka*. However, the grammarians did not acknowledge “suggestiveness” per se (although Bhartrhari did in some way: p. 304). Dr. Kumar De writes:

**It is well known that the philosophers refuse to recognise the** ***vyanjana*** **as a Vyapara sui generis, and even after it was put forward by the Alankarikas, they would explain it by another recognised function as** ***anyatha-siddha*****; it is not surprising, therefore, that orthodox grammarians or philosophers should entirely ignore it.**

Dr. Keith explains the Dhvani theory by connecting it with Sphota:

**The theory inds its origin in the analysis of language and meaning. The phrase, a herdsman’s station on the Ganges, is obviously as it stands absurd ; the denotation (*****abhidha*****) gives no sense, and we are obliged to ind a transferred sense (*****laksana*****) which gives us the sense of a station on the bank of the Ganges. This shows the incompatibility of the literal sense as one factor, and the possibility of giving an allied meaning as another. But this is not all: there is brought to us by such a phrase deliberately used in poetry a sense of the holy calm of such a station situated on the sacred stream with all its associations of piety. This, it is contended, is not given by implication, but by the power of suggestion which is derived from the poet’s purpose (*****prayojana*****) in applying the phrase. This doctrine of suggestion which the grammarians did not accept could be based on a philosophical opinion of the grammarians themselves. They recognized the Sphota, a mysterious entity, a sort of hypostatization of sound, of which action sounds were manifestations, and the same idea of the revelation of something inherent (*****vyanjana*****) is found in the Vedanta, where all is a manifestation of the underlying reality, the Brahman or absolute.**

Dr. Sushil Kumar De adds:

**This brings us to the consideration of the views of those who accept the concept of** ***vyangya*** **artha but dispense with the necessity of such a separate and unauthorized Vrtti as** ***vyanjana*****, which is regarded as included in other functions of sound and sense. Some Mimamsakas hold, for instance, that the so-called suggested sense is conveyed by the** ***abhidha-vrtti*** **or the denotative power of a word. In this connexion, the** ***dtrgha-vyapara-vadins*** **are said to have maintained that as a single arrow, discharged by a strong man, destroys by a single movement, called velocity, the armour of the enemy, pierces through his body and takes away his life, so a single word, used by a good poet, brings before us by a single power, called abhidha, the sense of the word, teaches us its logical connexion (*****anvaya*****) and makes us apprehend the suggested sense.**

Ironically, the grammatical term *jnapaka* is the same as the poetical theorists’ term *vyanjaka* (suggestive word or phrase). Not only that, on the one hand Anandavardhana upholds the notion that poetical suggestiveness is sourced in the grammatical system, but on the other, poetical rhetoricians rebuke dry philosophers such as grammarians and Mimamsakas for being unable to relish an implied sense in the scope of a *rasa*, especially implied meanings in the scope of *bhakti*. Dr. Kumar De remarks:

**The unexpressed meaning is bound up by means of deinite links with the expressed, without which it cannot exist; but it is wrapped up in such a manner as to make it possible only for the initiated in the poetic hieroglyphics to comprehend it in its subtlety. The unexpressed is not understood by those who know grammar and lexicon, but only by men of good taste and literary instinct who know the essence of poetry. It is the province of the** ***sahrdaya*****, the connoisseur, who is expert in discerning through the intricate meshes of veiled word and sense into the aesthetic relish of deeper signiicance, in which the pleasure of the beautiful is mixed up with the pleasure arising from the ineness of the problem itself. \[Sometimes, analyzing a verse is a challenge.\]**

The Kashmiris never mentioned that Suggestiveness is sourced in Bhartrhari’s Sphota theory, for two reasons:

1. Bhartrhari says the phonemes are unreal, in the sense that only the meaning of the sentence counts, whereas the Kashmiri Shaivites say the phonemes (Sanskrit phonemes) are real;
2. The same basic principles occur in the respective philosophies: Bhartrhari’s world view conlicts with Kashmir Shaivism: Bhartrhari says the world is different from Brahma whereas the Kashmiri Shaivites say the world is real.

The Kashmiris gave their respects to the Grammarians in name only: They ignored Bhartrhari, one of the chief grammarians. Whenever Abhinava quoted Bhartrhari, the citations were only about Bhartrhari mentioning a Sphota theory of another scholar; and elsewhere in the book, when Abhinava mentioned the grammarians, he implicitly referred to Bhartrhari and considered him an adversary. This cover-up reached the peak of ludicrousness: Ananda, Abhinava and Mammata exalted Patanjali’s Sphota theory to the point of saying that in poetics, even the literal meaning can be called *dhvan*i. The Dhvanikara never allowed such a usage.

He established that the word dhvani means “implied sense”. Dr. Sushil Kumar De writes:

**The word dhvani itself, as Anandavardhana pointed out, is sometimes used by the grammarians for the word or letters which reveal the** ***sphota*****. Mammata’s remarks in this connexion are pertinent. In his Vrtti on the deinition given by him of** ***dhvani*** **(i.4), he says that the** ***dhvani*** **is, according to the grammarians, that word which reveals the all-important** ***sphota*****, inasmuch as through it arises the knowledge of the word’s meaning. Others, by whom he signiies the writers on the** ***dhvani*** **theory in Poetics, carry this doctrine of the grammarians a step further and apply the term** ***dhvani*** **to the meaning, as well as to the word which is capable of suggesting a meaning superseding the one which is directly expressed. Intrinsically the two theories have scarcely any mutual connexion; but what the Alankarikas really wanted was an authority for their assumption of the power of** ***vyanjana*****, which the great grammarians did not acknowledge.**

Kavi Kamapura indicated that the poetical function called Suggestiveness is validated by ‘the’ Sphota theory. Combining the linguistic concepts of Patanjali’s Sphota with those of Bhartrhari’s, Kavi Kamapura stated that this is what Mammata had in mind when he referred to “the Grammarians”.

In expounding the Sphota theory, Kavi Kamapura incidentally criticized Pratyabhijnana: *pada-janya-pratitir janyate, sahakari- dardhyena pratyabhijnana-vad eva. pratyabhijnane pratyakse ’pi atitadi-purvavastha sphuraty eva, tena krtam sphoteneti, tan na. tatha hi pada-vyutpadana-samayo hi sphota-krta eva,* “\[Someone might argue:\] ““In a recognition (knowledge which is a mixture of direct experience and remembrance), the previous situation, even an old one, comes to mind even when there is the direct perception, so what is the use of a Sphota theory?” That is wrong. The explanation is this: The time when it is known that “This meaning of the word is taking place” is made by *sphota*.” (*Alankara-kaustubha* 2.5).

Kavi Kamapura did not see through the Kashmiris’ cover-up, but his opinion that the concept of *vyanjana* is sourced in the Sphota theory hits the bull’s-eye. The similarity between Suggestiveness and Bhartrhari’s Sphota theory is that each involves a lash of insight. It is high time to do justice to Bhartrhari.

## Bhartrhari’s theory of sphota

Bhartrhari lived around 500 CE. His theory of Sphota treats of linguistics and philosophy. In linguistics, the Kashmiri Shaivites adopted the standpoint of Kumarila Bhatta, but in philosophy they developed their own system: Pratyabhijna, or Recognition. It involves this sequence: *vismrti* (forgetfulness), *pratyaksa* (direct perception), and *smrti* (remembrance): In a lash of insight, the soul remembers its nature, and regains knowledge of itself. The term *abhijnana* in Kalidasa’s *Abhijnana-sakuntalam* refers to a similar process: Sakuntala went to meet Dusyanta, but he did not recognize her. Much later, all of a sudden Dusyanta remembered that he had married her. The term *abhijnana* denotes the transition from forgetfulness to remembrance. The preix prati emphasizes the notion of “return”.

Bhartrhari’s philosophy is a form of sonic theology and is based on this aphorism by Vyasa: *sabda iti cen natah prabhavat pratyaksanumanabhyam*, “If someone says this contradicts the validity of Vedic word, the reply is: No, because the universe originates from it, and this is proven by direct perception and by inference” (*Vedanta-sutra* 1.3.28). This *sutra* does not mean that *sabda-brahma* is the ingredient cause of creation. Rather, the purport is that meanings and other kinds of ideas did not originate with this creation, since they are archetypes that have always existed. For instance, what is said of Indra in the Vedas is said in reference to the category (*jati*) of Indra and does not necessarily denote the Indra of this universe, because if it only referred to the Indra of this universe, Vedic words could not be said to be eternal. Consequently Vyasa concludes: ata eva ca nityatvam, “For this reason also the Vedas are eternal” (*Vedanta-sutra* 1.3.29).

Jiva Gosvami rejects the Sphota theory: ata eva sphota-rupatvad vedasya nityatvam \[...\] vedantinas tu varna eva tu sabda iti, “\[The followers of Bhartrhari say:\] “Therefore the Vedas are eternal because they take the form of *sphota*.” The proponents of Vedanta, however, follow this principle: “The phonemes themselves are the sounds”” (*Sarva-samvadim* 11 of *Tattva-sandarbha*). In other words, the Sphota-vadins presume that what is eternal in the Vedas is not the individual words but the overall meaning of a sentence. This eternal meaning, they say, is recollected every time the Vedas are recited. The Mimamsakas and the Vedantists, however, accept that not only the meanings but also the phonemes and the words of the Vedas eternally exist. Nonetheless, Bhartrhari’s viewpoint is substantiated by looking at the variant readings of the same hymn, *Purusa-sukta*, in each Veda although the meaning is the same: *mukham kim asya kau bahu ka urupada ucyete* (*Rg-Veda* 10.90.11); *mukham kim asyaslt kim bahu kim uru pada ucyete* (*Sukla Yajur- Veda* 31.10); *kav uru padav ucyete* (*Taittinya Aranyaka* 3.12.13); *mukham kim asya kim bahu kim uru pada ucyete* (*Atharva-Veda* 19.6.5).

Yaska stated Audumbarayana’s opinion that words only exist in the mind and senses: *indriya-nityam vacanam audumbarayanah*. (*Nirukta* 1.1)

Kavi Kamapura accepts the Sphota theory and adds that the phonemes are eternal (*Alankara-kaustubha* 2.1-5). However, if it were said that the phonemes are necessary to convey a meaning, there would be no scope for the concept of understanding a meaning by means of telepathy, not to mention the concept of intuition. The language of the heart is universal:

**Poetry is the universal language which the heart holds with nature and itself. He who has a contempt for poetry cannot have much respect for himself, or for anything else. (William Hazlitt)**

Patanjali stated that the perception of a meaning can occur by intui­tion, not necessarily by means of properly pronounced phonemes:

*sabdartha-pratyayanam itaretaradhyasat sankaras tat- \|*  
*pravibhaga-samyamat sarva-bhuta-ruta-jnanam \|\|*

**“A word, its meaning, and a person’s perception of the meaning are intermixed because they are superimposed on each other. By yogic concentration on their differentiation, a person obtains the knowledge of \[the meaning of\] the sounds of any being.” (*****Yoga­sutra*** **3.17)**

Sankaracarya did not accept Bhartrhari’s Sphota theory: *sphota- vadinas tu drsta-hanir adrsta-kalpana ca, varnas ceme kramena grhyamanah sphotam vyanjayanti sa sphoto ’rtham vyanaktiti gariyasi kalpana syat*, “Those who adhere to the Sphota theory reject something obvious (words directly express their meanings) and imagine something imperceptible (*sphota*). Moreover, that doctrine assumes too much: “These phonemes, heard in sequence, activate the *sphota*, and then that *sphota* reveals the meaning”” (*Sarlraka-bhasya* 1.3.28). Similarly, Bhamaha rejected the Sphota theory: *sapathair api cadeyam vaco na sphota-vadinam*, “The words of the Sphota-vadis cannot be accepted even if they were to swear the Sphota theory is true” (*Bhamahalankara* 6.12).

Bhartrhari says Sphota is both the revealer and the revealed, a cause and an effect: *prakasaka-prakasyatvam karya-karana-rupata, antar-matratmanas tasya sabda-tattvasya sarvada* (*Vakya-padiya* 2.32). In like manner, Nagesa Bhatta derives the term *sphota* as: *sphutaty artho ’smad iti sphotah*, *vacaka iti yavat. evam ca varna- sphotadi-padanam varnadayo vacaka ity arthah*, “*Sphota* is that from which a meaning bursts forth. *Sphota* amounts to being a *vacaka* (an expressive thing, i.e. by means of phonemes) (Kumarila Bhatta disagrees with that). This means the phonemes of the words of a varna-sphota and so on are vacakas” (*Sphotavada* 2). Visvanatha Kaviraja stated: *vaiyakarana hi prathame vidvamsah, tan-mulatvac chabda-vyavaharasya. pradhanatvam ca sphotasyartha-pratyayana-rupa-phala-pratyasatya sphutaty asmad artha iti sphotah*, “Only the grammarians are the primary scholars, since grammar is the root cause of the usage of words. The fact that *sphota* is all-important is because it approximates the result which is the suggestion of a meaning. *Sphota* is so called because a meaning bursts forth from it” (*Kavya-prakasa-darpana* 1.4). The verbal root is sphut *visarane* (to burst, be manifest).

Nagesa Bhatta enumerates the types of Sphota: (1) *varna-sphota*, (2) *pada-sphota*, (3) *vakya-sphota*, (4) *akhanda-pada-sphota*, (5) *akhanda-vakya-sphota*, (6) *varna-jati-sphota*, (7) *pada-jati- sphota*, and (8) *vakya-jati-sphota* (*Sphotavada* 1). Nagesa Bhatta says a *vakya-sphota* is the most important *sphota* because the meanings of words culminate in a sentence.

In *Vakya-padiya*, Bhartrhari stated various interpretations of Sphota-vada, but his own Sphota theory is the *akhanda-vakya- sphota* (the meaning of the sentence is one and is more than the sum of its parts). Dr. Subrahmanyam explains:

**When the earlier dhvanis are emanated, then that was phonemes and words which are felt and not the Akhanda- vakya-sphota-sabda. Rather, the Sphota-sabda itself is felt not in its real form but in the unreal form of phonemes and words. It was through the last dhvani that the form of phonemes and words is gone and the real Vakya-sphota is manifested. \[...\] The Vaiyakaranas believe that the phonemes and words are artiicial. In fact they do not exist. Sentence is the real unit. The same is Sphota-sabda.**

**The theory of Naiyayikas that a group of phonemes is a word and that of words is a sentence, cannot be substantiated. Therefore, phonemes are genuine and words and sentences are nothing but illusion. \[.\] Therefore, neither the theory of Mimamsakas that only phonemes are real, nor that of Naiyayikas that words only are real can be taken for granted.**

Dr. Subrahmanyam adds:

**All the Vaiyakaranas have accepted that there is an entity called Sphota and it’s the origin of all Sabdas, and therefore they became famous as Sphota-vadins. \[...\] The roots of Sphota-theory were already there in ancient times, rather Bhartrhari can be considered to be the irst to elaborate, propagate and accentuate the theory. The famous Mimamsaka, Kumarila- bhatta and the Naiyayika of similar fame, Vacaspati-misra have vehemently refuted the concept. In his work ‘Sphota- siddhi’, Mandana Misra strongly advocated the theory of Sphota.**

Kumarila Bhatta disagreed with Sphota-vada. Still, Mandana Misra, who defended Sphota-vada, was a disciple of Kumarila Bhatta. Sankaracarya defeated Mandana Misra in debate, though the legend does not say whether Sphota-vada was a topic.

Sabara Svami (fourth century) invented *tatparya-vrtti* (the Drift) in principle. Bhartrhari’s Sphota, called *akhanda-vakya-sphota*, is practically the same. It seems that Sabara Svami endorsed Sphofa-vada: *purva-varna-janita-samskara-sahito ’ntyo varnah pratyayaka ity adosah* (*Sabara-bhasya* 1.1.1). The Drift became the essential aspect of Kumarila Bhatta’s Abhihitanvaya-vada: *Abhidha-vrtti* can only be used one time: It ceases to function after giving the meaning of each word; then *tatparya-vrtti* is needed to make the syntactical connection of the words, which amounts to the meaning of the sentence.

While discussing the different viewpoints in analyzing a sentence, Bhartrhari mentioned the views of the two Mimamsa schools that later became known as Prabhakara’s Anvitabhidhana-vada (*abhidha-vrtti* has the power to effect the meaning of a sentence) and Kumarila Bhatta’s Abhihitanvaya-vada.

Purva-mimamsa and Vyakarana have much in common: Both stress the importance of faultless words. Kumarila Bhatta orchestrated the decline of Buddhism by propounding the notion that Buddhist scriptures cannot be logically correct because they contain grammatical inconsistencies. On the other hand, the Sanskrit in the Vedas is irregular. Moreover, the Grammarians and the Mimamsakas (*athato dharma-jijndsa, Purva-mimamsa-sutra* 1.1.1) agree that life should be based on *dharma* (moral ethics) and that *dharma* is expounded in the Vedas: *dharmo jnanasya hetus cet tasyamnayo nibandhanam,* “If dharma is the cause of knowledge, then the Vedas are the root cause of understanding *dharma*” (*Vakya- padiya* 1.135). The Grammarians say the practice of *dharma* is conducive to liberation:

**The purpose of Vyakarana is claimed to be two-fold: Artha-jnana (cognition of meaning) and Dharma (a virtue useful in attaining Moksa—salvation). The latter is, in truth, of two types—Pravrtti and Nivrtti. The former is useful in attaining comforts in this world whereas the latter is for Moksa. Two kinds of relations and two types of purposes are proposed in verse 25.**

In addition, Bhartrhari expounded igurative usage. He even acknowledged the concept of implied meaning: *ya pravrtti-nivrtty- artha stuti-ninda-prakalpana, kusalah pratipatta tam ayathartham pratiyate*, “A clever person understands that praise and admonition as incentives to action and inaction respectively are unreal praise and unreal admonition” (*Vakya-padiya* 2.318).

In that regard, Bhartrhari said that sometimes the Vedas make false statements to prevent people from committing bad deeds, like a mother might tell her baby to stop crying or else a tiger will devour the baby. Punyaraja comments that Vedic statements which tell people that they will go to hell if they commit a prohibited act fall in that category, consequently such statements should not be taken at face value.

Thus Bhartrhari nowhere stated that his Sphota only involves the literal sense of the words. According to him, sometimes the sense of a sentence is not obtained from its form: Logical reasoning— especially the line of reasoning in Purva-mimamsa—facilitates the understanding of the real sense. Bhartrhari even says that the power of words is nothing but that logical reasoning: *sabdanam eva sa saktis tarko yah purusasrayah, sa sabdanugato nyayo ’nagamesv anibandhanah*, “Human reasoning is the power of words. That logical reasoning is in accordance with the scriptures. The reasoning of those who are not in line with the scriptures is invalid” (*Vakya-padiya* 1.138).

This is an example of igurative usage in the Vedas:

***Rg-Veda*** **4.58.3, which begins: “It has four horns, three feet, two heads,” is taken to be a series of metaphors: by the four horns are intended (*****abhipraya*****) the four priests, by the three feet the three pressings of** ***soma*****, by the two heads the patron of the sacrifice and his wife. It is, Sabara adds, “like praising a river by saying that a pair of water birds are its two breasts, a line of snow geese its brilliant white teeth, the silvery rushes its garment, and the dark seaweed its lowing hair.”** ***See Sabara-bhasya*** **on** ***Mimamsa-sutra*** **1.2.46. The distinction between** ***sruti*** **and** ***laksana*** **is drawn by Sabara in his comment on** ***Mimamsa-sutra*** **6.2.20. Kumarila’s analysis of metaphor and metonymy is found on** ***Tantra-varttika*** **on** ***Mimamsa-sutra*** **1.4.22.**

The whole verse reads:

*catvari srnga trayo asya pada dve sirse sapta hastaso ’sya \|*  
*tridha baddho vrsabho roraviti maho devo martya a vivesa \|\|*

**“He has four horns, three feet, two heads, and seven hands, and is bound in three ways. A bull (the best of men) intensely recites. The great Lord enters humans that way.” (Rg-Veda 4.58.3)**

The first half of the verse is rendered in *Bhagavatam* 8.16.31 and the second in *Bhagavatam* 11.12.17. Commenting on *Bhagavatam* 8.16.31, Sndhara Svami cites the verse, ignores Sabara Svami’s above interpretation and quotes Yaska’s explanation. Yaska writes: “The four horns are the four Vedas. The three feet are the three parts of the day. The two heads are the introductory portion and the concluding portion of a sacriice. The seven hands are the seven meters. He is bound in three ways, by the mantra portion of the Vedas, by the *Brahmana* portion, and by the *kalpa* portion (the rules for a sacrifice). “A bull intensely recites” means men invoke Him with hymns of the *Rg-Veda*, perform sacriice to Him by means of hymns of the *Yajur-Veda*, and praise Him with hymns of the *Sama-Veda*” (*Nirukta* 13.7).

In the form of transcendental sound, the Lord enters the body of a reciter of the Vedas (*Bhagavatam* 11.12.17-18). This refers to the four phases of Vedic sound: Para, Pasyanti, Madhyama, and Vaikhan (*Rg-Veda* 1.164.45). The above text from *Rg-Veda* involves igurative usage, and it occurs twice. These two separate Sphotas of the text constitute a *khanda-vakya-sphota*. The same occurs in verses of *Purusa-sukta* and so on. Therefore Bhartrhari’s theory that Sphota is one and undivided does not hold here.

In addition, Patanjali’s interpretation of the above verse constitutes a third application of figurative usage. According to him, the four horns represent both the four phases of speech and these four: noun, verb, preix, and preposition. The three feet are the three phases of time: past, present and future. The two heads are the two forms of Sabda (*sphota* and audible phonemes). The seven hands are the seven types of case-endings. He is bound in three ways: on the chest (the origin of vowels), in the throat (where the consonants are pronounced) and at the head (where the *anusvara* is made; or else in the nose).

Over and above that, Patanjali cites the verse to illustrate that one purpose of studying Sanskrit is that a similarity between the Lord and us may take place: *mahata devena nah samyam yatha syat iti adhyeyam vyakaranam* (*Mahabhasya*, preliminary). Nagesa Bhatta says this is actually the Purport (the implied sense); in other words, he says, the verse signiies that God makes a pure reciter of the Vedas non-different from Him.

Another example of Suggestiveness in the Vedas is in: *sarvam khalv idam brahma*, “Indeed, the entire universe is Brahma” (*Chandogya Upanisad* 3.14.1). This is igurative usage, by the logic that duality does not exist: *dvaitasyavastunah* (*Bhagavatam* 11.28.4). The real meaning is: “Brahma is the cause of everything.” That indirect meaning occasions an implied sense: The universe is pervaded, in potential, by transcendental potency: *etat sarvam idam visvam jagad etac caracaram* \| *para-brahma-svarupasya visnoh sakti- samanvitam* \|\| *visnu-saktih para prokta ksetra-jhakhya tathapara* \| *avidya-karma-samjhanya trtlya saktir isyate* \|\| (*Visnu Purana* 6.7.60-61). In that sense the world is transcendentally real (in part): This is the true meaning of Parinama-vada (Brahma / Vishnu transforms as the world). Vyasa states: *atma-krteh parinamat*, “\[Vishnu is both the ingredient cause and the instrumental cause of the world,\] because of a modiication, on account of making Himself (the world is His body)” (*Vedanta-sutra* 1.4.26). Pradhana, a material potency of Vishnu, is the ingredient cause (*upadana- karanam*). Baladeva Vidyabhusana states that Vishnu, in the form of transcendental potency, is the instrumental cause (*nimitta- karanam*).

Time has a dual nature: It is a transcendental potency and a potency of Maya (*Bhagavatam* 3.26.15-16): *tam evakalam dvedha laksayati* (*Bhavartha-dipika* 3.26.16). But since transcendental potency is not always present, the world is fundamentally unreal because the three *gunas* are unreal in the sense that they are not transcendental.

Similarly, Bhartrhari says his Brahma is both different from and not different from its potencies: *ekam eva yad amnatam bhinna- sakti-vyapasrayat* \| *aprthaktve ’pi saktibhyah prthaktveneva vartate* \|\| (*Vakya-padiya* 1.2). This is the same as Gaudiya Vaishnava philosophy, called *acintya-bhedabheda-tattva* (paradox; the principle of inconceivable, simultaneous difference and non­difference) (the universe is different and not different from God, a soul is different and not different from God, and God’s attributes are not different from Him). The philosophy of Paradox is substan­tiated as follows. This is a verse of *catuh-sloki*:

*yatha mahanti bhutani bhutesuccavacesv anu \|*  
*pravistany apravistani tatha tesu na tesv aham \|\|*

**“Just as afterward the ive elements both entered in large things and in little things and did not enter them, so I am in them and I am not in them” (*****Bhagavatam*** **2.9.35).**

At irst the outer coverings of the universe were created, therefore the subsequent entrance of the ive elements inside the universe is not really an entrance because they were already present, by taking the coverings of the universe to be part of the universe. That verse has its counterpart in *Bhagavad-gita*:

*maya tatam idam sarvam jagad avyakta-murtina \|*  
*mat-sthani sarva-bhutani na caham tesv avasthitah \|\|*  
*na ca mat-sthani bhutani pasya me yogam aisvaram \|*

**“I pervade all this world by My unmanifest form (Brahma). All things and all beings are in Me, but I am not in them, nor are all of them in Me. Behold My godly might.” (*****Bhagavad-gita*** **9.4-5)**

The theory of paradox is also substantiated in the very irst line of *Bhagavata Purana: janmady asya yato ’nvayad itaratas carthesu*, “\[We meditate on Him\] from whom the creation, the continuation and the destruction of the universe take place because of His connection (*anvayat*) in things (*arthesu*) and (*ca*) because He is altogether distinct (*itaratah*) from them” (*Bhagavatam* 1.1.1).

In the fourth verse of *catuh-sloki* in *Bhagavatam*, Krishna sum­marizes His teachings:

*etavad eva jijnasyam tattva-jijnasunatmanah*  
*anvaya-vyatirekabhyam yat syat sarvatra sarvada*

**“The inquiring by one who seeks to know the truth about the Soul should be done to this extent that it occurs in regard to everything at all times both in terms of connection and in the opposite way.” (*****Bhagavatam*** **2.9.36)**

In terms of connection (*anvaya*), the Absolute is in the world in the form of transcendental energy, or the Absolute communicates by symbols and by implied meanings, along the model expounded in *Purusa-sukta*. And by the negatory process, the opposite of a connection, nothing in the world is the Absolute: *neti neti* (not this, not that) (*Brhad-aranyaka Upanisad* 2.3.6). The concept of negation is exactly what is meant here. For instance: ***nisedha****- seso jayatad asesah*, “Glory to Asesa, who is the remainder of the negation” (*Bhagavatam* 8.3.24).

Sridhara Svami says “in terms of connection” means God is present in the effects, and “in the opposite way” means He, the cause, is completely distinct from the effects: *anvayah karyesu karanatvenanuvrttih. karanavasthayam ca tebhyo vyatirekah* (*Bhavartha-dipika* 2.9.36). This philosophy does not only relate to the Absolute: In everyday life, for instance, we examine a situation by assessing the pros and the cons, the qualities and the faults.

The philosophy of Paradox amounts to what I call the Uniied Theory of Vedanta: The two theories, Parinama-vada (the world is a transformation of God: the world contains transcendental energy) and Vivarta-vada (the world is unreal: the world is distinct from God and consists of material energy), are valid. This dual approach is substantiated in Bhartrhari’s philosophy, according to Gaurinatha Sastn (pp. 330-331 ahead). Practically speaking, the paradox is this: There is a God. There is no God. Both propositions are true. Although Brahma is a form of God, Brahma has no bearing on the world per se. Moreover, those who only say there is a God have not lived much, and those who only say there is no God have not lived much. As the British Empire sent India’s precious stones back to England and hence became rich, let the intellectual gems of India enrich the minds of scholars all over the world.

Thus, Bhartrhari’s Sphota-vada is a primitive yet complete theory, for it includes all the literary functions of the poetical theorists: Denotation (*abhidha*), Indication (*laksana*), the Drift (the Abhihitanvaya-vadins’ *tatparya*), the Purport (Mammata’s *tatparya*, which is the well-known *tatparya* of the Mimamsakas), and Suggestiveness (*vyanjana*). For this reason Abhinavagupta says Sphota-vada is not a valid methodology of interpretation: According to him, a cause-and-effect relation between literary functions should be established, based on specific rules, like in Purva-mimamsa philosophy these six are sequentially less important in determining the sense of a sentence: *sruti* (a statement from a sruti scripture), *linga* (the conventional sense supersedes the etymological meaning), *vakya* (the agreement in case ending), *prakarana* (the actual context), *sthana* (proximity to another context), and *samakhya* (the etymological meaning). Still, Bhartrhari mentioned such factors that narrow down the possible literal meanings down to one (VP 2.315-316). Mammata and many other poetical theorists cited him in their respective sections on *abhidha-mula-vyanjana* (Denotation-based Suggestiveness).

Bhartrhari says the meaning of a sentence is understood by means of a lash of insight (*pratibha*). This lash joins the meanings of the words, once those meanings have been severally understood. Bhartrhari said the lash is obtained either: (1) by means of one’s nature, (2) by good conduct, (3) by practice, (4) by contemplation, (5) by the result of performing good deeds, or (6) by being bestowed by a special person.

In conformity with Anandavardhana, Mammata used Bhartrhari’s concept of “a lash of insight” to explain how the readers perceive an implied sense, such as a *rasa*. Commenting on this, Baladeva Vidyabhusana says “the lash of insight” refers to the speciic predisposition (e.g. only a grown-up can understand about *srngara- rasa*): *pratibha vasana-visesah* (*Sahitya-kaumudi* 3.2). Therefore *sphota* is of the nature of a lash of insight. Suggestiveness is *dhvany-aloka* (the light for an implied sense). In other contexts, the term *pratibha* means creative intelligence. Thus, Rajasekhara explained the two meanings of the term *pratibha*: *karayitri* (creative intelligence) and *bhavayitri* (the cause of appreciation).

Ultimately, Bhartrhari’s Sphota is Sabda in the highest sense (Sabda-Brahma). The scriptures say there are two Brahmas: *dve brahmani veditavye sabda-brahma param ca yat, sabda-brahmani nisnatah param brahmadhigacchati*, “Two Brahmas are to be known: Sabda-Brahma and Para-Brahma. A person learned in Sabda-Brahma attains Para-Brahma” (*Visnu Purana* 6.5.64).

Bhartrhari, however, made no difference between the two: *anadi- nidhanam brahma sabda-tattvam yad aksaram, vivartate ’rtha- bhavena prakriya jagato yatah*, “Brahma, the Imperishable, has neither beginning nor end and is the Word Principle. The ways of the world revolve by the existence of meanings from that Brahma” (*Vakya-padiya* 1.1).

In this verse, the verb *vivartate* (it revolves, i.e. it manifests as an illusion) was the precursor of the term Vivarta-vada, a doctrine propounded by Sankaracarya. Bhartrhari comments on his opening verse: *tathedam amrtam brahma nirvikaram avidyaya kalusatvam ivapannam bheda-rupam vivartate* (*Vakya-padiya* 1.1 *vrtti*). Nonetheless, Bhartrhari’s concept of vivarta refers to Sabda- Brahman: The world is a modiication of the Word (*omkara*):

**For the irst time in the Vakya-padiya of Bhartrhari the theory of Sphota and modiication of word (*****sabda-vivarta-vada*****) has been established and discussed in detail. Really speaking, the theory of** ***sphota*** **is a unique achievement of Indian grammarians and as such it forms India’s outstanding contribution to the philosophy of language.**

Later in his introduction, Bhartrhari says the Veda, which branches out as various Vedas according to the tradition, is a resemblance of that Brahma and is the means to achieve it: *prapty-upayo ’nukaras ca tasya vedo maharsibhih* \| *eko ’py aneka-vartmeva samamnatah prthak prthak* \|\| (1.5).

The Sphota theory is sourced in this Vedic verse:

*catvari vak parimita padani*  
*tani vidur brahmana ye manisinah \|*  
*guha trini nihita nengayanti*  
*turiyam vaco manusya vadanti \|\|*

**“Speech is divided in four stages. Wise Brahmanas know them. The irst three stages appear only within the body. They do not occur externally. The fourth stage of speech is the sounds spoken by humans.” (*****Rg-veda*** **1.164.45)**

The *Bhagavatam* refers to Bhartrhari’s Sphota: *disam tvam avakaso si disah kham* ***sphota*** *asrayah, nado varnas tvam om-kara akrtmam prthak-krtih* (10.85.9). Commenting on this, Sndhara Svami mentions the four phases of speech, equating Sphota with Para Vak: *disam upadhikrtakasa-pradesanam avakaso disas ca tvam. kham samanyakasas tad-asrayah* ***sphotas*** *ca sabda- tan-matram* ***paravastha vag*** *ity arthah. nadah* ***pasyanti****. omkaro* ***madhyama****. varnas cakrtmam padarthanam prthak krtih prthak karanam abhidhanam yasmat tat padam. varna-padady-atmika* ***vaikhari*** *ca tvam ity arthah.* (*Bhavartha-dipika* 10.85.9).

The *Bhagavatam* equates sphota with *sabda-brahma* (*srnoti ya imam sphotam*, 12.6.40), according to Kavi Karnapura, or, according to Sridhara Svami, with *omkara*: *ko ’sau paramatma tam aha, srnotiti. imam sphotam avyaktam om-karam* (*Bhavartha- dipika* 12.6.40). Elsewhere, however, Sndhara Svami says *sphota* means sound in the form of Para Vak; *nada* denotes Pasyanti; and *omkara* denotes Madhyama: *kham samanyakasas tad-asrayah sphotas ca sabda-tanmatram paravastha vag ity arthah. nadah pasyanti. omkaro madhyama* (*Bhavartha-dipika* 10.85.9). Still, even in this context *sphota* denotes *sabda-brahma*, an extremely subtle form of sound.

Dr. Sukla writes:

**Nagesa believes in four types of vak** ***viz*****. Para, Pasyanti, Madhyama and Vaikhari and shows a vast difference between Para and Pasyanti. In this way Nagesa identiies Para with Sabda-brahma. The process of creation (*****srsti-prakara*****) described by Nagesa is completely explained in the treatises of Tantra, and according to them this Para or Sabda-brahma is the product of bindu which itself is the product of Maya and Purusa created by Bhagavat.**

Dr. Srinivas Murti expounds:

**The doctrine of the Eternity of Sabda (the Word) has a most fascinating and ancient history having its roots in the well-known Rg-vedic hymns to the Goddess of Speech— Vagdevi. The traditional formulator of this theory according to grammarians is Sphotayana Rsi, as referred to at the end of the present publication. As developed by grammarians like Bhartrhari, the theory of Sphota links up the transient phenomenon of the pronounced word to Brahman as Sabda- Brahman, the eternal Noumenon of every form of signiicant sound or word and indeed of every manifested object of creation. This is a view which strikingly brings up to our mind the conception of Platonic Logos (the Word) and the saying of the Christian scripture “In the beginning was the Word (Logos); the Word was with God; and the Word was God.” \[...\]**

**Sanskrit grammar is not merely a Science of Linguistics or an Art of correct speaking and writing. It is far more than mere Science or Art. It is also a Philosophy with its own Metaphysics. It is even more than this; for it is also a** ***moksa- sastra*****—a religion pointing a way to** ***Moksa*** **(Liberation) through the discipline or** ***upasana*** **of Sabda-brahman (Word­Brahman) conformably to the text “he who is well-versed in Word-Brahman (Sabda-Brahman) attains to the Supreme Brahman:**

*sabda-brahmani nisnatah param brahmadhigacchati \|*

**\[...\] The transition of the eternal Sphota or Sabda-Brahman from the highest level to the form of the spoken or audible word or sound at the lowest level is described as occurring in four stages technically known as Para, Pasyanti, Madhyama and Vaikhan. Para and Pasyanti are too subtle to be comprehended by our sense-organs except by the yogins. The Madhyama stage may be comprehended through practices like** ***japa*** **while the Vaikhari is what is uttered by the vocal organs and is capable of being heard by ordinary folk. Para is the Eternal Noumenon—Sabda-Brahman originating from Kundalini in Muladhara-cakra. This is also referred to as Nada. Pasyanti originates from Svadhisthana-cakra in the navel region. Both Para and Pasyanti are classed as Suksma (subtle) Sphota. Madhyama originates from Anahata-cakra in the region of the Heart where the voice of the silence is heard. It is also referred to as Antara (internal) Sphota. Vaikhan originates in Visuddha-cakra in the region of the throat and is manifested as Bahya (external) Sphota through utterance by the vocal organs and is capable of being heard by others. It is because of this nexus that we can, if we will, place ourselves in tune with the ininite Sphota (Sabda-Brahman), through the Vaikhari sound or Bahya-Sphota if we practice the necessary discipline.**

Bhartrhari raised grammar to the level of a philosophical system: *idam adyam pada-sthanam siddhi-sopana-parvanam, iyam sa moksamananam ajihma raja-paddhatih*, “Grammar is the irst step on the ladder to perfection, and is the straight royal path for those who talk about liberation” (1.16); *asannam brahmanas tasya tapasam uttamam tapah, prathamam chandasam angam ahur vyakaranam budhah*, “Grammar is the topmost austerity of the ascetics” (1.11).Dr. Subrahmanyam remarks:

**4. He elucidated the concept of Patanjali that through the employment of Sadhu-Sabdas one would know the real form of Veda and thus get on track for attaining Dharma (this point is criticised by Somananda in Siva-drsti). \[...\]**

**8. He construed the Vaiyakarana-siddhantas along with Advaita-vedanta and Veda-pramanikatva (authority of Vedas). He brought Sphota on a par with Apurva of Mimamsakas, Isvara of Naiyayikas, Pilu of Kanadas, Sunya of Bauddhas and Brahman of Vedantins.**

Bhartrhari speciically talks about the last three phases of speech: *vaikharya madhyamayas ca pasyantyas caitad adbhutam, aneka-tirtha-bhedayas trayya vacahparampadam* (*Vakya-padiya* 1.143). But according to Dr. Subrahmanyam, Bhartrhari discusses Para Vak in verses 17 and 18 and in verses 112 to 115 of *brahmakanda* (the irst chapter of *Vakya-padiya*). For example:

*atratita-viparyasah kevalam anupasyati \|*  
*chandasyas chandasam yonim atma chandomayim tanum \|\|*

**“The soul who has mastered grammar and is it for the Vedas cons­tantly perceives the unique source of the Vedas, a source which is a form of the nature of the Vedas.” (*****Vakya-padiya*** **1.17)**

Bhartrhari’s Sphota theory is the ideal explanation of how the Maharishis cognized the Vedas in their trance:

**While all this is true of us ordinary mortals, the great sages are credited with the power of cognizing the indivisible** ***sphota*** **directly, without going through the process of errors. Not only do they cognize it themselves, they also impart it to others. That is what Yaska has stated in his** ***Nirukta*** **I.20.**

**The irst Rsis realised** ***dharma*****, that is, they acquired it. It is implied that they acquired the Mantras also, without being taught by anybody. Their successors were of inferior calibre; and could neither realise the** ***dharma*** **nor acquire the Mantras by themselves. To them, the irst Rsis imparted the Mantras by establishing the relation of teacher and pupil. This second batch of Rsis thus got the Mantras by listening to the words of their teachers. They are, therefore,** ***Srutarsayah*** **(Rsis through instruction). \[...\] That the** ***Vrtti*** **was inluenced by the** ***Nirukta*** **in the interpretation of** ***Vak*****. I.5 and 137 is shown by the fact that, in the explanation of the former stanza, the relevant passage from the** ***Nirukta*** **is actually quoted, and that in the explanation of the later stanza there is a reference to three kinds of Rsis. What the** ***Vrtti*** **says about the three kinds of Rsis is not identical with what the** ***Nirukta*** **says about them.**

Bhartrhari’s theory of Sphota perfectly applies only to the Vedas because Bhartrhari’s Sphota is nitya (eternal), i.e. a word has an eternal set of given meanings—according to tradition, this idea was irst formulated by Katyayana—and the Vedas convey archetypical meanings, not to mention that the words of the Vedas must be properly recited to achieve the desired effect, whereas in real life a *sphota* (lash of insight) does not always involve a meaning of an eternal nature.

Thus, Bhartrhari wanted to raise grammar to a philosophical system (*darsana*), and for that purpose he discussed the *pramanas* (means of obtaining valid knowledge):

**In a regular darsanika work, there would be discussions about valid and invalid knowledge,** ***prama*** **and** ***bhrama*****. But the** ***Vakya-padiya*** **is not a** ***darsanika*** **work. After all, it is the work of a grammarian; and although it does set forth a** ***darsana*** **as the view relating to the nature of the ultimate Reality and its manifestation just referred to, it shows that Bhartrhari is chiely concerned with establishing the authority of Grammar. It can be authority only if it gives valid knowledge about something; and it is in this connection that Bhartrhari is led to make some observations on valid knowledge, and how it arises.**

Moreover, Bhartrhari says Sanskrit is a divine language set in its ways, but he admits that over time foreign words became Sanskritized. Bhartrhari extended his theory of Sphota to language in general, and this is where his theory begins to collapse, for these reasons:

- Outside of the Vedas, there is no need to consider Pasyanti Vak, much less Para Vak;
- Poets invent words and derive some others in various ways, and so there is not always an eternal connection between a word and its meaning;
- In the scope of the Vedas, a Sphota concerns an archetypical meaning, whereas in poetry new ideas are invented all the time, thus a Sphota in poetry is not always *nitya*. Besides, even a wrong idea can be called sphota. So why bother calling a meaning Sphota, which is said to be *sabda-brahma* by nature? However, there is no doubt that Sarasvati, the goddess of language, can become manifest in the form of a special meaning in anyone’s words whenever she wants to.

## Philosophy inluences poetic theory

The Dhvanikara’s acceptance of Kumarila Bhatta’s *tatparya*, which implies the rejection of Bhartrhari’s Sphota theory, is an important, although neglected, event in the history of Kashmir Shaivism. The irst documented evidence of the Kashmiris’ rejection of Bhartrhari’s theory is in the second chapter of Somananda’s *Siva-drsti* (c. 860 CE). Utpala, Abhinavagupta and other Kashmiris rejected Bhartrhari’s Sphota-vada. Abhinavagupta (c. 950-1020 CE) was Somananda’s great-grand-disciple.

**The Kashmiri Shaivite Tradition**

The reason the Kashmiri Shaivites rejected Bhartrhari’s Sphota theory is that according to Bhartrhari, the phonemes of a word are unreal. The Kashmiri Shaivites cannot accept this because it implies that the world is unreal, whereas in their view the world is real, since it is a manifestation of Siva.

Bhartrhari’s Sphota is on the topic of *vakya* (sentence): The *vakya- sphota* is the meaning-bearer. Kumarila’s *tatparya* provided an ideal alternative to Bhartrhari’s theory because that *tatparya* is the literary function which accomplishes the overall sense of a sentence and because the Mimamsakas say the phonemes are real. The latter point is important for Kashmiris. For instance, Abhinavagupta states that the phonemes are manifestations of Siva. Dr. Raffaele Torella explains:

**But there is something that keeps Abhinavagupta away from a whole-hearted acceptance of the whole of Bhartrhari’s conception: irst of all, Bhartrhari’s dismissal of the phonemes and his considering them as pure abstractions. This must have seemed unacceptable to Abhinavagupta, in his position as a tantric master. He cannot ignore that practically any tantra belonging to the Saiva tradition deals with the phonemes, assigning them a central place both in ritual and speculation. What he says in the Isvara-pratyabhijna-vivrti-vimarsim, precisely addressing the Vaiyakaranas, sounds like a proud vindication: ‘For us, the “totality of sounds” is the supreme Lord himself, the goddess Matrka \[or the alphabet in the usual sequence\] - both distinct and not distinct \[from Him\] - is His Power, the eight alphabetic classes are the eight Powers of the Rudras, the fifty phonemes are the ifty Powers of the Rudras’. If Abhinavagupta intends to give space to the role of phonemes, this cannot but take place at the expense of the** ***sphota*** **doctrine, which in fact he totally ignores. Will this result in going closer to the Mimamsa? Why not? After all, both the Mimamsakas and the Saivas feel as their primary duty the defence and exegesis of the Sruti, as embodied in the Vedas for the former, in the Tantras for the latter.**

All this enthusiasm of the Kashmiri poets for Kumarila Bhatta’s *tatparya* came to an abrupt halt when Mammata stopped inviting the Abhihitanvaya-vadins to the poetical party. He not only discarded the Drift, he indirectly pointed out that in one sense Kumarila Bhatta’s tatparya is the same as Bhartrhari’s Vakya-sphota: *akanksa-yogyata-sannidhi-vasad vaksyamana-svarupanam padarthanam samanvaye tatparyartho visesa-vapur apadartho ’pi vakyarthah samullasatlty abhihitanvaya-vadinam matam. vacya eva vakyartha ity anvitabhidhana-vadinah*, “The opinion of the Abhihitanvaya-vadTs is this: Although it is not the expressed meaning of the words, the *tatparya artha* (the Drift), whose form is a particularity, is the sense of the sentence and is apparent in the syntactical connection (the prose order) of the meanings of the words by the force of *akanksa* (mutual requirement of words), *yogyata* (congruity), and *sannidhi* (mutual proximity of words, i.e. uttered without delay) (*tatparya artha* = *vakya artha* = *apadartha*). The Anvitabhidhana-vadis, however, say the expressed meanings of the words is the sense of the sentence (*vacya artha* = *vakya artha*)” (*Kavya-prakasa* 2.6 *vrtti*).

Mammata’s statement that Kumarila Bhatta’s *tatparya* is *apadartha* (not the meaning of the words taken at face value), that is, *avacya* (not literal), implicitly validates the grammarians’ opinion that the irst perception of the meanings of the words of a sentence is unreal. For instance, in the sentence *gaur anubandhyah,* the phonemes of the word *gauh* do not reveal the actual meaning of the word. We must look at the case ending of its adjective to understand that here the word *gauh* is masculine, and so it means ox, not cow. Similarly, the individual perception of the phonemes of a word is unreal until they form a coherent whole: *satyam yat tatra sa jatir asatya vyaktayah smrtah* (*Vakya-padiya* 3.1.32). Bhartrhari stated that just as a word cannot only be understood as the sum total of individual phonemes (since the meaning of a word is understood from a dictionary, and so on), a sentence cannot be understood only by the meanings of the words in the sentence. The words must be linked by means of some function. Bhartrhari says the initial illusoriness of the meaning of a word in a sentence signiies that the phonemes are unreal, but Kumarila and Abhinava argue that the phonemes—the Sanskrit phonemes—are real nonetheless. Bhartrhari’s philosophy as such is not restricted to the Sanskrit language nor to Prakrit languages.

Abhinavagupta, however, says the meaning of a sentence obtained by the Drift is *vacya* (expressed): *sabdavagataih padarthais tatparyenayo’rtha utthapyate sa eva vakyarthah, sa eva ca vacya iti* (*Locana* 3.33). The Drift was ignored by Mammata, Visvanatha and Kavi Kamapura, although they mentioned it in passing. Mammata mentioned the Drift and wrote kesucit (according to some persons)—*tatparyartho ’pi kesucit* (*Kavya-prakasa* 2.6)— implying that he himself did not acknowledge it. Dr. Kane notes:

**But it does not appear that Mammata entirely approved of this view. Otherwise he would not have said “*****kesucit*****”, but would have simply declared that** ***tatparya*** **is one of the functions. Our author also appears to hold views similar to Mammata.**

Mammata propounded his own explanation of the *tatparya*, but he never explicitly applied it to poetics. He even contradicted himself on the topic. Thus, throughout his magnum opus Mammata accepted the Anvitabhidhana-vadins’ methodology of using the term *vacya* (literally expressed) to denote either the meaning of a sentence or the meaning of a single word. Regarding the former, commenting on his rule *saksat* ***sanketitam*** *yo’rtham abhidhatte sa vacakah* (*Kavya-pradtpa* 2.7), he indicated that there is no need of the *tatparya* to explain the sentence *gam anaya* (bring a cow), which is a classic example in Anvitabhidhana-vada. But in the ifth chapter, Mammata sought to prove the validity ofthe function called Suggestiveness by demolishing Anvitabhidhana-vada, specifically by saying that the meaning of the sentence *gam anaya* is *avacya* (not literally expressed), hence *tatparya*, much less *vyanjana*, is not in the scope of the theory called Dirgha-vyapara-vada (any nondirect meaning can be explained as an extension of Denotation). Govinda Thakkura clariies that according to Mammata, the meaning of the sentence *gam anaya* is *avacya* because that meaning is not sourced in a dictionary and the like: *anvitabhidhana- naye tu padartha-samanyanvitah svarthah sanketa-visayah. gavadi-visesanvitanayanadi-rupas tv* ***asanketita*** *evety avacya eva vakyarthah* (*Kavya-pradpa* 5.47). Nagesa Bhatta concurs: *go- karmakanayanadi-viseso ’vacyah* (*Uddyota* 5.47). But it could be argued that *gam anaya* is a set phrase, as in “to give punishment,” and so the meaning is literal, like a *rudha* word (a igurative word that has become literally expressive due to the profuseness of the usage).

For his part, to validate *vyanjana*, Visvanatha ignored Mammata and reiterated Abhinavagupta’s explanation that a rhetorical function (esp. a*bhidha* and *laksana*) can only be used one time: *viramya vyaparah sabda-karma-buddhy-adnam padarthavidbhir nisiddhah* (*Locana* 1.4). The Dhvanikara used the *tatparya* twice.

Mammata’s *tatparya* is the real sense of the speaker’s words. In essence, in poetics Mammata’s *tatparya* is arrived at by rewording the sentence (the statement means to express more than what it says) and is exactly the same as the *paryayokta* ornament (circumlocution). And if Mammata’s *tatparya* is suggestive, it is classed as the suggestive factor called *vakya-vaisistya* (the suggestive specialty of the real statement) (*Kavya-prakasa* 3.21). Mammata’s *tatparya* was wrongly ignored by other poetical theorists—although Visvanatha accepted *vakya-vaisistya* (*Sahitya- darpana* 2.16)—because it explains the concept of *paryayokta*.

Another reason the Kashmiri Shaivites rejected Bhartrhari’s system is that it is based on only three phases of speech (Pasyanti, Madhyama and Vaikhari), whereas the Kashmiri Shaivites propound the quadripartition: Para Vac occurs before Pasyanti. Not only that, the Kashmiris say Bhartrhari is wrong in equating Sabda-Brahma with Pasyanti (or even with Para Vac). Dr. Raffaele Torella expounds:We can detect three main targets in Somananda’s criticism:

**1.   the identiication of** ***para-brahman*** **with** ***sabda-rupa-pasyanti-paravac*****,**

**2.   the claim of** ***vyakarana*** **to go beyond the narrow domain of grammar proper and constitute a world-view, and**

**3.   as a world-view, its preaching the unreality of manifested universe, just as within the field of linguistic speculation it upholds the basic unreality of sounds with respect to the** ***sphota*** **they are supposed to reveal.**

**It is apparent that we are in front of two competing world views.**

Thus, in the preliminary of the second chapter of *Siva-drsti*, Utpala, the commentator, says Somananda aims at exalting Isvaradvaya- vada (non-difference between the world and God) over the Grammarians’ Sabda-Parabrahmadvaya-vada (non-difference between Sabda-brahma and Para-brahma): *isvaradvaya-vada eva yuktiyukto, natu sabda-parabrahmadvaya-vada iti vaktum, vaiyakaranopeta-sabdadvaitam tavan nirakartum upakramamana aha* (*Siva-drsti* 2.1 vrtti).

In *Manjusa* and in *Sphota-vada*, Nagesa Bhatta added Para Vak to Bhartrhari’s Sphota theory. Other works of note in the ield are *Sphota-tattva-nirupana* by Sesa Krsna, *Sabda-kaustubha* by Bhattoji Diksita, and *Vaiyakarana-bhusana* by the latter’s nephew Kaunda Bhatta: “So far as the treatment of Sphota theory, Samasa- sakti, Dhatv-artha, Tin-artha, Ladesa, etc. is concerned, Nagesa has almost followed the line of Koundabhatta.”

On behalf of Grammarians, Nagesa Bhatta says the poetical theorists’ function called Suggestiveness must be acknowledged. But that does not invalidate Sphota-vada in any way.

Bhartrhari is not at fault for expounding the tripartition, because it is mentioned in the scriptures:

**In fact, things are more complex than they seem. In the Brahmanic tradition, which Bhartrhari refers to, there are traces of both a quadripartite system (cf. the famous passage from the** ***Rgveda catvari vakparimita padani*****... and** ***catvari srnga*****..., already quoted by the** ***Nirukta*** **(*****Parisista*****, XIII.7 and 9 respectively), and a tripartite one (cf.** ***tisro vacas*****. in the** ***Rgveda*****;*****ghosim*****,** ***jata-nirghosa*****,** ***nirghosa/aghosa*** **in the** ***Mahabharata*****).**

Gaurinath Sastn remarks:

**It is, however, surprising that though Punyaraja seems to differ from Bhartrhari in regarding Pasyanti as a manifestation of the Eternal Verbum and not as the Eternal Verbum itself, he does not make any comment either by way of elucidation or dissent on the scheme of manifestation of the Eternal Verbum as conceived by Bhartrhari. This warrants us in drawing the conclusion that there arose a difference of opinion in the school of the grammarian in later years;**

Dr. Subrahmnia Iyer adds:

**It is quite possible that Bhartrhari may have intended to convey some correspondence of the three Kandas of the** ***Vakya-padiya*****, dealing respectively with the Sabdatattva, Vakya, and Pada, to the three stages through which Vak passes, namely,** ***pasyanti, madhyama*** **and** ***vaikhari*****. Helaraja deinitely associates the third Kanda with the Vaikhari or** ***trtiya vag-avastha*****.**

Perhaps this explains why Dhvany-aloka consists of four chapters.

Further, Somananda says Bhartrhari is wrong because unreal words cannot possibly give rise to Sphota, which is real: *sphotasyasatya-rupair hi padadyair vyangyata katham* \| *pasyantyah satya-rupaya asatyair vyangyata na ca* \|\| (*Siva-drsti* 2.60). The debate regarding the reality of the phonemes is like the argument over whether a coin is made of the heads side or the tails side. It is made of both. During a recitation of Vedic sound by a proper speaker, the sound gradually becomes infused with transcendental potency. For instance, the Prana phase of Vedic sound, also called Para Vac, is imbued with material Prana and transcendental Prana, according to Visvanatha Cakravarti: *prakrtaprakrta-prana-mayasya*. In a proper recitation of the Vedas, the phonemes gradually become imbued with transcendental energy (*Bhagavatam* 11.12.18).

Nagesa Bhatta does not use the term “unreal”. He simply quotes Bhartrhari:

*pade na varna vidyante varnesv avayava na ca \|*  
*vakyatpadanam atyantam pravibhago na kascana \|\|*

**“Just as the letters themselves do not give the meaning of the word and just as the parts of a phoneme taken separately do not constitute the phoneme, so there is no use in analyzing the words separately from the sentence.” (*****Vakya-padiya*** **1.74)**

Kumarila Bhatta expresses the same basic idea: “The phonemes and the words are only means in achieving the sense of the sentence, like the lames of pieces of wood only serve to give heat.”Moreover, Somananda, Utpala, Helaraja—Abhinavagupta’s teacher of *Vakya-padiya*—and others wrongly pointed out characteristic tenets of Advaita-vedanta in Bhartrhari’s philosophy. Dr. Gaurinath Sastn explains:

**The third theory, which regards time as a divinity, differs from the second only in afiliating the time-force to the Absolute Brahman instead of the individual subject. \[...\]**  
**According to the commentator the third theory is the theory of Bhartrhari. \[...\]**

**We have seen that in the transcendental plane the Eternal Verbum and Kala-sakti should be viewed as an uncompromising unity, that is, as one and the same principle, which differentiates itself into two aspects on the eve of creation. We have also seen that besides this Kala-sakti which is described as the Eternal Power of the Eternal Verbum, there lie in that plane innumerable other Powers, all belonging to the Eternal Verbum but mutally exclusive, and yet each of them identical with the Eternal Verbum in the same way as Kala-sakti is. It has been shown that the plurality of the Powers does not affect or contradict the unitary character of the Sternal Verbum in spite of the fact that they are held to be eternal and identical in essence with the Eternal Verbum. It should be carefully noted that at the pre-cosmic stage the Powers remain indistinguishable from the Eternal Verbum. \[...\] It will be our endeavour to study what exactly is meant by the grammarian when he asserts that the Eternal Verbum is the material cause of the phenomenal world. Does the grammarian mean that the Eternal Verbum transforms itself into the phenomenal world or dees he suggest that the Eternal Verbum is the changeless background to the stupendous panorama of the phenomenal world?**

**The doctrines of real change (parinama) and appearance (vivarta) may be studied together. According to the former a cause undergoes a change and we have an effect. An effect, therefore, may be looked upon as subsisting in a subtler form in its cause. According to the latter, however, a cause does not undergo any change and yet we seem to cognize its effect. \[.\] It is contended by the exponents of the doctrine of parinama that like curd coming out of milk the world comes out of the Ultimate Principle and is as much real as the material cause of it. The most celebrated exponents of the doctrine ofparinama are the Sankhyaites according to whom the material cause of the world is Primordial Matter (Prakrti). It may be noted as well that the Yoga school of Patanjali, the Pasupatas, the Madhvas, the school of Nimbarka and the Vaisnava school of Bengal, all accept the view that Prakrti is transformed into the phenomenal world and thus subscribe to the doctrine of parinama. Similarly, Bhatta Bhaskara,the Pancaratra sects and others who declare that the Brahman changes into the material world, believe in the doctrime of parinama. Ramanuja, Snkantha, Bhaskara Raya and others who opine that the Brahman as determined by Prakrti is the material cause of the world, are also adherents to the doctrine of parinama.**

**The most outstanding exponent of the doctrine of vivarta is Sankara, according to whom the stupendous appearance of the phenomenal world is founded on the reality of the Brahman. \[...\]**

**According to the Trika writers of Kashmir the universe is neither a parinama nor a vivarta of the Supreme Reality. In their opinion Consciousness (Caitanya) is the only reality and is all-embracing and all that we experience lies within it. Thus what is popularly called external is held to be a relexion on Consciousness as on a mirror. The universe is an image within Consciousness. The manifestation of the universe is due to the unrestricted and spontaneous freedom of Consciousness and is a process of relexion (abhasa). \[.\]**

**Patanjali, the author of the Vyakarana-mahabhasya, is of opinion that word is nothing but internal consciousness. He suggests that internal consciousness exists in the form of subtle speech and when it becomes manifest, it takes the form of word. Needless to say, Bhartrhari upholds the theory of Patanjali and seeks to explain its underlying implications.**

Dr. Mithilesh Caturvedi observes: “Kaiyata says in his commentary on the *Mahabhasya* that he could cross the ocean of the *Bhasya* only with the help of the bridge constructed by Bhartrhari. Vitthala places him at the top of those who could reveal the secret of the *Mahabhasya*.” Caitanya Mahaprabhu’s explanation that all verbal roots are energies of Lord Krishna (*Caitanya-bhagavata, madhya-khanda* 1) is sourced in *Vakya-padiya*. Dr. Subrahmania Iyer remarks:

**The main ideas of Bhartrhari regarding Brahman, the ultimate Reality, are given in the irst four Karikas of Kanda I. There are many other Karikas where the main ideas are supplemented or elaborated. In fact, one can say that right through the** ***Vakya- padiya*****, Bhartrhari has this ultimate reality at the back of his mind, even when he is dealing with what appear to be purely linguistic or grammatical topics. It runs like a thread right through his work and gives it a kind of unity. When he is speaking about** ***jati*** **or** ***dravya*****,** ***sadhana*** **or** ***kriya*****,** ***dik*** **or** ***kala*****, he somehow connects it all with Brahman.**

Gaurinath Sastn continues:

**Let us now turn to the doctrines of change (*****parinama*****) and appearance (*****vivarta*****) to see how far the theory on which the grammarian bases his philosophy is in agreement with either of them. It is worthy of notice that Bhattoji-diksita in his** ***Sabda-kaustubha*** **writes that Bhartrhari, while dealing with the nature of word, has incidentally discussed the illusory creation of the objective world and kindred topics, and that this may very well be likened to the inding of a precious gem in course of a quest after an insigniicant cowrie. \[...\]**

**Evidently, therefore, the philosophical system of the grammarian, in the opinion of Madhavacarya \[Vidyaranya\], is based on the doctrine of** ***vivarta*****. \[...\] On the other hand, there is evidence to prove that as early as the time of Vacaspati, Jayanta-bhatta and Santaraksita there were at least two different schools of the philosophy of** **Sabda-brahman, one explaining it in the light of the doctrine of real change and another basing it on the doctrine of illusory appearance. Thus we ind Vacaspati observing in the** ***Nyaya-kanika*** **that the philosophy of Sabda- brahman** **has been interpreted by exponents of the two schools of** ***parinama*** **and** ***vivarta*** **in their own ways. Jayanta-bhatta represents the grammarian’s philosophy as based on the doctrine of** ***vivarta*** **but is cautious enough to point out that there might be schools of thought which would interpret it from the viewpoint of the doctrine of** ***parinama*****. Santaraksita again informs us through his** ***Tattva-samgraha*** **that a twofold interpretation of the philosophy of Sabda-brahman may be attempted. \[...\]**

**It appears to us, therefore, that Bhartrhari was not familiar with the difference in the connotations of the two terms which is so usual in later philosophical literature. That the term** ***vivarta*** **was used to convey an import which is expressed by the term** ***parinama*** **can be gathered even from the writings of Sankara himself who has used the term** ***vivarta*** **to explain the Sankhya view of the evolution of the universe. We therefore, conjecture that the term** ***vivarta*** **came to gather its peculiar sense of formal transformation or apparent self alienation in the writings of teachers of the Monistic Vedanta who came after Bhartrhari, and it is, therefore, nothing unusual that Bhartrhari’s position is sought to be explained in the light of either of the two doctrines of** ***parinama*** **and** ***vivarta*****. With Bhartrhari there is no question of any marked distinction in the connotations of the terms** ***parinama*** **and** ***vivarta*****. \[...\]**

**Both Punyaraja and Helaraja appear to explain the view of Bhartrhari from the standpoint of Absolute Monism as advocated by Sankara. Punyaraja identiies the** ***Kala-sakti*** **of the grammarian with the** ***avidya*** **in the system of Sankara \[...\]. Helaraja, however, has the courage of conviction to describe in unambiguous language the view of Bhartrhari in the light of Sankara’s Absolute Monism. \[...\] Though Bhartrhari has not a word to suggest that he recognizes** ***avidya*** **as a power of the Eternal Verbum, Helaraja speaks not only avidya but also of** ***avidya-sakti*** **\[...\].**

**Somananda-natha in his** ***Sivadrsti*** **has surveyed the doctrine of Verbal Monism of the grammarian and criticized it in order to establish his own theory which, though a form of Monistic Idealism, yet accords to the phenomenal world a status of reality. According to him the grammarian regards** ***Pasyanti*** **as the Ultimate Reality in his system of philosophy beyond which there is nothing that enjoys a status superior to or higher than it. The grammarian describes** ***Pasyanti*** **as the transcendental consciousness in which the distinction between word and import, the knower and the knowable, vanishes and the diversity of objective realities does not appear. Somananda-natha, however, does not agree with the grammarian in thinking that this is the highest transcendental state which should be looked upon as the Ultimate Reality. He points out that** ***Pasyanti*** **is regarded as the immediate prius of** ***madhyama*** **in which temporal order and sequence (*****krama*****) manifests itself. In accordance with the law of homogeneity of cause and effect** ***Pasyanti*** **also should be supposed to be endowed with the latent power of embodying sequence, otherwise the sequential order in** ***madhyama*** **would be unaccountable. This obviously negates the claim of** ***Pasyanti*** **to be ranked as the Ultimate Reality, which is** ***ex hypothesi*****, not compatible with the slightest trace of sequence —spatial or temporal. \[...\]** ***Pasyanti*** **owes its name to the fact that it surveys (√** ***drs*****) the entire universe in all its phases. \[...\]**

**Somananda-natha deinitely says that in the opinion of the grammarian the transformations of** ***Pasyanti*** **into the objects of the world are all illusory and not real transformations.**

**It is, therefore, interesting to note that a celebrated teacher of Saivagama like Somananda-natha, who is held in high esteem by Abhinavagupta and others and whose authority is beyond question, does not interpret the doctrine of Bhartrhari in the light of the teachings of his own school. According to Utpaladeva, the author of the gloss on the Sivadrsti and worthy disciple of Somananda-natha, Bhartrhari is a champion of Verbal Monism according to which the universe is an illusory appearance. He represents Bhartrhari as upholding the view that the world-process proceeds as a result of the operation of** ***avidya*****, which is neither real nor unreal (*****anirvacya*****) and hence does not present an opposing reality.**

**Somananda-natha lourished in the latter part of the 9th century A.D. Utpaladeva, who came immediately after Somananda-natha, his teacher, was in all probability well acquainted with the doctrine of** ***vivarta*** **as advocated by Sankara. It is not therefore in the least surprising that Utpaladeva and his teacher, too, would elucidate the grammarian’s philosophy in the manner of Sankara’s idealism which was current at that time. \[...\]**

**But we should not fail to point out that however much the later stalwarts might have tried to read Bhartrhari’s philosophical outlook in the light of their own favourite doctrines, it is extremely dificult to explain from their points of view some of the fundamental features of the grammarian’s philosophy. Thus if the philosophy of Bhartrhari is interpreted in the light of the doctrine of** ***vivarta*** **as sponsored by Sankara, the status accorded to** ***Kala-sakti*** **or, for the matter of that, to all other Powers (*****Kalas*****) cannot be explained with idelity to Bhartrhari. That the** ***Kala-sakti*** **is ultimately real and has the same ontological status as the Eternal Verbum does not seem to admit of doubt. But Sankara would never accept the ultimate reality of any power (*****sakti*****) co-existing with the Absolute.**

**Let us draw this chapter to a close by venturing a bold suggestion that Bhartrhari’s philosophy may be explained in the light of the doctrine of relexion (*****abhasa*****) as sponsored by the Trika writers of Kashmir. Bhartrhari called the Vedantic monists by the name Trayy-anta- vedins. **Somananda attacks it precisely because this is theoretically too close to the new Saiva** ***darsana*** **that he has set out to build. This could explain the total lack of fair play in his attitude to Bhartrhari, which resembles the iery ightings between insiders rather than the cold and dismissal of a full outsider. By “total lack of fair play” I refer both to the unnecessary sarcastic remarks and the punctilious pointing out of seeming contradictions in terminology, which show a Somananda deliberately unwilling to catch the gist of Bhartrhari’s conception and expound it by doing justice to its boldness and originality. **Generally speaking, we know very little of what happened in the atelier of Indian philosophers, owing to the total lack of personal notes left by them, not to speak of “working” journals. For example, we know nothing of the inner developments which made Sankarananda abandon Saivism and embrace Buddhism (or viceversa), or of the possible intellectual wanderings of a Mandana Misra between Purva and Uttara Mimamsa. Apparently less dramatic but at least equally puzzling was the change of attitude of the** ***Pratyabhijna*** **school towards Bhartrhari, which took place very quickly in the span of only one generation and caused a difference between Somananda and Utpaladeva, the recognized founder of the** ***Pratyabhijna*** **and its systemizer, respectively, closely bound to each other by a direct** ***guru-sisya*** **relationship.**

**Two radically different world-views are at stake here: the depersonalized universe of the Buddhists, made of discrete and discontinuous realities tentatively connected in the ultimately unreal net of** ***vyavahara*** **— unreal insofar as it is the product of a “secondary” conceptual thought —, and the absolutely unitary universe of the Saivas, identiied with and penetrated by a supreme Person, Siva, who runs through it like a golden thread unifying all the seeming multiplicity and dynamically transforming the apparently other into himself and himself into the other. Closely connected with Utpaladeva’s choice of establishing the Buddhists as the main opponents is his adoption of Bhartrhari as the main ally. At this point some questions may be asked: why Bhartrhari? and again: can he really be “used” against the Buddhists?**

**What Utpala needed was a shared, if controversial, strong “philosophical” argument. The omnipervasiveness of language is an epistemological version of the omnipervasiveness of Siva, and at the same time calls for the integration into the spiritually dynamic Saiva universe. \[...\] The supreme Consciousness is the supreme Word, Pasyanti becomes a power of the supreme Lord. In this way, the formerly despised Bhartrhari is raised to the core itself of the Pratyabhijna. Did Utpaladeva accept the whole of Bhartrhari’s doctrine? I should say not. He accepted just what matched his own well-gauged requirements. Other very significant aspects of Bhartrhari’s thought are instead rejected, like the theory of** ***sphota*****. Apparently, Utpaladeva did not propose his own answer to the crucial question “what is the meaning-bearer?”**
