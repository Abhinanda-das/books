# Does Decoherence Solve the Quantum Problem?

## Does Decoherence Solve the Quantum Problem?

JANUARY 27, 2024

Reading Time: 5 minutes

Quantum mechanics creates a problem of Realism because of the assumption (since Greek times) that each thing is just one thing. This axiom breaks in quantum mechanics because each thing is a collection of multiple possibilities. This problem has resisted a solution because it is widely and incorrectly supposed that macroscopic objects are just one thing and the problem of multiple possibilities is unique to subatomic particles. Attempts to solve this problem therefore focus on how multiple possibilities become just one. Decoherence is one such attempt. In this post, I will discuss why the classical physical assumption about reality (that it is just one thing) is false even for macroscopic objects before talking about how Decoherence tries to address this issue although it is not a genuine solution to the problem.

Let’s begin with a macroscopic object to illustrate the problem of multiple possibilities. Suppose that you have a pen in your hand. Most people, most of the time, would call it a pen. In such cases, we can say that the thing in question is just one thing, and the classical realist assumption that one thing is just one thing is true. However, occasionally, we can also use that pen as a toy, paperweight, doorstop, weapon, and so on. Suppose we take into account all these uses. In that case, the classical assumption of Realism fails because whatever we called a pen earlier is in fact a collection of possibilities, with the pen only being more likely than a toy, paperweight, doorstop, weapon, and so on. In fact, whether something is treated as one out of the many possibilities depends on the environment, the user, the specific situation, etc.

Now, we have to say that the thing in question is ideally a pen, but possibly other things. Ideality is not subjective; we can also define it objectively. For instance, the pen will not work as an ideal toy, paperweight, doorstop, or weapon. A gun is a much better weapon; a large and heavy object is a much better paperweight; something that offers much more resistance is a much better doorstop; and a radio-controlled car is a much better toy. The pen, on the other hand, works ideally only as a pen. By putting each thing to the test of what it works as ideally, we can conclude what each thing is. Since this test can be performed by anyone, therefore, ideality is objective. And yet, we cannot determine the ideality without comparing what something does to what other things do. Something is ideally a pen only in contrast to infinite other things. Therefore, even as ideality is an innate property of the thing in question, it cannot be known by observing the pen in isolation.

This requires us to revise Realism in several ways: (a) reality is ideality, (b) ideality is innate, and (c) ideality is known through contrasts. If we don’t know what something is ideally, we can use it in many possible ways. Since this applies to every macroscopic object, therefore, the quantum measurement problem doesn’t disappear for macroscopic objects, unless we choose to use each thing in only one way. Realism holds true in the macroscopic case only because we typically choose to use everything in one way. Since that is a choice that “collapses” many possibilities into one, therefore, the macroscopic reality is also the result of an observer making a choice to collapse possibilities.

This basic property of collapse through an interaction is used in Decoherence. The environment or observer is treated as yet another quantum system with its own possibilities. When two sets of possibilities interact, the possibility common between them becomes more likely, while possibilities not common between them become less likely. The system now reduces to the more likely possibility, which appears to be a collapse. The important thing is that this kind of collapse doesn’t require a non-material consciousness. It just requires another quantum system. This is why many proponents of Decoherence say that they have “solved” the quantum measurement problem.

But before we accept Decoherence as a solution to the quantum problem, let’s try to understand the principle involved through an intuitive example of two humans interacting with each other. Suppose a husband is the possibility of watching a movie or going shopping, while a wife is the possibility of reading a book or going shopping. When the husband-wife possibilities intersect, both of them go shopping because it is the common possibility between them. Through their interaction, possibilities are reduced to actuality. In this reduction, we don’t have to suppose that the husband or the wife is a “consciousness”. We can also say that they are two quantum ensembles that interact with each other, which causes their possibilities to intersect, which reduces them to a common possibility, which becomes the observed actuality.

We can extend this example by varying the likelihood of possibilities. We can say that the husband’s likelihood of going shopping is 0.1 while his likelihood of watching a movie is 0.9. Similarly, we can say that the wife’s likelihood of going shopping is 0.5 and the likelihood of reading a book is 0.5. Due to husband-wife interaction, both go shopping even though the husband on his own would have most likely watched a movie. Thereby, even an unlikely event becomes actuality by a combination of two sets of possibilities. However, if the husband were to spend his time with a male friend with a similar likelihood of possibilities, then both would likely watch a movie, instead of going shopping.

Once we understand the principle behind Decoherence, then we have to recognize that choices are still involved. For example, there is a choice for a husband to be with a wife or a male friend. Once the choice of companion has been made, then the subsequent outcomes are determined by the commonalities between the interacting companions. Thus, Decoherence doesn’t eliminate choice. It only shifts the choice from the activity one wants to do to the companion one wants to be with. Instead of saying that a husband decides what to do, we say that he chooses to be with someone. Their interaction then naturally produces an actuality. The choice is now the selection of a companion.

Decoherence is a general pattern under which circumstances force choices on us. Given a circumstance, our options are so limited that we are compelled to act in a specific manner. We say that we had no choice in the matter. It is true. To make different choices, we have to change our circumstances. For example, if we don’t want the alternatives forced on us by a bad company, then we have to change our company. In a different company, we will automatically make different choices. But that is not the elimination of choice. It is just a different kind of choice. The circumstances that force choices on us are not “given”. The process of changing the circumstances to change the choice can be long and complex, and certainly harder than making a choice within the circumstances, but a choice made with hardship is still a choice.

Whenever there is a choice, there is indeterminism. Decoherence doesn’t solve the indeterminism, although it shifts the indeterminism from a state within a quantum system to the choice of another quantum system. Since the universe is infinite such systems, each quantum system can decohere with infinite other quantum systems, to produce infinite different actualities. For there to be one out of infinite actualities, each system must select the quantum systems it decoheres with. The sequence of mutual interactions cannot be predicted by a mathematical formula. Therefore, Decoherence isn’t a solution to the quantum problem; it is merely the replacement of one problem with another.
