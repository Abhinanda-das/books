# Early Christian Missionaries and the Car Festival

## Early Christian Missionaries and the Car Festival

Rajkishore Mishra

The historic Car Festival at Jagannath-**Puri** in Orissa is an important facet of Hindu civilization in Indian Peninsula since ages. It is important because it annually registers global brotherhood and offers an unprecedented access to the humanity at large to come closer to the divinity. Man and God and God and Man become one in the Ratha Jatra. No distinction in whatever form exists.

India has always been a land of mystery and fables to the peoples living in seas beyond. More so is Purusottama Kshetra or Jagannath **Puri**. The Westerners right from the 14th century looked askance at **Puri** and pondered what type of the deity the people were worshipping over there which could attract thousands of men and women round the year. After the British occupation of Orissa in 1803, sensible British officers took it a matter of policy to exhibit religious tolerance in India and vowed not to interfere with public faith, be it centring round idolatry or otherwise. This was indeed a very pragmatic approach shown by them in their own interest.

Charles Grome, the Collector of Jagannath (**Puri**) in 1805 and George Webb, the Collector of Cuttack in 1807 at the behest of the Governor General in Council made exhaustive probe in respect of the management of the Temple of Lord Jagannath which included various modes of ritual worship, temple revenues, the role of *matha*-s, the inflow of pilgrims to **Puri**, the structure of the prevailing Pilgrim Tax etc in a rational manner. No disrespect was shown by them. The British even honoured the Raja of **Puri** as the Superintendent of the Temple and as the First Servitor of Lord Jagannath by 1809, the practice which was said to have been discontinued since 1760 A.D. during the Marahatta rule.

The missionaries who came in direct contact with Orissa soon after the British occupation were terribly reactive. They even accused the colonial government for extending financial grant to the temple which they construed as an encouragement to idolatry and superstition in India. Their another accusation was the Rath which was encouraging the gullible, illiterate people to get crushed to death beneath its wheels so that the victims would be redeemed, which indeed, was a blatant lie. The following descriptions show the level of organised vilification of such missionaries and travellers from the West right from the 14th century. Missionary Friar Odoric's account in 1321 A.D. was the earliest account of the **Puri** Car Festival which runs as follows :

"Annually on the recurrence of the day when that idol was made, the folk of the country come and take it down, and put it on a fine chariot; and then the king and the queen, and the whole body of the people, join together and draw it forth from the church with loud singing of songs, and all kinds of music and many pilgrims who have come to this feast cast themselves under the chariot, so that its wheels may go over them, saying that they desire to die for their god..." Gladwin reports in 1590 :

"In the town of Pursotem on the banks of the sea stands the temple of Jagnaut, near to which are the images of Kishen, his brother, and their sister, made of sandal-wood, which are said to be 4,000 years old ... The Brahmins ... at certain times carry the image in procession upon a carriage of sixteen wheels, which in the Hindooen language is called Rahth; and they believe that who ever assists in drawing it along obtains remission of all his sins."

William Brutun records in 1632 : Unto this Pagod or house of Sathan ... doe belong 9000 Brammines or Priests, which does dayly offer sacrifice unto their great God Jaggarnat, from which Idoll the city is so called..." He also refers to how people offer themselves a sacrifice to this Idoll. Just after four year one Portugeese missionary named Sebastine Monrique came to **Puri** via Pipli in 1636. It is doubtful whether he had an access to the Temple. He mentions : 'the idol is a stone statue of huge height with one leg broken' and further says that the temple is situated near the river Ganges. About Jagannath's broken leg he cooks up a fantastic story that how Jagannath after enjoying an atrocious meal was hurled from heaven by Christ, the God and as the result of which he broke one of his legs.

Francois Bernier who travelled in the Moghul Empire from 1656-68 also came to **Puri** during the Car Festival in 1671 A.D. Looking at the chariot he exclaimed : a superb wooden machine; a chariot of hellish triumph; the machine is set on fourteen or sixteen wheels ... like those of a gun carriage... I know not how many grotesque figures, nearly resembling our monsters which we see depicted with two heads, being half man and half beast, gigantic and horrible heads, satyrs, apes and devils. Thomas Bowrey, a missionary, was in India for a decade from 1669. He was also critical about the concourse of men and women during the Car Festival of god Jno Gernaet (Jagannath). The Diabolical Chariot is "engraved with the shapes of men and women dancing, as also many hideous shapes of satyrs, bulls, bears, tigers, elephants, rhinoceroses etc..."

Alexander Hamilton who visited **Puri** in 1708 A.D. has furnished a second-hand report on the Car Festival and has mentioned : Jagarynat's effigy is carried abroad in procession, mounted on a coach four storeys high and people fell flat on the ground to have the honour to be crushed to pieces by the coach wheels."

After the British occupation of Orissa, Harcourt visited **Puri** and witnessed the Car Festival in 1805. According to him the festival was witnessed by at least five lakhs of people. He says : "I was astonished at the order and regularity preserved. There was not the least tumult or disposition to violence." But hardly after four years in 1809 A.D. a poem of Robert Southe was published on the Car Festival of Lord Jagannath which is blasfamous :  
The ponderous car rolls on and crushes all Through flesh and bones it ploughs its dreadful path Groans rise unheard, the dying cry and death and agony Are trodden under foot by your mad throng Who follow close and thrust the deadly wheels along."

Rev. Dr. Buchanon (1806) and Rev. James **Peggs** (a Cuttack Missionary in 1826) also vilified Lord Jagannath. Andrew Stirling in his article in Asiatic Researches XV published in 1825 A.D. has doubted the allegation of self-immolation under the chariot wheels. He thought a very few cases that were reported were either accidental or due to long suffering and excruciating pain.

James Fergusson witnessed the Car Festival in 1837 A.D. He published his impressions in his book 'Picturesque Illustration of Ancient Architecture in Hindustan' in 1847 A.D. as follows : "I was most agrreably disappointed to find the pilgrims hurrying to the spot talking and laughing, like people going to a fair in England, which in fact, it is." He saw no victims crushed under the wheels of the Chariot.

William F.B. Laurie, a missionary in his chapter **Puri** and the Temple of Jagannath, in his book, "Orissa, the Garden of Superstition and Idolatry" has jeered at people's faith in Lord Jagannath and the display of their religious sentiments during the Car Festival. His fidelity to Christianity and abhorrence of non-Christian faith prompted him to make the following comments :

"The entire scene of the Rath Jatra savours, to an incredible extent of the ludicrous, the barbarous and the awful. The eager expectation, the unceasing din of a great multitude, the acclamations of "victory to Jagannath !" which rend the car when the images are brought forth in an erect posture ... There you may picture to yourself Christianity shuddering; there, morality weeping ..."

Mr Laurie has depended on Stirling's 'Account of Orissa' and Brij Kishore Ghose's *The History of* ***Puri*** *with an Account of Juggunath* (published in 1848) for acquainting himself with the prevailing tradition in **Puri**, but has delibeately spat out his venom on idolatry and the British Government's policy of religious tolerance and appeasement. To him, the temple of Jagannath which was 'a familiar beacon to the navigators of the Bay of Bengal' was 'the crowning piece of Hindu Superstitions.'

Muslim Travellers, i.e. Mahmud-bin- Amirwali of Balk, Bahar-Ul-Asrar (1626) and Ghulam Husain Salim, the author of 'Riadu's salatin' (1736), unlike their European counter- parts are rather appreciative of Hindu tradition and have not held people's faith in abhorrence.

The fact remains that the Car Festival drew enormous people from far and near to **Puri** even in the 19th century. Rev Lacey, a missionary who attended the Car Festival in 1842 had made an involuntary remark : 'Had there not been a grievous famine affecting the province so as to prevent the Ooreas from attending the pilgrimage, there would have been 400,000 instead of 200,000 pilgrims. Prof. P.Mukherjee in his article, 'Car Festival under the East India Company' has given quite an apt comment :

"The Christian missionaries used to visit **Puri** during the Car Festival to vilify Jagannath and to persuade the pilgrims in distress to embark christianity. Their accounts are naturally biased."

It is worthwhile to make a mention of Rajendra Lal Mitra's reaction in the so-called alien accusation of self-immolation during the Car Festival. Mr. Mitra had no hesitation to quote Madame Henrietta Caraciolo's description of the Car Festival or the 'Feast of Assumption' in Sicily where innocent babes were exposed to suffering and death for the mere purpose of decoration and scenic effect.' Mr. Mitra says, such a charge if tolerated would lead to the stoppage of even the most innocent amusements of the people in every part of the world and suggests the propriety of closing the River Thames, in the bed of which many a person expiates his or her pecuniary difficulties.
