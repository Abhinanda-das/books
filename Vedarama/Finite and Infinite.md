# Finite and Infinite

## Finite and Infinite

We often see debates between personalists and impersonalists regarding the “size” of Brahman. The impersonalist says that Brahman is infinite and therefore it cannot be reduced to a form. Form, according to the impersonalist, especially understood as a person with a body, is finite. Thereby, the personal form of the Lord cannot be equated to Brahman, although Brahman can descend in a finite form. Ultimately, these descended forms of Brahman are for the less intelligent people who cannot grasp the infinite.

The impersonal conception of finite and infinite is based on physical length. By finite, the impersonalist means a measurable length. Similarly, by infinite, the impersonalist means something that cannot be measured or quantified as a number. However, there is another semantic conception of size or length in which finite and infinite are not contradictory. In this post, we will discuss how concepts reconcile finitude with infinitude.

The simplest concept is knowledge. Everyone uses the word quite comfortably. We have an intuitive grasp of what the word means. But ask anyone to explain the meaning of knowledge, and it will be difficult. For example, the word knowledge includes numerous subjects such as logic, epistemology, mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, psychology, linguistics, sociology, economics, geology, hydrology, meteorology, archeology, astrology, cosmology, and so on. The word knowledge has an infinite meaning such that nobody can know all that it includes. And yet, we don’t need to know all the expanded components of knowledge to understand what the word means. People who use and understand the word knowledge don’t know or understand everything included in it. They intuitively know and understand the word knowledge as something finite that is actually infinite.

The same can be said of the word beauty. There is beautiful art, music, poetry, literature, dance, city, technology, body, face, dress, animal, tree, human, sky, planet, society, culture, and infinite other things. The word beauty can be applied to everything denoted by a concept. Therefore, the word beauty denotes something infinite. And yet, everyone uses the word beauty without knowing all that the word includes in itself. In fact, most people who talk about beauty don’t think about all that is included in the word. They understand it as something finite, although if we try to enumerate it, it will be actually infinite.

This problem is not confined to general concepts like beauty and knowledge. It can be seen even in the case of ordinary concepts like red or sweet. Nobody can practically enumerate all the meanings of red because there are infinite shades of red and infinite flavors of sweet. But everyone can use the words red and sweet and communicate what they mean without enumerating all their infinite components. Red and sweet are both finite and infinite.

Mathematicians encounter this problem when they try to form sets such as those of Natural Numbers, Rational Numbers, Irrational Numbers, Real Numbers, and so on. We intuitively understand these words, so we can easily communicate the meaning. But the entire universe is not enough to fully enumerate even one of these sets because the sets are infinite. Therefore, words such as Natural Numbers, Rational Numbers, Irrational Numbers, Real Numbers, and so on, are both finite and infinite. We can understand the meaning easily, so it is finite. But we need infinite time and space to enumerate the components of the set denoted by that word, therefore, the meaning of that word is infinite.

Sāñkhya explains this through the doctrine called Satkāryavāda, which means that the effect resides in the cause eternally. Each concept is a cause and its various instances are its effects. For example, the concept knowledge is a cause and various subjects included in knowledge are its effects. The concept beauty is a cause and various instances of beauty are its effects. The effects reside eternally in the cause, which means that the concepts of knowledge and beauty eternally include the various instances of knowledge and beauty. This eternal existence of the effect in the cause is called avyakta or unmanifest. It makes the concept appear finite although it hides within itself infinite variety. When that infinite variety is expressed, the effects are called vyakta or manifest. Thus, the cause is finite but the effects are infinite. The unmanifest state is finite but the manifest state is infinite.

Mīmāṃsā explains this idea through the doctrine called Arthavāda, which indicates that reality is word and meaning. The meaning denoted by the word is finite such that everyone can understand what the word means. But that understanding of the word is incomplete. Factually, nobody can fully understand any word because the examples of that word are infinite. The example of knowledge and beauty can again be given. Everyone understands what the word knowledge or beauty means. But that understanding is factually deceptive because within these words resides an infinite variety unseen to almost everyone.

Whatever looks finite to us is deceptively so. That is because reality is words. Infinite variety is hidden within these words in an unmanifest state. That variety can be expanded from the words to make the same thing infinite. Expanded variety is compressed into the words to make them seem finite. Therefore, our perception of finitude is false. It is created by the fact that infinite variety is compressed in an unmanifest state within, such that it appears finite to us. That variety can be further compressed to make something significantly smaller. The same variety can be further expanded to make something significantly bigger. Due to compression and expansion of reality, all notions of size are potentially illusory.

Thus, Vedic texts describe the creation of the universe from a seed into a gigantic tree. When that tree collapses into a seed, the process is called dissolution. On this gigantic tree there are fruits which in turn contain the seeds that are capable of expanding into trees and then produce fruits and seeds. Thereby, a seed contains not just the tree, but also infinite fruits and the seeds in them. The whole truth is present within a part of that truth as the seed produced from the fruit, which was produced from the seed. The tree is in the seed and the seed is in the tree. The fruit is within the seed and the seed is within the fruit.

The seed looks small to us but it has infinite generative capacity within. It lies unmanifest in the seed. When that generative capacity is activated, then the seed produces infinite variety. This is not just the nature of spirit. It is also the nature of matter. Matter in Vedic texts is infinitely expandable and compressible. The scientific postulate of the conservation of matter is rejected in the Vedic texts. The ideas of finite and infinite are also rejected. Everything that seems finite to us is actually infinite. We can say that finitude is just an appearance rather than reality. That finite appearance is infinite reality.

When all that seems finite is actually infinite, then mathematics doesn’t apply to reality because how are we going to quantify something that appears finite but can expand infinitely? Quantification itself rests on the premise that what seems finite is actually finite. When that assumption is false, then quantification is false. We can still pragmatically measure things in so far as infinite unmanifest variety may not be fully expanded so quantification can be applied to how much it is currently expanded. Such quantification is like measuring the size of the seed, disregarding the generative capacity within it.

The difference between matter and spirit is that spirit always expands while matter alternately expands and contracts. Since the expansion of matter is terminated and reversed by contraction, therefore, there is a sense in which everything material always appears finite although in reality, it is also infinite. But since the expansion of spirit is never terminated or reversed by contraction, therefore, there is a sense in which everything spiritual always appears both finite and infinite although in reality, it is infinite. Spirit appears finite because it is just like the words knowledge and beauty that appear finite. Spirit appears infinite because it is just like infinitely expandable words.

We can contrast these ideas of spirit and matter with two other ideas—monotheism and impersonalism. Under impersonalism, spirit is infinite and static. Brahman doesn’t expand or contract, in impersonalism. In contrast, under monotheism, spirit is finite and static because it is personified in a finite form. God or the soul don’t expand or contract in monotheism. It is very hard even to say that God is infinite because He is supposed to have a body, which is conceptualized just like our body, and that body seems finite.

These two ideas—i.e., monotheism and impersonalism—are the result of applying materialism (the quantification of matter, which rests on the separation between finite and infinite) to spirit. As already noted, materialism (i.e., quantification) is false even for matter in the Vedic texts. Matter in the Vedic texts is a reversible expansion in contrast to spirit which is ever-expanding. Factually, both spirit and matter are infinite and finite. Everything that appears simple is infinitely complex. Simplicity is just an appearance and complexity is reality. Just like we can understand the words knowledge and beauty although that understanding is deceptive, similarly, simplicity or finiteness is just an appearance.

Thus, when we speak about the Vedic understanding of matter or spirit, we are equally in opposition to materialism, impersonalism, and monotheism. Our opposition is not sectarianism. It is rooted in the claim that everything is word-meaning, and hence, it is simultaneously finite and infinite. The fact that we worship a finite form of God doesn’t mean God is finite. It just means that we understand God like we understand the words knowledge and beauty. Even the stone, wood, or metal comprising the deity is infinitely expandable. But the infinite is also compressible because it is meaning. We can summarize that meaning like we can summarize infinite subjects into the word knowledge.

Therefore, what most people call “God” (in monotheistic religions) is called satyam-param (the supreme truth) and jñānam-advayam (non-dual knowledge) in Vedic texts. He is called satya or truth because He is eternal. He is called satyam-param because He is the eternal truth that originated all other eternal truths and temporary facts. If any confusion is left, then we have His description as non-dual knowledge in which all opposites are reconciled. This knowledge is not a static, fixed, or finite idea without a generative capacity. This knowledge is a person with the generative capacity. In fact, the personhood of knowledge is the generative capacity within knowledge to expand itself infinitely and eternally.

Thus, if we know “God” we know the supreme truth and non-dual knowledge in either a summarized or expanded form. The initial understanding is summarized—like the word knowledge. The progressive understanding is expanded—like the many types of knowledge. There is no limit to expansion. Hence, there is no limit to understanding “God”. It goes on for eternity. Nobody can say that they have fully understood “God”. Even if they see “God” face-to-face, they don’t fully understand Him because He is infinite.

Those who contrast finite with infinite are dualistic, as they rely on the duality of finite and infinite. Those who contrast everywhere with somewhere are dualistic, as they rely on the duality of somewhere and everywhere. Those who contrast one thing with everything are dualistic, as they rely on the duality of one thing and everything. Those who advance the infinite, everywhere, everything idea of the supreme truth are dualists because their choice of words is inherently riddled with some dualism. It is certainly not non-dualism.

Non-dualism cannot be reduced to one side of one dualism—i.e., everywhere, everything, infinite, etc. Non-dualism is both sides of all dualisms—everywhere and somewhere, everything and something, infinite and finite, etc. This non-dualism is consistent with Sāñkhya (Satkāryavāda) and Mīmāṃsā (Arthavāda) which help us understand the nature of non-dualistic reality. All positions that contrast Vedānta non-dualism with that in Sāñkhya or Mīmāṃsā are in fact dualistic positions pretending to be non-dualistic.
