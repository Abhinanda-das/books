# Five Mystical Experiences in Indian Wisdom Today

## Five Mystical Experiences in Indian Wisdom Today

India is the fountainhead of tenets enabling individuals to transcend by means of the intellect rather than intoxicants.

Five magical experiences involving transcendental consciousness are well documented in Indian lore:

1\.   **Samadhi:** Trance in deep meditation

2\.   **Siddhi:** Yogic superpower

3\.   **Krsna-prema:** Trance in love of God

4\.   **Kavya-cid-rasa:** Trance in relishing poetry

5\.   **Metta-bhavana:** Trance in love for all beings.

These mystic states can be reached nowadays too. Each belongs to a separate tradition of Indian wisdom.

The following describes the various aspects of each state in terms of renown, popular beliefs, theoretical knowledge, method of attainment, and sensation.

## 1 Samadhi

*Samadhi* is meditative trance. Its ultimate form is enlightenment. A famous enlightened yogi in the Modern Age was Paramahamsa Yogananda (1893-1952). His bestseller *Autobiography of a Yogi* (1946) was Steve Jobs’ favorite book.

*Samadhi* is the final stage in Patanjali’s eightfold yoga system:

1\.   **Yama:** The five kinds of self-control:  
A. **Ahimsa:** No violence to any life form,  
B. **Satya:** Honesty,  
C. **Asteya:** No stealing,  
D. **Brahmacarya:** Continence,  
E. **Aparigraha:** Minimalist lifestyle.

2\.   **Niyama:** The five virtuous habits:  
A. **Sauca:** Purity of mind, body and speech,  
B. **Santosa**: Contentment,  
C. **Tapas:** Persistence and austerity,  
D. **Svadhyaya:** Study of the self,  
E. **Isvara-pranidhana:** Seeing the Spirit everywhere.

3\.   **Asana:** The two kinds of posture:  
A. Stretching exercises before sitting for meditation,  
B. Meditative pose.

4\.   **Pranayama:** Breathing:  
A. Breathing exercises before meditation,  
B. Regulating the breath during meditation.

5\.   **Pratyahara:** Controlling the mind.

6\.   **Dharana:** Focusing the mind with high concentration.

7\.   **Dhyana:** Deep meditation.

8\.   **Samadhi:** Trance in Brahman.

The Upanishads and other Vedic texts expound the concept of Brahman, the omnipresent field of consciousness, from which all souls originate. An experienced meditator can cognize Brahman.

Patanjali briefly discoursed on yoga in his book *Yoga-sutra* (c. 350 BCE). In modern culture, yoga only refers to *asana* (posture), the third aspect mentioned above, but ultimately the technical meaning of yoga is *samadhi* (trance): *yogas citta-vrtti-nirodhah*, “Yoga is the cessation of mental functions” (*Yoga-sutra* 1.2).

The definition of yoga in *Yoga-sutra* is also found in *Bhagavad-gita: yoga-samjnitam*, “It is called Yoga” (6.23). The *Bhagavad-gita* explains the process of meditation in chapter 6, stating that the objective of meditation is to unite the soul with Brahman, the universal Soul: *yatra caivatmanatmanam pasyann atmani tusyati*, “**In that state, by contemplating on the soul by means of the soul one derives satisfaction in the Soul**” (6.20).

In *Bhagavad-gita*, the word *samadhi* is mentioned only once: *ka bhasa samadhi-sthasya*, **“What are the characteristics of a person who has incorporated the state of trance?”** (2.54).

The *term* samadhi is used one time in *Vedanta-sutra: samadhy-abhavac ca*, **“\[A soul is real,\] also because otherwise there would be no trance”** (2.3.39).

The *Bhagavad-gita* describes the highest stage as *nirvana* (topmost bliss): *santim nirvana- paramam*, **“The highest state of peacefulness is nirvana”** (6.15).

*Samadhi* is a concept in the two main kinds of Indian philosophy: Vedanta and Buddhism. The main difference between the two is that only the former regards the soul as real. The branch of Vedanta called Advaita-Vedanta is similar to Buddhism in many respects. In my article “Why Advaita is not Vedanta”, I have shown that Sankaracarya’s opinion that the soul isn’t real contradicts many canonical texts of Vedanta.

Gautama Buddha, Sankaracarya and other perfected yogis are called *jivan-mukta* (liberated while living) because they achieved full trance in Brahman and because in the waking state they maintain the state of *saksin* (witness), also called *drastr* (independent observer): the soul is the witness of one’s mental functions and bodily functions.

There are two states of trance:  
1\.   *sabija-samadhi* (trance with awareness of oneself as a soul): *ta eva sa-bijah samadhih* (*Yoga-­sutra* 1.46);  
2\.   *nirbija-samadhi* (trance without any awareness of individuality): *tasyapi nirodhe sarva-nirodhan nirbija-samadhih* (*Yoga-sutra* 1.51).

At the beginning of meditation, the meditator identifies with the mind, as in the normal waking state. Later, in the stage just before trance, the meditator’s consciousness is clearly felt to be distinct from the mind: the consciousness is no longer the mind, but is the witness of the mind. This experience is itself a great accomplishment. It is a form of wise insight insofar as it is the realization that the real self is different from mind and body.

In *sabija-samadhi*, however, the soul is only aware of its existence. This is the first stage of transcendence. It is also called *turiya* (the fourth state) because it lies beyond the three common states of consciousness: wakefulness, dreaming, and deep sleep, and because it is beyond the three modes of material nature: *sattva* (goodness), *rajas* (passion) and *tamas* (ignorance).

The second stage of trance is famously known as *nirvikalpa-samadhi*. In that state of bliss, the consciousness feels oneness with the universe. Many expert meditators have experienced this state.

In Western culture, an enlightenend being is any wise man, such as Eckhart Tolle. In the Hindu tradition, however, an enlightened person is someone who not only reached *nirvikalpa-samadhi*, but who also always maintains the mode of *saksin* (witness) in the waking state too. Such a person obtains the title “Paramahamsa” (topmost swan). It is also the name of the fourth level of Sannyasa, the renounced order of life.

The Hindus believe that several, if not many, lifetimes dedicated to the practice of deep meditation are required in order to become enlightened. Any yogi who falls from the path continues in a future life (*Bhagavad-gita* 6.41-43).

However, most Hindu yogis seek enlightenment only because it guarantees them *moksa*, liberation from the cycle of birth, death and rebirth. In *Bhagavad-gita* (2.72 and 5.25), as a pun the term *brahma-nirvana* (full bliss in Brahman) is used as a synonym of *moksa* (liberation). For instance: *sayogibrahma-nirvanam brahma-bhuto ’dhigacchati*, **“That yogi becomes transcendental and achieves disappearance in Brahman”** (5.24).

In 1995, I had the privilege and honor of spending six months in a meditation retreat in Canada with a group of expert meditators. The facilities were run by the Transcendental Meditation organization founded by Maharishi Mahesh Yogi (1918-2008). Those expert meditators called themselves “the Purushas”. I was initiated by one of them. Most of the Purushas were Americans waiting for their facilities to be constructed in North Carolina, USA. The experience was a blessing.

Maharishi began teaching his technique in the West in 1959. In 1968, the Beatles traveled to Rishikesh in northern India to take part in a Transcendental Meditation (TM) training course at Maharishi’s ashram.

Maharishi had been a disciple of Brahmananda Sarasvati (1871-1953), the spiritual headmaster of the Jyotir Math monastery in the Himalayas. That monastery is one of the four spiritual centers established by Adi Sankaracarya (788-820 CE).

The myths regarding the status of an enlightened being are bewildering to many. For instance, the followers of the Maharishi organization believe he had the same status as Sri Krishna (c. 3200 BCE). To them, Maharishi was always a *Wizard of Oz*-type character. His headquarters were in Holland, in a castle formerly occupied by the Nazis.

The only person allegedly enlightened I have met is Yogeshwar, also known as Sant Shri Balak Yogeshwar Das ji Maharaj. I saw him twice in Radha-kund, Uttar Pradesh, India, in 2010 and 2014. He lives in the Himalayas. I’m not aware of his background, but he has a large retinue and is arrogantly proud of himself. He doesn’t speak English. I never talked to him, but I looked directly in his eyes and believe he genuinely is enlightened.

## 2 Siddhi

The Hindus believe an enlightened being has supernatural powers. Conversely, many also believe that anyone, such as the fraud Sai Baba, who has amazing supernatural powers is either an enlightened being or the reincarnation of an elevated soul.

Paramahamsa Yogananda was self-realized and had astonishing yogic abilities. In his autobiography, he mentions other enlightened yogis who had superpowers. But although Ramakrishna Paramahamsa (1836-1886), the guru of Svami Vivekananda (1863-1902), is widely believed to have been an enlightened being, he had no yogic powers.

The word *siddhi* literally means “success” or “perfection”, but in this context the sense is yogic superpower. The term for enlightenment, however, is *samsiddhi*, “complete success” (*Bhagavad-­gita* 6.43) or *yoga-samsiddhi*, “complete success in uniting the individual consciousness with the universal consciousness” (*Bhagavad-gita* 6.37).

Traditional organizations that teach deep meditation are many. The methods too are manifold. But few organizations also offer training in a *siddhi*. And the methods taught are based on only one book: Patanjali’s *Yoga-sutra*.

There are many **siddhis**. A bunch are listed in the Puranas. The *Yoga-sutra* lists many more. Traditionally, however, these eight *siddhis* are regarded as the main ones:  
**Anima:** The ability to become extremely small, like an ant.  
**Mahima:** The ability to become infinitely large.  
**Garima:** The ability to become heavy as a mountain.  
**Laghima:** The ability to become weightless.  
**Prapti:** The power to obtain anything.  
**Prakamya:** The power to do anything.  
**Isita:** The power to control anyone and anything.  
**Vasita:** The power to bewitch others.

Attaining enlightenment is often viewed as a mythical goal, one almost impossible to achieve, therefore some zealous practitioners spice up their meditation routine with a technique to achieve a yogic superpower.

The yogic power named *laghima* (levitation) is taught to select monks in some Buddhist monasteries in Nepal. The yogi Milarepa (1052-1135) could fly at will in a cross-legged posture, according to the biography *Tibet’s Great Yogi Milarepa* (1928), translated from Tibetan by Walter Evans-Wentz.

In the culture of the Catholic Church, many saints, such as Saint Francis of Assisi, are officially recognized as having had the ability to levitate.

The TM organization teaches *laghima*. The TM practitioners call it yogic flying. It’s a mental and physical exercise of hopping while cross-legged, and is the central aspect of the TM-Sidhi program. A TM-Sidha is a practitioner of Transcendental Meditation who has accomplished the TM-Sidhi programme, the training for yogic flying.

In the US, Maharishi began teaching yogic flying in 1976. Nowadays, an international yogic

flying competition is held every year. Participants try to hop the highest and also endeavor to cross a certain distance by hopping the fastest.

The Maharishi University of Management in Fairfield, Iowa, contains two golden domes where students can practice meditation and yogic hopping in groups.

Katy Perry, Jim Carrey, and David Lynch are advocates of the Transcendental Meditation technique.

The program of yogic hopping is highly structured. Normally, there’s a two-hour session in the morning and another in the evening. One session includes thirty to forty minutes of TM, the repetition of the nineteen English phrases (*sutra*), breathing techniques (*pranayama*), yogic postures (*asana*), and rest periods.

To sell the TM-Sidhi program, the TM movement claims a TM-Sidha is the basic unit of world peace. The TM organization inspires their followers with the idea called the Maharishi Effect, which means an increase in world peace. According to TM propaganda, a great peacemaking effect is made on society when the number of TM-Sidhas reaches more than the square root of 1% of any given population. When such a number of TM-Sidhas regularly meditate as a group, there is a significant reduction of negativity, hostility and violence in the city where they are.

In addition, the TM movement sells their *siddha* program by promising that this advanced course of TM leads to enlightenment. The Purushas whom I met in Canada practiced yogic hopping. But their leader warned me that endeavoring to practice the *siddhis* is a distraction on the path to enlightenment because yogic hoppers become overly proud of themselves. Hence I declined the offer of the Canadian director of the TM-Sidhi program to learn yogic hopping. It’s the only mystical experience in this list that I did not achieve. Placing the feet in the lotus pose is a major requirement to master the TM-Sidhi technique. Even so, some TM-Sidhi practitioners have complained of permanent damage to their knees.

Patanjali discusses a plethora of *siddhis* in the third chapter of Yoga-sutra. He warns that an interest in developing these powers is a major distraction to the true seeker: *te samadhav upasarga vyutthane siddhayah*, **“The siddhis are obstacles to samadhi. They are accomplishments to those who stray from the path”** (Yoga-sutra 3.37).

## 3 Krsna-prema

Vedanta has two branches: Advaita and Vaishnavism. The goals of Advaita were expounded: enlightenment, liberation, and yogic superpowers.

Vaishnavism prioritizes bhakti, devotion to God. Bhakti is for people who are sentimental. Advaita, on the other hand, focuses on meditation.

Of the four main groups of Vaishnavism, the first two are the biggest:  
1\.   The Visistadvaita school of Ramanuja Acarya, well-established in Madurai, Tamil Nadu  
2\.   The Dvaita school of Madhva Acarya, based in Udupi, Karnataka  
3\.   The Dvaitadvaita school of Nimbarka Acarya, popular in Rajasthan and Vrindavan  
4\.   The Suddhadvaita school of Vallabha Acarya, based in Nathadwar, Rajasthan.

All Vedantic sects except one propound the idea that the goal of life is to become liberated from the cycle of birth and death. The exception is Gaudiya Vaishnavism, an independent bhakti sect whose founders, in the fifteenth century, were associated with the Madhva sampradaya.

The Gaudiya Vaishnavas, so called because the sect originates from Gauda (Bengal), do not seek liberation from the cycle of birth and death. Rather, they long to attain *krsna-prema*, love of God. They call this the fifth goal of life, which supersedes the well-known four of Indian culture: *dharma* (moral duty), *artha* (career), *kama* (sensory pleasures) and *moksa* (liberation).

In Gaudiya Vaishnavism, Krishna is God. Three cities are regarded as major places of worship: Navadipa (Bengal), Jagannatha Puri (Orissa), and Vrindavan (Uttar Pradesh).

Advaitins and Vaishvanas have different interpretations of the core texts of Vedanta philosophy. I have explained the matter in my article “Why Advaita is not Vedanta”. In short, Gaudiya Vaishnavas argue that Krishna is God and is superior to Brahman. They say the nature of God is Rasa (mojo), and this Rasa, which is spiritual (transcendental to the three modes of nature), can be experienced in the form of *bhakti-rasa* (devotional joy), which arises while reading literary texts based on stories of Krishna or while taking part in sessions of devotional chants.

Moreover, the Gaudiya Vaishnavas’ concept of love of God differs from the concept in other religions in the sense that *krsna-prema* is a particular religious experience. Although the Gaudiya Vaishnavas sometimes use the term *krsna-prema* in the general meaning of love of God like other religious groups might do, the difference is that in Gaudiya Vaishnavism, *krsna-prema* also refers to a specific kind of ecstatic occurrence.

In this experience, the devotee reaches the goal of devotional activities. After years of the spiritual practice of chanting the Hare Krishna mantra, reading devotional literature, hearing devotional music, and so on, the devotee, following the moods expressed in writing by the great gurus of the past, sometimes feels the dejection of separation from Krishna and at other times tastes the joy of having a deep connection with Him.

In the experience of *krsna-prema*, the devotee feels a kind of bliss which is both the acme of dejection and the acme of profound affection. The scriptures of Gaudiya Vaishnavism describe this state as a mix of poison and nectar, that is, a mix of poison-like pain and nectar-like bliss: the poison of the utmost dejection of feeling separation from Krishna and the ambrosia of the utmost joy of having a heartfelt connection with Him. For example:

*ei prema-asvadana, tapta-iksu-carvana*  
*mukha-jvale, na jaya tyajana*  
*sei prema ydnra mane, tara vikrama sei jane,*  
*visamrte ekatra milana*

**Relishing love of God is like chewing hot sugarcane. The mouth burns, but you don’t give up chewing. Only a devotee in whose mind abides love of God knows the way it occurs. It’s like poison and nectar mixed together.** (*Caitanya-caritamrta* 2.2.51)

*pidabhir nava-kdla-kйta-katutd-garvasya nirvasano nisyandena mudam sudhd-madhurimdhankdra-sankocanah \|*  
*prema sundari nanda-nandana-paro jagarti yasyantare jnayante sphutam asya vakra-madhuras tenaiva vikrantayah \|\|*

**My dear beautiful young lady, when a person experiences the rise of love of Krishna, all the acrid and sweet effects of the love will vividly manifest in the heart. This love acts in two ways. It brings one close to death because its poison is worse than the poison of a snake, yet by effusing various kinds of delight it shatters the gods’ pride of owning the nectar.** (*Vidagdha-madhava* 2.18, of Rupa Gosvami)

In the state of *krsna-prema*, the devotee tears up and becomes completely chocked up, being confounded by the simultaneous occurrence of the contradictory emotions of dejection and joy. This is covertly described in Bhagavata Purana:

*sri-suka uvaca*  
*iti gopyah pragayantyah pralapantyas ca citradha \|*  
*ruruduh su-svaram rajan krsna-darsana-lalasah \|\|*

**Suka said: “Having eminently sung in that fashion, O king, the gopls, who eagerly desired to see Krishna, prattled in various ways and wept in a sweet pitch.”** (*Bhagavatam* 10.32.1)

The gopis, the girls of Vrindavan, prattled (*pralapantyah*). The sense of this participle is derived from the meaning of the corresponding noun: *vyarthalapah pralapah syat*, “*Pralapa* (prattle, babble) means saying words that have no meaning” (*Ujjvala-nilamani* 11.87). In Indian aesthetic theory, the tenth and final stage of separation is death, that is, a death-like state, meaning the will to live is gone. It is the same as the *vyabhicari-bhava* (transient emotion) called *mrti* (death), characterized by uttering unclear syllables, according to Rupa Gosvami: *prana-tyago mrtis tasyam avyaktaksara-bhasanam* (*Bhakti-rasamrta-sindhu* 2.4.99).

## 4 Kavya-cid-rasa

Of all kinds of artistic writings in the world, Sanskrit poetical literature is not well known, yet it deserves a special place for several reasons:  
1\.   The theory of Rasa (aesthetic delight);  
2\.   A rich tradition: there is a plethora of natural phenomena used as various symbols of life, and the heroes and heroines follow codes of conduct expressive of real love;  
3\.   The figures of speech are many, and imagination in the scope of figures of comparison is unbounded;  
4\.   The meter of a verse has a strong rhythm which accentuates the music of the sounds;  
5\.   The rules of composition are such that a text always has a good vibe, for example: *madhurena samapayet*, “One should end a composition with sweetness”;  
6\.   The highest aesthetic experience, *kavya-cid-rasa* (transcendental aesthetic delight in poetry), is a type of self-realization.

Rasa, aesthetic delight, is the combination of *ananda* (joy) and *camatkara* (astonishment).

To this day, the theory of Rasa continues to confound scholars. Around the world, professors of Sanskrit literature do not understand that the four theories of Rasa related by Mammata in *Kavya- prakasa* (c. 1070 CE), the bible of Sanskrit poetics, correspond to two, not one, experiences of aesthetic delight.

In my book *Versified History of Sanskrit Poetics* (2017), I have explained the matter, and have also expounded Pandita-raja Jagannatha’s twelve theories of Rasa.

The Sanskrit poetical rhetoricians busied themselves with the questions:  
A.    How is aesthetic delight aroused in a reader or spectator?  
B.    What is the nature of aesthetic delight?  
C.   Who is qualified to experience aesthetic delight?

There are two fundamental experiences of aesthetic delight in literature:  
1\.   Enjoyment of the art  
2\.   Rapture.

The enjoyment of the poetic art occurs when a *sthayi-bhava* (foundational mood) becomes relishable.

There are ten major foundational moods in Sanskrit literature: *srngara* (romantic love), *hasya* (humor), *vira* (heroism), *raudra* (anger), *karuna* (lamentation), *bhayanaka* (fear), *bibhatsa* (horror), *adbhuta* (only amazement), *santa* (peacefulness), and *bhakti* (devotion to God).

In a text, a foundational mood enhanced by specific factors becomes relished, by the definition: *vibhavanubhava-vyabhicari-samyogad rasa-nispattih,* **“The effectuation of aesthetic delight occurs from the blend of vibhava (stimuli), anubhava (effect in a character), and vyabhicari- bhava (emotion in a character)”** (*Natya-sastra* 6.32).

Rapture takes place after the enjoyment of the art has occurred. Once the foundational mood is being relished, the enjoyment increases by dwelling on the meanings of the text, and eventually turns into rapture when an outstanding implied sense is understood and bliss floods the mind.

In literature, this is a once-in-a-lifetime epiphany, brought about by years of dedication to the art, but in terms of onstage performances the experience of rapture may occur while watching an excellent comedian on stage, for instance.

Rapture, the highest aesthetic experience, is a type of self-realization. Pandita-raja Jagannatha made this clear by drawing from Abhinavagupta’s Rasa theory, which incorporates the notion of transcendental experience as formulated in Bhatta Nayaka’s theory.

No other literature in the world involves theory in the scope of such experiences.

Pandita-raja Jagannatha stated: *cid eva rasah*, *“The transcendental consciousness is the aesthetic delight”* (*Rasa-gangadhara*).

In his own treatise on poetics, Baladeva Vidyabhusana, the famed Gaudiya commentator on Vedanta-sutra, agreed with Jagannatha by rephrasing his dictum and replacing the word *cid* (transcendental consciousness) with *atma* (soul): *atmaivanandamse vibhavadibhir bhagnavaranas tad-vyanjita-raty-ady-avacchinnas tat-sannibhas ca rasah*, **“The soul whose layer of its aspect of bliss is shattered by the vibhavas (the persons and the things), the anubhavas (the bodily reactions), and the vyabhicari-bhavas (emotions) in the text is the Rasa, which is particularized by the foundational mood, implied by those ones, and which is like that bliss.”** (*Sahitya- kaumudi* 4.10) (Kavya-mala ed. p. 31)

## 5 Metta-bhavana

*Metta-bhavana* is the Buddhist meditative practice of cultivating loving kindness toward all sentient beings.

It is a Pali term which means “meditation in loving kindness”. The trance-like state referred to in this list of mystical experiences is the quintessential state in *metta-bhavana*.

The Pali word *metta* comes from the Sanskrit *maitri* (friendship). In both languages, *bhavana* means contemplation.

*Metta-bhavana* is an adjunct to Buddhist meditation, and is taught as a guided meditation, often in groups.

In India, *metta-bhavana* is taught in many Vipassana meditation retreats. The practitioners are not allowed to speak for the entire duration of their stay, i.e. ten days or more.

It’s a technique whereby we radiate loving kindness and goodwill toward all beings, deliberately charging the atmosphere around us with the calming, positive vibrations of pure and compassionate love.

*Metta-bhavana* is a type of meditation filled with so much altruistic love that it makes the seekers of enlightenment and other kinds of meditators look like narcissistic maniacs.

A person learns *metta-bhavana* through a guided meditation in five steps. Prior to beginning, however, meditators are taught that greed, hatred and delusion are defilements of the mind: The meditators must acknowledge the existence of those three, understand how they come into existence, and realize they are mental obstacles to be overcome. According to Buddhist teachings:

So long as negativities such as aversion dominate the mind, it is futile to formulate conscious thoughts of goodwill. \[...\] For this reason, the technique of *metta-bhavana* is introduced only at the end of a Vipassana course, after the participants have passed through the process of purification. \[...\] *Nibbana* can be experienced only by those whose minds are filled with loving-kindness and compassion for all beings. Simply wishing for that state is not enough; we must purify our minds to attain it.

At the first stage of *metta-bhavana*, the meditators are taught to express loving kindness toward oneself. The affirmation for this stage is something like: **“May I be happy and free from suffering.”** (*Visuddhimagga* 9.8)

The next steps are to direct one’s loving kindness toward:  
2\.   A loved one  
3\.   A neutral or indifferent person  
4\.   An enemy or someone they dislike  
5\.   All sentient beings.

A mystical experience in *metta-bhavana* is reached in deep meditation, when the Heart Chakra bursts open and unconditional love for all beings powerfully flows from the heart.

## Afterword

How the ways of practicing loving kindness should continue in day-to-day life is explained variously according to each culture. In Vedic culture, for instance, much emphasis is put on empathy:

*sarva-bhuta-sthitam yo mam bhajaty ekatvam asthitah \|*  
*sarvatha vartamano ’pi sa yogi mayi vartate \|\|*  
*atmaupamyena sarvatra samam pasyati yo 'juna \|*  
*sukham va yadi va duhkham sa yogi paramo matah \|\|*

**A man who, acknowledging the concept of one God everywhere, reveres Me who am situated in all beings is, in fact, a yogi who abides in Me even while living his life in various circumstances. My dear Arjuna, in My opinion, a man who sees the happiness and suffering of others as though their happiness and suffering are his own is the topmost yogi.** (*Bhagavad-­gita* 6.32-33)

In Western culture, Maslow’s pyramid is the most famous model of personal growth. His hierarchy of needs is a motivational theory in psychology. Abraham Maslow’s pyramid, introduced by him in 1943, represents his five levels of a person’s hierarchy of needs. Not everyone moves through the hierarchy in a unidirectional manner but may move back and forth between the different types of needs.

Self-actualization, the highest level, is the fulfillment of one’s potential professionally, academically, athletically, or creatively, ideally for the benefit of humanity. Prior to this stage are these four levels representing various basic needs:  
1\.   Food, lodging  
2\.   Safety, job security  
3\.   Love and friendships  
4\.   Self-esteem due to a successful career.

In self-actualization, a person comes to find a meaning to life that is important to them. While self-actualizers hail from a variety of backgrounds and a diversity of occupations, they share notable characteristics in common:

Some of such characteristics which distinguish self-actualized individuals from the rest of humanity are as follows (Maslow, 1954, 1970).  
1\.   Self-actualized people are accepting of others’ as well as their own flaws, often with humor and tolerance. Not only do self-actualized people fully accept others, they are also true to themselves rather than pretending in order to impress others (Talevich, 2017).  
2\.   Self-actualized people also tend to be independent and resourceful: they are less likely to rely upon external authorities to direct their lives (Martela & Pessi, 2018).  
3\.   Can cultivate deep and loving relationships with others.  
4\.   Tendency to exude gratitude and maintain a deep appreciation even for the commonplace blessings in life.  
5\.   Can often discern between the superficial and the real when judging situations.  
6\.   Seldom depend upon their environment or culture to form their opinions.  
7\.   Tendency to view life as a mission which calls them to a purpose beyond themselves.

The need for self-actualization simply means the need to become everything one is capable of becoming. Therefore, in 1970 Maslow added levels to his pyramid, involving a change of hierarchy:  
5\.   Need for knowledge  
6\.   Need for aesthetic experiences  
7\.   Need for self-actualization  
8\.   Need for transcendental, mystical experiences, etc. (www.sloww.co/transcendence-maslow/)
