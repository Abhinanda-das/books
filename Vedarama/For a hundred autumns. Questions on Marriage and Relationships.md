# For a hundred autumns. Questions on Marriage and Relationships

## This book

For the past forty-five years, people have been asking me questions about the spiritual path of yoga described in India’s wisdom texts, the Vedas. And for nearly forty of those years I have been married. So naturally, people have often asked me questions on relationships, marriage, and family – and how they can be part of an authentic spiritual path.

Sometimes, my questioners have been struggling with challenges to their relationship – whether arguments, adultery, or boredom; or they have discussed positive life stages such as their wedding, conception, child-rearing, the mid-life crisis and more.

In this book I have collected many of those questions, and my answers, and organised them according to themes. I think it makes for a good conversation and I hope you will find it helpful.

The title of this book is taken from the 8,000-year-old Sanskrit wedding vow:

**“May we remain together, listen to joyous sounds together. May we always experience well-being, and speaking loving words, may we remain together for a hundred autumns.”**

Many hundreds of people have helped me write this book. It is based on thousands of hours of exchanges with them, as well as collected traditional and modern wisdom. But the views expressed in these pages are my own.

## First questions

## What’s the purpose of marriage?

The Vedic tradition urges every human being to aspire to four types of success: good thoughts, good deeds and selfless service to others, the achievement of sustainable wealth and health, the experience of love and happiness, and spiritual freedom, (dharma , artha , kama and moksha ). The last one is the most important of all, and the other three are meant to support that main aim. Becoming completely free is the goal of life, and therefore the purpose of marriage.

## How can being married set me free?

It may sound contradictory, but the bonds of marriage can help you achieve real freedom. You’ll spend most of your life married, so your work towards the four goals of life will be done with a partner by your side. Having someone with you on that long journey of life will be a great support and encouragement for both of you. It may happen gradually, but ultimately a husband and wife learn the most important lessons in life from each other, and it is those lessons that award you your freedom.

## What are those lessons?

The Vedic scriptures explain that we are eternal living souls placed in this world because we rebelled against the service of God. Our selfish ego caused us to think we could be happy by being the centre of the universe. Although that was long ago, we still tend to see everything and everyone as meant for our pleasure. Marriage changes that. In order to be happily married, both partners must learn the gentle art of service to each other. And that’s one of the main lessons in life: that true happiness comes from service.

## But will marriage make me happy?

Marriage is not supposed to make you happy. It’s supposed to make you married, and once you are safely and totally married you have a structure of security and support from which you can find your happiness.

## You’re saying that marriage will make me unhappy?

No, but you must look for the right kind of happiness at the right time. There are three types of happiness: the first is happiness that comes from ordinary pleasure. It’s based on gaining something pleasing for the senses, and it comes and goes very quickly. The second is happiness derived from making others happy, and it stays in the heart for longer. The third happiness is joy, the deepest inner pleasure. It comes from the soul. In Sanskrit language these are known as bhoga , sukha , and ananda , respectively. The first is all about serving your body and mind, the second about serving others, and the third is about serving God. In life we are meant to grow gradually from the selfishness of childhood to the selflessness of adulthood. The end of this growth is the realisation that service to God produces the ultimate joy. Marriage can help you progress through these three stages. In the beginning of marriage, you might well be young and a little selfish. If you look for happiness while you’re selfish, you’ll never find it. Happiness is only found by giving it to someone else - and by being very patient!

## So how would you describe marriage?

In every civilised culture, marriage is the joining of a man and a woman exclusively to each other for life. It is a union witnessed by the bride and groom’s parents, the community, by the government and by God. So, it is a social, psychological, physical, and spiritual bond to accomplish the four aims of life with the goal of service to God. These bonds are also the boundaries that mark the marriage. When we know that a couple is joined in this way, we respect those boundaries. Like growing a precious crop, the boundaries allow them to grow within a field with four strong hedges.

## What do you mean by ‘social bond,’ and why is that important?

It means that as a community the couple’s friends, relatives, work colleagues and well-wishers come together to witness, bless and celebrate the union of the couple in a wedding ceremony, and that they help the couple honour their commitments to each other. Even if the couple moves away, the social bond means their community still stay in touch and help with their relationship if they can. We believe that the married couple is the foundation of the family, and the family is the foundation of the community. So, to keep the community strong, it is in our interest to keep every couple strong in their marriage.

## You said physical and psychological bond – aren’t they the same?

A physical bond means that the married couple care for each other’s physical needs by working to provide financial and domestic essentials. A husband and wife make sure the other never goes hungry, always has clothing and comforts, medical treatment and physical care. Physical also includes sexual, and as a community we safeguard their marriage and protect them from adultery by understanding that any sexual intimacy takes place only between them.

Psychological or emotional bond means that the married couple learn to think together, complement each other’s mental development and care for each other’s sense of mental well-being. As a couple, they also learn to adjust their strong, individual sense of ‘I’ into a ‘We.’

## So, the community should be involved in every detail of a couple’s life?

No, not every detail – couples need privacy, too! But we think that society has become just a little too removed from actually helping couples. In years gone by, the community would all know a young couple, live close by to them, and help them with the early years of their married life. This is a vital element of support that is now often missing. Perhaps there would be less troubled marriages if the community made happy marriages more of a collective priority.

## Isn’t it up to the couple to help themselves?

Of course, but there are social conditions in which marriages thrive, and there are other conditions in which they struggle. A couple must do all within their power to keep their relationship dynamic and healthy, that’s true, but the community must play its part. Divorce is very painful, and has long-term repercussions for the partners, the children, and the community itself. It should be avoided at all costs, and prevention is better than a cure. Many divorces are in fact preventable, and a helpful circle of friends, family members and the community can make all the difference.

## What do you mean by spiritual bond?

The purpose of marriage is for the couple to help each other make spiritual progress. Everything the couple does is meant to be done in the light of that aim. Therefore, marriage also includes a promise that they will help each other cultivate essential spiritual qualities and safeguard each other’s progress through mutual support. In every marriage, partners develop spiritually in their own way, and at different speeds, so it’s important they help each other without any undue pressure or unrealistic aspirations. They can choose the most suitable practices and goals and help each other through any challenges. Essential spiritual qualities include internal and external cleanliness, cultivation of knowledge, charity, self-control, aversion to finding faults in others, freedom from anger, compassion, steady determination, gentleness, generosity, modesty, forgiveness and honesty.

## But what if we have different religions?

There may be religious differences, which are mainly external, but the spiritual aspect, which is universal, is above the particulars of the religious. What we call ‘religion’ has much to do with how we express the internal feelings of our connection with God. The sacred texts and language we use, the rituals and ceremonies, the places we worship and the teacher we follow – these are details and are all secondary to the essentials. With a sense of spiritual affinity, it is possible for a couple whose religions are apparently different to have a happy marriage. But if a couple simply considers the external details, rather than the essence, they will never agree on anything.

## Many people live together these days – is marriage absolutely necessary?

Many people want to have the pleasure of sexual intimacy with minimum responsibility, or they want to lessen the cost. There’s the story of a foolish farmer who had a large chicken that laid very big eggs. The farmer sold the eggs in the market and got a good price, but the chicken ate a lot of grain and the farmer had to also pay for that. He thought to himself: ‘I want to keep the back part of the chicken because it makes me money, but I don’t want the front part of the chicken because it costs me money.’

Thinking in this way, he took an axe and cut the chicken down the middle. Of course, the chicken died, leaving him with nothing. This is called ardha-murgi-nyaya or ‘half-chicken logic.’ So, people in general want to have the pleasure of sex without being married, or they want to be married without having to care for children. This has resulted in many different approaches to traditional marriage.

## But in general, traditional marriage seems to be an old-fashioned idea, doesn’t it?

Fashions of marriage may come and go, but marriage as a permanent, life-long commitment has no substitute. Besides, the fashion for alternatives to marriage have all been tried and the results are painfully evident. For instance, casual sex is a relationship with no commitment and has resulted in thousands of teenage pregnancies, up to a third ending in abortions. It leads to emotionally cheated women and parentless children, many of whom have mental health problems in later life. It robs both mother and child of companionship, support and the trust needed for a proper relationship.

## But why is living together so wrong?

Because there’s very low commitment in the relationship. Expressions of undying love aside, your only commitment is to share the same living space. You are legally unprotected and in general, according to marriage guidance experts, there is more fighting in the relationship. Couples who live together tend to put safety first, and don’t ‘rock the boat,’ while married couples take more risks to confront deeper issues, so they evolve more.

Couples who live together have a 50% chance of divorce after the first child is five years old, whereas the risk for married couples stands at 8%. It may be that couples feel they have an advantage in living together because there is a ‘back door’ to the relationship. The problem is that the ‘door’ is often used. Even if couples live together before they get married, there is still an 80% greater divorce rate, because couples who have lived together before marriage are three times more likely to cheat when they finally do get married.

## But why a religious marriage, why not just legal?

Being legally married is important, but just being legally married makes the marriage weak. Legal marriage is easily done – but so is legal divorce. Just knowing that you can be easily divorced creates fuzzy thinking about your lifelong commitments to each other. It is known as

‘conditional marriage,’ where you remain together ‘as long as we like each other,’ but it contributes to you both being unable to make the marriage work properly. The difficulty with divorce – as easily as the law has now made it – is that the emotional trauma is no less even though divorce has become easier. Divorce is still a main cause of stress and poor mental health.

If it happens at all it should only take place when all other solutions have failed. Every attempt at saving the marriage should have been made. It is an option only in cases of abuse or final desertion. The sacred texts do provide conditions whereby a divorce is possible, such as desertion, but the same texts urge that any difficulties should be worked through. The ‘easy option’ of divorce has severe repercussions and is not ‘easy’ at all. In today’s world, so afraid of commitment have many become, that thousands do not get married at all, and the figures for divorce have consequently dropped.

Do traditional ideas explain how to keep love alive? Indeed, they do, but they also explain that true love requires sacrifice, and that it may require a lifetime to develop it. In marriage there’s a progression of different kinds of love, each of them nobler than the one before. You might call the first love a kind of craving. Craving springs from infatuation, and is romantic, emotional, and physical - but by itself it leads to an unsuitable relationship if you’re not careful. This type of love must be there to procreate, but it needs strong boundaries. When it ends, some think that the relationship is over, but it’s merely time for the next type of love to begin. Companionship love literally means having a good friend who we eat our meals with. We take interest in their life, do things together with them, and share thoughts while travelling. This is a true companion, and our marriage partner must be a companion on our journey. The next ‘C’ is for Caring . Caring love is a love that is one of selfless service, traditionally caring for each other in sickness or health, whether rich or poor. With this kind of love dominant, we support or offer encouragement, and choose not to say some things if they may hurt or be unhelpful. We don’t push our partner for sex or service. This love doesn’t turn you into a ‘doormat,’ but it is sacrificial. The final type of love is Commitment . It means that: ‘I will love you under circumstances in which you may no longer be lovable.’ This love is based not on feelings but promises. Without these, a lifelong guarantee, confidence and trust doesn’t happen. It is this commitment that sets you free. It gives peace of mind but is not a licence for complacency. All four types of love are required throughout a marriage, despite commitment being the very foundation of the relationship, and selfless service the guiding principle.

## What can I realistically expect from marriage?

Well, realistically, there are two extremes: the cynical person expects too little and it turns into a self-fulfilling prophecy. The romantic person expects too much and becomes disillusioned by reality. To be realistic we must realistically consider human nature, our bodies, minds, emotions but also take into consideration God’s grace. If we ask God to help us then we’ll receive healing, forgiveness and blessings – and that’s real.

But love needs hard work. It is a verb not a noun, therefore love means an action that needs to happen. We should not think of being ‘in love’ but of practically loving someone with acts of kindness and service. You can only get out of a marriage what you put in. We should also know – realistically – how to create a marriage, how to sustain it, and most of all how to protect it as the years go by.

## Preparing for marriage

## How do I prepare for marriage?

Here’s a few practical things you can do before you take the plunge:

Develop Personal Maturity by asking these questions: Who am I? Do I like solitude or company? Do I like working with people or things? Do I seek financial success? What would I like to achieve in 20 years? If money was no object what would I do? Where do I want to live – in the country or the city? (some kind of plan must be there).

Your own personal matters need to be resolved before ‘infatuation.’ Whoever your partner is, they are not going to heal your troubled childhood or your bad habits. You’ll need to be in good health, have somewhere to live, and you’ll need money. Do not get married without making these arrangements first.

Learn to relate to others : You don’t need to learn about ‘craving’ because that comes naturally enough, but companionship, caring and commitment you do! What would it take for you to become a more caring and committed person, a good companion for someone? Find a person with a good and happy marriage and learn from them.

Acquire Contentment: To be content when married, you must learn to be content when single, so you can make a sober decision when the time comes. Do not look to another person to ‘complete’ you, no matter what modern relationship books tell you.

## Is it easy to get married for the wrong reasons?

Yes, and many people do! Here are some bad reasons to get married:

• I’m in love - (infatuation alone is a bad reason because you may become infatuated with someone else later).

• He said he loves me. I think I could love him.

• It will help me sort my life out (marriage isn’t therapy, your problems will resurface).

• I need to get married because everyone else is married – I don’t want to miss out.

• My parents urged me to. I want to make them happy.

• I feel sorry for him (respect doesn’t come from sympathy).

• I need someone to sort my life out. I think she could do it.

• I want to improve my life with money and a visa.

• My parents died and I feel so lonely now.

• If not him, then who? If not now, then when?

## I can’t say that I have none of those reasons, are you saying I shouldn’t get married?

Nobody marries for only the right reasons, we are all selfish, but if you only have the wrong reasons then you should think again. But if you have decided to get married, here are some suggestions for making the right choice.

1\. You are looking for a match, not your twin. Differences help us develop but too much difference between prospective partners can be an unhelpful sign. Differences attract, but they also create a healthy tension. Don’t be put off by differences but remember that there is a thin line between tension and drifting apart. You are looking for an adequate match – not an exact match. The idea of finding a ‘soul mate’ is largely a myth.

2\. Remember that you are looking for potential, not perfection. Marriage is not like nailing two pieces of wood together, but more like two trees growing together.

3\. Don’t just feel, with your heart – think with your head!

4\. Beware of infatuation, it can creep up on you before you’ve had time to think properly.

5\. Take advice from three happily married couples and parents.

6\. Take your time, there is no need to rush. But don’t wait for ever, either.

7\. Remember to be always honest.

## Can astrology be helpful in making a choice of partner?

Yes, in eastern cultures it is the traditional first resort of someone looking for a suitable partner, but best done by a qualified person. Astrology has several divisions. One is predictive or ‘telling the future.’ We are not concerned with that here. The important division in choosing a marital partner is known as ‘compatibility astrology.’ There are thirty-two points of comparison a competent astrologer is looking for between the chart of the young man and woman.

If there is no prospective partner at the time of making a comparison, the chart will reveal the elements a suitable partner should possess. ‘Suitable’ means that the personalities would match rather than conflict. It will make choices easier. In the old days someone would be there to do the looking for you – a matchmaker. They would be armed with your astrological reading and know the sort of person who would be a good match. However, astrology is but one element involved in making a good choice.

## If I do meet someone, what comes next? I want to do it the right way.

In former years, society was arranged so everyone knew the steps to take. In India a prospective bridegroom would either be approached by the bride’s father, or he would ask a brother or uncle to ask the father for him. He would not approach the girl directly. In the western world there were also indirect ways of finding out if a girl would be interested. If the answer was ‘yes,’ then a period of ‘courtship’ would begin. It was all very gradual and had its own rules and procedures. Now we want an agency or an ‘app’ to take the steps for us!

## I’ve already met someone, and we seem to get on well. At what stage do we need to tell anyone else?

That depends on the cultural background and family tradition of you both. One of you may come from a more traditional family than the other. Normally it is the girl’s prerogative to inform the boy when she feels that time has arrived. Generally, it is when one or both of you feel that your relationship could have the beginnings of something longer term. It may also depend on your age. If you are young and your parents are still involved in your decision-making, it would be wrong to leave them out of this important area of your life. Parents can provide helpful advice, no matter how different their general opinions on everything else seem to be.

## But maybe you have some guidance on the modern stages of ‘courtship?’

Well, no-one seems to use that word now, but ‘courtship’ is a traditional word to describe an important stage. In that stage the first thing is to decide what you want: make a list of Social factors: do they fit in with your friends, your preferred occupation (varna ), and do they want children. Then there are some Psychological factors: will they empathise with your outlook, your manner, your hopes and concerns. What about your sense of humour? Will they understand your problems? (Do not choose someone with the same problems!) Physical : Are you attracted to their looks, character and personality? Spiritual : do they have any kind of spirituality, a daily practise, a compatible philosophy? Do they study, meditate, give in charity, share their wisdom with others? Remember in all this that compatibility grows, and that you’re looking for an initial, not full, compatibility.

## This is a good list. Is there more?

You can make a list: of (i) Essential things (ii) Important things (iii) Desirable things and (iv) Irrelevant things. Place the following into the above four categories: personal wealth, career and aspirations, their housing preferences, their desire to travel or remain local, their religion and faith, vegetarian, political views, sense of humour.

## What about going out together? What’s your advice?

Take it slow. Remember that in the beginning you’re seeing whether a friendship could develop, nothing more. Keep boundaries. Avoid forming a relationship when you or the other person is in a position of spiritual or institutional authority or respect. It tends to change the perception of the relationship. Many women fall in love with their priests, but the results are often disastrous. Friendship – and that is what you are trying to establish - is based on a high level of equality and mutual respect. Avoid touching.

Next comes what you might call ‘low-level courtship.’ You are trying to discover more about them without becoming attached prematurely. Try to avoid becoming involved with anyone who has persistent problems like mental instability, dishonesty, drugs, gambling or pornography, violence or being secretive. Ask them questions to establish the facts. Did they have a history of brief relationships before meeting you? Do they have friends? You need a partner, not a patient.

At this level challenges mainly come in four areas - Social : Their values, expectations, culture, social structures they belong to, country of origin and so on. Psychological : their mother language, education, whether they feel uprooted as a person; their expectations of you as a potential partner, their troubled past. Financial and Career : What are the practical prospects of their career development and long-term domestic maintenance? Private ownership of home and savings? Spiritual : Religion and moral values, their core beliefs and practices. Their hopes for spiritual progress. You also need to discover their age, health, history. There should be no sexual arousal at this time. Keep it cool.

## So, I know a lot about them. What happens now?

Things get more serious at the next level, which is when you’ve discovered more about them and you’re developing the relationship because you think you want to go ahead and meet them regularly. At this stage you are still careful and watching how they behave in different situations. For instance, the social dimension. Meet them in different social settings. Do things in a non-formal setting. See their family. Understand that they may become like their parents. Ask about their interests, their greatest social mismatch, and their aspirations.

Again, there’s a psychological dimension where you should see them in different situations and at different times of day. How do they handle disappointment or problems? Do they take it out on you? Can they apologise and say, “I was wrong” when needed? Do they stand up for their rights? How do they handle conflict situations? Are they bringing unresolved problems with them? Are they kind? Are they team players? If your well-being depended on their judgement, could you trust them? Can the two of you communicate? Can you express deep feelings? Or do they engage in mostly ‘surface-talk?’ Do you ever feel ashamed of this person in a social situation?

## What else?

You must also consider the area of sexual attraction, asking yourself whether there is a spark. But arousal muddies the waters, and prevents you having a clear view of all the other factors, so don’t place yourself in compromising situations. This stage is a transit point, not a destination.

## What about the spiritual aspect?

This area is often the last to be considered but where your spirituality is the most important factor of your life, it might well be the first aspect you consider. Questions could include: what do you actually believe in? Does it actively influence and direct your life? Do you want to move forward in your chosen path? What is their path, and can they harmonise with you? Do your ideas clash? What will you do about that? Spiritual considerations and religious group affiliations are important, but you should not be so much interested in marrying a member of the same group that you fail to consider whether the person is suitable for you – in all the categories.

## I find this all quite analytical. Shouldn’t I just let it flow and see what happens?

Many people have done exactly that, and then drifted into a long-term relationship or marriage, not considering all the factors involved. The results have often been disastrous. You should make it easy for yourself at a later stage, by being considerate at an earlier stage. Marriage is scientific more than romantic. At least before you plunge ahead to an engagement, please ask these Twelve Tough Questions:

1\. Do I have any concerns? Is there anything about this person that rules them out as being my lifelong partner?

2\. Are there things that I make excuses for? Am I making a subconscious attempt to conceal some incompatibility?

3\. Are there things about them that I expect to change? Will they change or am I being too hopeful? Do I honestly feel they’ll change after we’re together? Can I learn to live with their bad habits if they don’t change?

4\. How do they fit my ideal of a marriage partner? What about them is essential, important, desirable or irrelevant? Are they missing some things I previously thought essential?

5\. Do I respect this person? Am I proud of them sometimes? Am

I embarrassed or annoyed at some things they do or say

6\. Do I have the best sort of friendship with them? Can we talk honestly and be open with each other?

7\. Can I live with the focus of their life? What motivates them? Where do I fit into that vision? Are they interested in helping me reach my goals?

8\. Is this relationship having a positive or a negative effect on my life? Does this relationship encourage me to do the good things and discourage me from doing the bad? Does it generate psychological energy, or does it take energy to keep it going? Do I feel stimulated, excited and challenged, or am I just weary? Do I feel ‘more me’ with them or ‘less me?’ Do they liberate or confine me?

9\. Do I love them enough, and in the right way?

10\. Can I be content with this person? Can I make this person my only partner? Or am I keeping my eye out for someone else?

11\. Do my friends and family support this relationship?

12\. Do I want to share my life with this person?

## If I go through all these ‘pre-flight checks,’ how long should I wait before getting married?

An engagement should take as long as required – but not too long. It will take some weeks or months for you to get to know each other, but when you are both sure you should go ahead. We are all afraid of commitment these days, and so sometimes engagements go on for years. The couple expect something quite miraculous to happen to really show them they’ve both made the right choice – but the miracle doesn’t quite happen. So, engagements can easily turn into ‘spending a weekend away together,’ which can quickly become ‘living together.’ It is best to avoid that as it leads to a weaker relationship in the future.

## We want to get engaged – how should we do it?

The Vedic engagement is known as vag-danam , and it is the giving of a promise by the girl’s father to the young man’s family that he will be giving his daughter to the young man on a chosen date. There are several variants of the ceremony, but the spoken or written promise is the essence in all the versions. While modern engagements are mostly private, consisting of the boy asking the girl if she will marry him, then presenting her with a ring to symbolise her new status, the traditional version involves the girl’s family and friends, and a public promise. After the verbal promise, the father will present the young man with a symbol of his promise, presentable at the commencement of the wedding ceremony. Today, ceremonies are arranged that incorporate both the traditional and the modern ring-giving elements.

## How long after the engagement should we get married?

In the traditional, Vedic engagement, the date of the wedding is already announced, so everyone can then work towards it. In the modern engagement it is left open-ended so the couple can decide themselves when the ‘big day’ will be. It’s also the last period during which the relationship can be called off. One big problem with this arrangement is that couples sometimes don’t fix a date at all. Many are afraid of the impending commitment, and so ‘living together’ appears to be the cautious and easy option. That way they never have to make a decision on the date of the marriage, and they eventually decide not to have a wedding.

But for all the reasons we’ve already discussed, a decision not to have a wedding is a poor one and contributes to a much weaker relationship in the long run. It also makes a nonsense of the engagement itself, since getting engaged means ‘engaged to be married.’ Engagement periods, traditionally, can run from a few days, weeks or months to a year. More than one year is too long. Whatever length of engagement you have, it is not an excuse for sexual intimacy. It is a waiting period during which you should both think clearly and plan your future.

## At the moment we just can’t afford a wedding. What shall we do?

In the old days it was the bride’s father who paid for the wedding. Then, over the years, it became the fathers of both families who paid, and now it seems to be the couple themselves who pay! The reason for this seems to be the ever-increasing costs. It is now a major obstacle for young couples but seems to have more to do with changing fashions, greedy marketing and unscrupulous venue owners than it does the actual cost of a traditional wedding.

All that is really required for a wedding is two families, one priest, some friends, a fire and some ghee , a few flowers and fruits, and a few additional items of negligible cost. Ancient scripture has nothing to say about expensive designer bridal gowns, hotel hire for eight hundred guests, an entertaining DJ, a video cameraman, a river of alcohol and a honeymoon in the Maldives. Pundits are still available who will conduct a ceremony for you without it costing a small fortune.

## What does a traditional wedding consist of?

The essence of a wedding ceremony is the kanya-dan , the father giving his daughter to a young man. Before and after this there are various ceremonies. The rituals vary according to different regions of India and local traditions or family customs, but they are all based around the following pattern:

1\. Reception of the groom. 2. Entrance of the bride. 3. Bride declares willingness to proceed by garlanding the groom. 4. Priest utters a sankalpa – a declaration – of who is to be married to whom. 5. Worship of Vishnu, along with family deities. 6. Father places his daughter’s hand in the groom’s hand, then pours water, thereby giving her as a bride. 7. The couple exchange their vows. 8. Their upper clothes are tied together. 9. Bride, bride’s parents, and groom offer grains into the sacred fire. 10. The couple circle the sacred fire, taking seven steps along with promises to each other. 11. The groom gives the bride a necklace known as a mangala-sutra and marks her hair-parting with red kum-kum powder. 12. Family, friends and guests offer congratulations. 13. The newly married couple take blessings from their parents. 14. Married ladies come to offer blessings to the couple. 15. The couple feed each other for the first time.

## How long does all that take?

This type of wedding can be completed in under two hours, or even much less. It may sound lengthy, but the time passes quite quickly, and it is so enjoyable and uplifting that all feel blessed simply to watch it. It is worth remembering that weddings such as these are an essential feature of sanatan dharma , a cultural tradition of many thousands of years. By becoming married in this way, the couple not only collect many blessings with which to begin their life, they also perpetuate the oldest culture on Earth and help safeguard it for the future.

## Do we still need a legal wedding? After all, it’s only a piece of paper.

The marriage certificate is a piece of paper, that’s true, but it is also an extremely important document. Your passport is also a piece of paper, as is your driving licence. So too are your university degrees, the deeds to your home, your job contract and your mortgage agreement. Indeed, every single important promise that you’ve made to anyone can be reduced to a ‘piece of paper,’ but such papers are symbols of the important promises you’ve made. It’s the promises you’ve made that are important.

The document and your signature give witness to your promise. In the case of a civil wedding ceremony, you are given a legal document to show that you’ve been married according to the laws of the country. This is an important protection for both husband and wife, and it stands as yet one more set of vows that will serve to keep you together happily throughout life. It is also an important document that your great-great grandchildren will cherish many years in the future.

## Why do we need both a religious wedding and a legal ceremony?

The civil ceremony marries you in the eyes of the law and you are then registered with the government as a married couple. The religious wedding marries you in the eyes of God. Both are helpful on the journey of life. If you have a religious wedding you are also required to be legally married, and sometimes this can be facilitated within the ceremony itself. And if you have a civil ceremony you should also plan some sort of religious wedding. Why not have both? You’ll find that both are important, but for different reasons.

## Stages in a marriage

## I’ve just got married, what should I look out for?

In the first year, a marriage is like a newly born baby. It’s a delicate thing which needs careful attention and tender, loving care. Couples can be surprised and distressed at early problems. Little things become annoying. You are now sharing everything, the same spaces, the bathroom, the same conversation, the same food. The infatuation, excitement and romance of life before marriage is wearing off, and it’s becoming apparent that ‘marital bliss’ is going to take a little more work. Occasionally, this results in someone feeling disillusioned, disappointed, angry, or gives rise to feelings of panic or suffocation. The young husband or wife thinks: “Have I made a mistake?” As a result of these feelings, many marriages do not last eighteen months.

## What would you suggest for the first year?

Thank you for asking - here are some suggestions for the early days:

Firstly, expect to make an effort . You are building foundations. Listen and try to understand. Keep calm, don’t shout. Any effort you make is an investment that will pay off richly later on. The amount of effort required to prevent problems is much less than the effort needed to cure them later.

Second, be prepared for difficulties . Some degree of confrontation and even conflict is inevitable at this stage. Marriage is like a ballroom dance. One person moves forward, and the partner moves back. It is a learning process and can be painful and sometimes humiliating. The real test is not the difficulties but how you handle them. Here are some more:

## Take Time and Make Time

Give each other time. Getting used to each other in a marriage and learning how to relate as newly-weds takes time. Don’t try to rush things. It’s a bit like two ships coming together in the sea and then docking – slowly. Make time when you can come together and talk – not when either one of you – or both of you – are exhausted.

## Tread Gently

Creating intimacy is best achieved by gentleness, not by force. Try to see the world through your partner’s eyes. Be aware of their hurts or unfulfilled needs. Be gentle when approaching those areas, or issues. Find the gentlest form of words to make your point. It is always easy to raise the voice in order to make a point, but much more can be accomplished by gentleness.

## Learn to Communicate

Divorce is accompanied by the cries of ‘You never listened to me!’ and ‘If only you knew how I felt!’ We communicate through tone and body language. We must try to enter the world of emotions, not just words. Don’t assume you understand each other just because you speak the same language. Listening is best done with ears and eyes. Be honest when you don’t understand. Apologise when you misunderstand and try to see how it occurred.

## Learn to Love

In the first year of marriage craving love is strongest but you can strengthen caring love through kindness and understanding; companionship love through sharing interests and doing things together; and commitment love by reminding yourself of your vows. Have your first wedding anniversary with friends and celebrate your commitments to each other.

## We’ve been married three years now, and things have definitely changed. Did I make a mistake?

Not at all! The only thing that has happened is that you’ve been married for three years – and nothing ever stays the same. Remember that you need to make an effort at marriage. You’ll need to be a bit of a scientist. The law of gravity states that things fall instead of rise, and the law of entropy states that things become degraded as time goes by. If you don’t want things to fall down, you’ll have to keep them supported, and if you don’t want things to become more disorganised and chaotic, you’ll need to provide ongoing organisation. The same goes for marriage. If you don’t work at it, it will fail. Just as Lord Krishna comes to re-establish dharma, similarly we must make efforts to restore dharma and proper working order in our marriage. Almost constant vigilance is required to stop a marriage breaking down.

## That sounds a bit daunting. Is there an easy way to do it?

It may sound daunting, but everything in your life with moving parts requires maintenance – why should your marriage be any different? Think of your car. You’d never forget to put in oil, water and fuel, would you? What about checking the air in your tyres? If you forgot any of these essential things your car wouldn’t work. It would still look like a car, but not function as one.

## Treat my marriage like my car? Is there a maintenance checklist?

Here are some practical suggestions: (a) Bring your marriage into your decision making (b) Take positive action to keep your marriage as your main priority: make appointments together in your diaries and keep them. Identify the main challenge to your marriage and make a stand.

(c) Seek to handle pressures so they bind you, rather than part you. Build an arch so that pressure binds it! (d) Celebrate your achievements in marriage. Keep photo albums and anniversaries. (e) Don’t allow for the possibility of failure. (f) Keep your relationship fresh through surprises, gifts, trips out. (g) Arrange ‘dates’ every two weeks and take each other somewhere new. (h) Join together with other happily married couples for social events. (i) Keep the relationship dynamic by having spiritually minded friends.

## We do a lot of that already, but how do we adjust when unexpected things crop up?

Look for hazards on the road ahead and plan for them because these will pose threats to marital happiness and growth. The biggest changes can cause the most stress, whether they are happy changes or sad ones. Most couples struggle with the big changes that come with having a baby or a second baby, for instance. Then there’s unexpected loss of earnings if your job changes, increased costs, sickness, changed social routine, teenagers and middle age. Your marriage must be strong enough to survive all of these, so you’ll need to plan for them, so you’re not too surprised when they come. You might call this ‘planning for the negatives.’ But don’t worry, they don’t come all at once!

## Hmm…that all sounds a bit negative. Do you have any good news?

I’m sorry to depress you! Yes, most of married life is good news, but planning ahead will make sure you have even more good news. In addition to planning for challenges, you should do positive planning, too. You can both set yourself goals, and have a mission statement for yourselves individually, but also for your marriage. What will you do together? See yourselves accomplishing things as a couple. Visualise it. Make it real, then work towards it, step by step. Take small steps towards your goal by arranging smaller goals and dates to reach them by. Reward yourself for any accomplishments. Encourage and support each other in your respective, individual goals. Celebrate each other’s achievements.

## Our communication hasn’t been so good recently, I feel we don’t connect.

Despite lots of different ways we can communicate these days, we don’t seem to do it any better. The best advice for a happy marriage is to make good and effective communication a priority. Spend more time in conversation without the distractions of a screen in the room – or in your hand! Always be courteous and tender, and always speak in a low volume at the beginning of a conversation. Remember that nothing discourages conversation as much as a harsh response. Learn to express yourself, and your feelings, in an effective way. Men (especially) you need to listen – with your mouth shut! Please don’t interrupt! Be supportive – validate and encourage your spouse in conversation.

## Sometimes we just have nothing to talk about. And when we talk, we argue. What should we do?

There are four threats to genuine communication. You can look for these and try your best to eliminate them. That will be a good start:

The Plateau Effect – when we’re learning a language, we tend to learn just enough to get by. It’s often like that in a relationship after some time. We speak to our partner in short phrases rather than whole sentences. Go deeper! Ask more questions!

Creeping Silences – We often hurry to get ready for work in the mornings, and don’t speak very much; then at the end of the day we’re tired, and again we don’t speak much. These routines invite silences into the home, which then gradually erode the ability to share thoughts. You’ll both need to encourage discussion by asking questions and listening to the answer.

Unexploded Bombs – This is another type of silence but more deliberate. There are difficult issues you both need to discuss but you procrastinate because you fear expressing yourself inadequately, or you want to avoid an argument. So, you just don’t talk about it. The solution is to acknowledge that it’s a difficult issue but that the issue needs to be talked about.

Increasing Deafness – This is a reluctance to listen to your partner, or only pretending to listen. Either of them is a conversation-killer and therefore dangerous for a healthy relationship. You need to listen, however difficult it may be, and then you need to speak carefully with understanding. Imagine your partner to be a mirror that reflects back to you some uncomfortable truths about yourself. Treat what they say as something that will help you in the long term.

## Is so much time really required in just maintaining a marriage?

Yes, it is. You need to make time for marriage, and for all you both want to do. Just as you made time to be with each other in the beginning, it is still important later, and especially when you become busy. You should spend time together every day and do something special together every two weeks at least. You’ll also need to make dedicated and uninterrupted time to have more risky discussions.

Then there’s time you’ll need for planning. These times don’t have to be romantic exchanges, they can be business meetings for financial planning, house improvements, career moves and holidays. The most difficult time to schedule is for problem management sessions. They are the hardest type, but they must be tackled. In all that you do, think longterm. We live in an instant, micro-wave society, with all its urgency, but our ancestors thought in terms of decades to create buildings and societies. Similarly, you’ll need to learn to think in five-year periods. Plan for later life and then live your life towards that.

## Everything you say is fine, but sometimes we just don’t understand each other. What can we do?

Everything can be learned over time, and so you can also learn to understand each other. Although in our post-modern world we like to say that men and women are the same, they are different in many ways. Knowing the differences and being able to respond appropriately will help you. For instance:

1\. Women’s adolescence finishes at 18, men’s at 30.

2\. Men are single-tasking, women can often multi-task with focus.

3\. Men and women have different attitudes to marriage. Women think their husband will change (but he doesn’t) and the husband thinks the wife won’t change (but she does).

4\. When out shopping, she knows exactly what to buy, and where to get it, but he doesn’t have a clue.

5\. She knows all about the children, but he can’t imagine what they need.

6\. Men moan more when they get sick.

## What about forgiveness? If I do something wrong, they always remind me about it.

The best practice is to admit your mistakes and to forgive theirs. When you do something wrong you can try to hide it or blame someone else, but the best thing is to admit it! The difficulty is that admission of guilt may open you up to rebuke and diminish your authority - but without it you cannot resolve matters. People only admit when they are wrong where there is a possibility of forgiveness, so for admission of mistakes to be possible in a marriage (and it is important) the couple must be equally willing to forgive each other. During the Vedic wedding ceremony there is a warning given to the groom to always be prepared to forgive his wife. Therefore, we say that marriage means to be repeatedly giving - and forgiving. Once you’ve admitted a mistake – and been forgiven – you can move forwards. And its best if neither of you bring up the other’s past mistakes ever again. Once forgiven, it should be forgotten.

## When we got married, we were compatible, now we’ve both changed as people. How do we deal with that?

You must beware of complacency. The deadly enemy of every marriage is taking each other for granted. Check that nothing has quietly crept in to alienate your partner. Work at appreciating them. Give an unexpected gift at an unexpected time. When dealing with the inevitable changes, you must be philosophical. The person you married is still the same person. By loving them in a different way you’ll discover a deeper version of them. Not everything is on the outside.

Above all, stay real. Things change and you need to adjust – don’t pretend that it’s the same as before. When children are born, when you change your job, when you move to a new house, suffer a bereavement, have a period of illness, have a mid-life crisis - all these events change things. You’ll need to re-evaluate every time and come up with positive responses to changing needs. Some of those changes will be financial, but some emotional. Treat your marriage like your body, do a healthcheck occasionally, and see what needs some attention, medicine or exercise.

## You say ‘love them in a different way’ – what do you mean?

Do you remember we talked about the four ways of loving someone? All four of them are relevant at every stage of marriage, and you’ll need to love in every way you can in order to keep your marriage a happy one. ‘Craving love,’ although mostly strong in youth, is still relevant later. Adultery is common enough that partners need to protect their marriage against it. In situations where your mind is pulled to someone attractive in a social setting, you can refocus your thoughts on your husband or wife. Resist or reject thoughts of others, otherwise you will create a fantasy and compare your partner unfavourably to that. In fact, fantasies are generally destructive, giving us an entirely unrealistic view on any person or situation.

Even though they are married, and even though they are surrounded by married friends, a couple still must work at appearing attractive to each other. There are two extreme views: one is that physical looks are everything, and the other is that looks don’t count at all. Both are wrong. You need to keep the attraction alive, without becoming caught up in the illusion it creates. The answer to that conundrum is just one word: balance!

## You mentioned other types of love?

Yes, companionship love includes sharing each other’s hobbies and interests if possible but leaving them alone if they need space. It also means facing life’s challenges and disappointments and being a support for each other at those times. When you have fun together, and go through trials together, it strengthens all the bonds between you.

Caring love means doing for your partner the things they like, and refraining from doing other things they don’t like, such as making them the object of jokes, or criticising them in public.

Commitment love involves making an act of will against any feelings of anger, hurt, disgust or disappointment. This type of love must not fail because all the others depend on it. The key to remaining committed is to regularly remind yourself what, and who, you are committed to. Our society has produced people who know all about the biology of a relationship but not about the commitment. They know about the engine but not how to drive the car.

## What about social life?

There is a social dimension to marriage. When you become married you create a new social unit. You have broken free of the past, and all the ways you behaved when you were single. There are new rules now.

You are part of a new society – filled with other married people. Other married couples can be a great source of strength and support for you, and in turn you will help them. You can learn from each other. You can choose other couples who’ll bring out the best in you. So, here are a few suggestions how to live in this new society: Do not say to others anything you could not say with your spouse there. If you still have friends and social activities from when you were single, you must examine them to see whether these now help the marriage. Defend each other in public – it provides a united front and will avoid dangerous conversations like: ‘My wife/husband doesn’t understand me…’ which leads to cracks in any marriage.

There’s social life inside the home, too, with all the social dynamics that happen when people live with each other. You’ll need to create new patterns. For instance, who does what in the house? Who does the dishes, the weekly shopping, the laundry, the cooking or the dustbins? Do you have ‘traditional’ or ‘modern’ roles? You can both start new traditions of the way “we” do things, as opposed to the way ‘I’ individually did things before marriage.

Finally, remember that children impact every aspect of your marriage, and will be a dynamic part of the micro-society you’ve created in your home.

## How can we avoid conflict over money?

Even minor issues around money can produce low-level stress, and a major money problem can cause any number of explosive rows. Advice:

1\. Be cautious about expenditure. Three things are to be got rid of as soon as you can – fire, disease and debt.

2\. Money isn’t happiness – but neither is poverty. As a married couple you need wealth and need to plan for that wealth.

3\. Earn honestly.

4\. Keep track of your money.

5\. Budget for expenses. Try to plan for unforeseen expenses like illness, education, housing, a pension and remember to save money.

6\. Don’t save on some things and spend lavishly on others. Examine where your money is going.

7\. Don’t buy big things on impulse – read the small print.

8\. Be wary of ambition. Your family should not suffer. Your treatment of money is a test of character, both how you make it and how you spend it.

9\. If you do have financial problems, don’t blame each other.

10\. Don’t get involved in any dubious financial schemes.

11\. Give in charity until it hurts – but give sensibly, to deserving causes.

## Spirituality and marriage

## You seem to have a lot of advice about marriage, where does it all come from?

The Vedic tradition, otherwise known as Sanatan Dharma , is many thousands of years old and survives to this day. Millions of marriages have been blessed by the wisdom contained in the Vedas. You see, just because a culture is old, it doesn’t mean it becomes useless. In fact, the wisdom of a tradition is more useful when it’s old precisely because it’s been tried and tested over many years. Whatever has proved itself to be useful will have survived, and whatever was not useful will have been left behind. The fact that people still refer to the Vedas for life guidance means that the teachings have remained relevant.

## But the world has changed since the Vedas were written. How can they still be relevant?

Because even though much has changed, people are mostly the same. Technology may be increased, but human problems remain fundamentally the same. Science has improved many areas of life, but also created new anxieties. We now know a lot more about the world, but we still need help with the basics of human relationships. The Vedas mainly deal with the things that don’t change – the things that are of permanent value - and they have a lot to say about the human spirit, and how we can be happy.

## But I thought the Vedas were mainly prayers and ceremonies?

Veda simply means knowledge – any kind of knowledge. Although part of the Vedas is prayers and ceremonies, there are corollary books known as Upavedas that deal with very practical issues such as medicine, mathematics, architecture, town-planning, politics – and how to have a great marriage. The Grihya Sutras explain how husbands and wives can earn a livelihood, create a home, raise a family and prosper in health, play their part in greater society and achieve personal happiness and spiritual progress.

## Where does the Krishna movement fit in?

The Hare Krishna movement is part of the Vedic tradition. The philosophical portion of the Vedas is known as Vedanta – the ‘end of knowledge’ – and is comprised of texts known as the Upanishads. The Bhagavad-gita contains much wisdom from the Upanishads. The great original guru, whose name was Srila Vyasadeva, wrote a compilation of the Upanishads known as Vedanta Sutra , and then wrote a commentary on that known as the Bhagavata Purana or Srimad Bhagavatam . The Hare Krishna movement is based mainly on the Bhagavad-gita and Srimad Bhagavatam.

## How does it all help with my marriage?

In surprisingly direct and practical ways. Three sources give different but complementary teachings for life balance, self-awakening, and deepdown satisfaction. The first is the Grihya Sutras. These texts explain the dynamics between a husband and wife, the need for psychological balance, their suggested respective roles within the home, their relationships with relatives and friends, and with society generally.

Second is the Upanishads. They explain that our outer, temporary identity is the body, and the inner identity is the conscious self. The body is the vehicle and the self the passenger. Of those two the inner self is more important because it is the conscious observer and therefore the real person. Any relationship which acknowledges this self and its needs will be a happier one. The third element of wisdom is that there is a Universal Self, of whom we are a tiny part. When we see ourselves – and our primary relationships – as being related to this eternal source, we can experience a joy that will relieve us of all anxieties and worries.

## Why is Krishna important?

The speaker of the Bhagavad-gita is Krishna; and the subject of the 18,000 verses of the Srimad Bhagavatam is Krishna. From both these sources it can be concluded that Krishna is the Universal Self of the philosopher, the goal of mystic yoga, and the God of religion. The purpose of life is to awaken the inner self to the reality of Krishna, to experience Him directly, and to serve and love Him. The effect this can bring to a marriage is quite revolutionary, and when both husband and wife serve Krishna together, their marriage becomes not only unbreakable, but successful and joyful.

## Do you have to get married in the temple to have a Krishna marriage?

Not at all. You may have your marriage ceremony wherever you like. To have a ‘Krishna marriage’ you just have to invite Krishna into your marriage. To do that you simply follow the advice given by the Lord in His Gita: “Always think of me, offer me your respects to me and worship me. Absorbed in me, you will surely come to me.”

## How does that work in practice?

When both husband and wife understand they are connected to Krishna, then they look at each other in a different way. They don’t regard each other as property, but as a gift from Krishna, as someone to be served, and as a fellow devotee of Krishna. One recently married follower asked our spiritual master, Srila Prabhupada, what her relationship to her husband should be. He replied, “Just as the right hand has a relationship with the left hand through the body, so you have a relationship with your husband through Krishna, with Krishna in the centre.” In this way, Srila Prabhupada explained, the right and left hands are like the husband and wife, and the body is like Krishna. They are connected to each other, but through their connection with Krishna.

## You say the home is a sacred space. Can you say something more about that?

The goal of life is to reawaken our relationship with God. When both husband and wife make this their joint goal, their marriage and their home become a sacred ashram , a place of spiritual dedication. Spiritual practice is the best way to focus our perspective on the eternal, and to derive the greatest inner happiness. With such happiness within, any conflicts or problems in the relationship will be minimised. It will give us extra strength in all ways. Circumstances will be there which will tempt us to snap, become angry, collapse in despair, or walk out. But our spiritual life will give us the strength to cope.

In an Indian wedding, when the aunties tie the red and white upper cloths together, they create a knot. This knot represents Krishna, the balancing principle within the marriage. Krishna is a very real presence, and married people can derive great benefit in their lives by remembering Him each day.

This can be done by something known as sadhana , or ‘the means to reach the goal.’ The details of this are described in other books, but in general they are as follows:

Early to bed, early to rise – create some time in the morning for meditation.

Meditate – mantra meditation is a powerful and uplifting experience. Explore the tradition – studying the ancient texts will satisfy your mind.

Create sacred space – an altar will be a focal point for your home. Practice compassion – choose your foods with non-violence in mind. Invite the sadhus – sharing your space and food with spiritual people. Volunteer – find your local temple and get involved by offering service.

Festivals - celebrate the days of joy in the ancient calendar.

## I like Krishna, and the teachings. But what does it mean to be a devotee of Krishna?

To be devoted to anyone means to be attracted to them, to be inspired by them, to think of them a lot and to be dedicated to them. To be a devotee of Krishna simply means to try to think of Krishna always, offer your respects to Him, and place Him in the centre of your life – even while being engaged in all your work and family responsibilities. Being a devotee means to accept Krishna as your guardian and protector, and to offer Him worship and some practical service. It means keeping the image of Krishna in your home, having your food blessed by first offering your meals to Krishna, and spending some time each morning reciting the holy names of Krishna, in the form of mantra meditation. If you do these things, you’ll be a devotee of Krishna.

## What else do married couples have to do?

When the mood of selfless service has been awakened, it spills out into serving others. Married couples serve others in their neighbourhood, community and society. Once, a newly married man asked Srila Prabhupada for advice on what was the most important element of married life. His guru told him: ‘Every day, before you and your wife eat your dinner, go to the door and call out three times: ‘Is anyone hungry? Please come and eat!’ When the young man felt that perhaps his guru had not understood the question, he waited a few minutes and asked again. The reply was the same.

Householders, known as grihastas , should try to serve food to others. Even more, they should try to serve the sadhus by inviting them to their home. Although calling out into the street is more practicable for India and might be regarded as slightly odd in other countries, the principle remains the same. There are many alternative arrangements that can be made to distribute cooked food or provisions. Practising generosity is a positive principle and will help make the home a sacred space. By sharing your meals with others, you will both become happy.

## Don’t the devotees of Krishna follow all kinds of rules? I don’t like rules.

There are devotees of all sorts, from new beginners to very dedicated people - and everyone in between. What they have in common is that they’ve all adjusted their life in some way so they can remember Krishna better and more often. You can call these adjustments ‘rules’ if you like, but they are all rules that the devotees have chosen for themselves. Rules can only be followed voluntarily, never by force or social pressure. You say you don’t like rules, well, no-one likes rules made for them by other people. But when you choose to follow your own personal rules - because they help you to be healthier, richer, better educated or just a better person, that’s different. You’ve chosen a goal, and you work towards your goal following the rules that will get you there. Following rules as a devotee of Krishna is like that. You choose how spiritually committed you’d like to be, and then choose which rules you follow.

In the beginning, for instance, we find that most devotees choose to become vegetarians. They like the idea of not eating any animal food. They don’t really find it a problem. Later, they may choose to make their meals sacred by offering everything they eat to Krishna. It then becomes prasada . Although you could describe this as a rule, it’s a rule that’s quite easy because prasada tastes so good. When a person becomes committed, they choose a certain number of names of Krishna to recite every morning. The Hare Krishna mantra is chanted in what is known as harinam-japa . Reciting the mantra on a circle of 108 wooden beads completes one set of recitations known as a mala or ‘round.’ Many devotees chant one, two, four or eight of these ‘rounds’ each day, and dedicated devotees chant sixteen. In order to improve the quality of their devotion to Krishna, dedicated devotees will also abstain from any impure or unhealthy foods, such as meat, fish or eggs, and any intoxication. They’ll avoid gambling, too. In addition, dedicated married couples also undertake to use their sexual energy only when they wish to conceive a child.

## Sex only for conception? Is that even possible?

It’s possible, but perhaps not in the beginning. It requires spiritual maturity, meaning it’s only possible when both husband and wife jointly decide they will aim for a high spiritual goal in their relationship. When a married couple live like that, it’s a form of yoga . In the Srimad Bhagavatam , Lord Krishna describes this to His friend Uddhava, so for many centuries it has been a recognised element of a spiritually progressive marriage. In turn, the founder-acharya of the Hare Krishna movement, Srila Prabhupada, passed the instruction on to his disciples. But again, it is only practicable if the couple are convinced of the reasons for it, the goals they want to achieve, and if they both have sufficient determination to make the commitment.

## Well, I don’t think it’s for us. We didn’t get married just to be celibate.

Understood. It’s for a minority that wish to attempt it, not for everyone. You can still be a devotee of Krishna without following that rule. But it’s an option for you to consider, and one that may make more sense to you as your life progresses. Marriage is not about complete celibacy, of course, but you might like to consider the health benefits of temporary celibacy. The ancient Ayurveda describes that it contributes to core strength, a sharp mind, excellent memory, and that it boosts chances of conception while helping to produce healthier children. Too much sex debilitates a man and serves to reduce the attraction between husband and wife. So, following a regulated pattern of sexual activity, and dedicating that important energy to the act of conception, can be a healthy and spiritual boost for a marriage.

## Conception, pregnancy and raising children

## Is it compulsory to have children?

It’s not compulsory, and no-one can compel you to have children if you both don’t want to. However, the falling birth rate should make us all consider our contribution to the future of the larger community. The trend for having no children, or for having only one child, is a growing one, and there will be alarming consequences soon if the pattern does not change.

## Why are many couples deciding not to have children?

You might say ‘market forces’ have played a role in this trend, in that both husband and wife must now work to maintain one home, particularly in the city, and that’s not easy with children to care for. We could also say ‘social trends,’ because the pattern of life for a woman now does not automatically include motherhood. It is regarded by some as ‘liberating’ for a woman not to have children. She can concentrate instead on her career. But there are often regrets over those choices, generally in later life. Taking everything into consideration, it is still more natural and life-enhancing for a couple to have children and raise a family.

## Why do you say that having children is ‘more natural?’

The bodies of male and female are designed for reproduction, both physically and psychologically. Our bodies are amazing biological machines that have countless inbuilt functions, and a large part of the healthy functioning is to create the next generation. There may be many man-made reasons why we consider it to be more troublesome, or less convenient, to have children, but ultimately, we should not fight what is natural.

## I thought that God prefers people with no children – the sadhus and swamis ?

He likes everyone! Don’t forget that His best friend Arjuna was a married man. It’s true there are a minority who give up the idea of marriage and family so they can dedicate more time and energy to the spiritual path. We honour their sacrifice and we’re grateful for their leadership. But if you choose the path of marriage then you mustn’t spend your time wishing you weren’t married. Try to follow everything that God has ordained for your path. You cannot borrow from someone else’s path or choose only the things you like to make up your own path. The path of the grihasta – the married person - includes having children. If you are a celibate student or a swami , then your path is to have no children. But marriage is a different path. Each can have the best way of life when they follow a path ordained by God.

## You cannot be a sadhu and be married?

The word sadhu means a good person, and of course people can, and should, be good when they’re married. Married life is not a licence for unlimited sexual indulgence, and many couples periodically abstain from sex in order to dedicate themselves to their spiritual path. But total celibacy in married life is not recommended by the scriptures, and neither is it possible for most. If you desire to live a simple, more spiritual life, and you want celibacy to be a part of that life, then better to stay single. Don’t make your life unnecessarily complicated. Just as butter cannot be placed near fire without melting, so men and women cannot be close together without one of them ‘melting.’ If men and women want to enjoy each other’s company that is possible within sacred marriage, and in marriage the purpose is having and raising children.

## What is being a parent like if you’re a devotee of Krishna?

Just as marriage is a relationship for mutually helping each other on the spiritual path, becoming parents gives an opportunity to invite more souls into the relationship, and to then help them along their own spiritual path. It is a special gift and privilege to help them in this way. The Vedas teach us that an individual soul is attracted to certain parents, so if you plan to be the caretakers, providers and nurturers of those special souls you can look forward to having a special child. Your child may be already a spiritually advanced soul, and this birth may be their last.

## How many children is a good number?

How many can you care for? The Vedic wedding blessing is for eleven children, and just a few years ago there were still many couples who would have at least five or six. Times have changed, especially with city living, and that may not practicable for you. But the old wedding blessing remains: “May you be fertile and give birth to many happy children.” Children are indeed our future – the next generation – so you should have as many as you are able to care for physically, emotionally and spiritually.

## How do we conceive a child – I hear there are some special things we can do?

Two things are important: for both parents to be in the mood of inviting a soul to take shelter with them, and to ask the Lord to graciously bless them with a child. Traditionally, there is a selection of prayers and a small ceremony known as Garbhadhana Samskara . In addition to these spiritual elements, there are also some other practical aspects such as choosing the right time for conception, both astrologically and biologically.

## We are having difficulty conceiving. What is your advice?

There are many cases where a couple conceive almost effortlessly, even without planning, and there are also many couples who desperately want a child but are unsuccessful. The first thing to remember is that patience is required when conception is not immediate, or even after many attempts. Whether it is a belief or a science, the Ayur Veda prescribes careful calendar planning in order to ascertain the right day for conception. Adjustment of diet, with separate types of foods for husband and wife, is also recommended. There are also herbal remedies which can create a better environment for fertilisation. If all the natural means have failed, it is quite acceptable to explore other means offered by medical specialists.

## Should we adopt children if we cannot conceive?

This could be a positive option. If you can help a child have a better opportunity in this life, then that will be a special intervention for them and a blessing for you both. It would be a very charitable and compassionate thing to do – but also a great responsibility.

## I am pregnant – what is your advice?

The baby in the womb can hear from sixteen weeks, so lots of chanting and musical kirtan is great for the mother’s connection to her baby and for the baby’s spiritual development. And because the growing child is nourished entirely from what types of food the mother eats, lots of Krishna prasadam is also recommended. Good amounts of the right type of exercise and healthy nutrition is also suggested. The mother should avoid foods that are too salty, pungent, acidic or spicy. Six weeks or one month before delivery any unusual exertion should be avoided, including energetic climbing of stairs or riding in a jolting vehicle. There is another small ceremony for safe delivery and blessings – the Simanta Nayanam Samskara .

## I am confused about raising children in today’s world.

Give your children the most guidance and knowledge when they are young. You’ll equip them with a spiritual suit of armour for life. Ensure that spiritual practice is done in a joyful and not a forced way – with music and song, drama, art, dance and stories. Make sure that you model spiritual practice for them and that there is no discord between what you preach and what you practice as children are very observant and it creates doubt in their mind. Another important recommendation is to limit electrical devices on very young children – such as screens and mobile phones - as it hinders optimum brain development.

## What about schooling?

As much as possible, the education your child receives should be in line with your highest values. Schools tend to opt for a common denominator of morality, and you’ll have to check if everything you’d want to give your child has been provided by their teachers. If there is a school that is in line with your spiritual values that is always the best: good strong academics combined with excellent moral and spiritual guidance. If you don’t live near such a school then you may provide their spiritual education through Sunday schools at your local temple, nearby sanga group gatherings or in the home. You may also choose to home-school your child and connect to a home-schooling network. It has been shown that a child who receives the same messages at home and at school prospers and remains balanced in outlook.

## How can I ensure that my children remain in Krishna consciousness when I have relatives who are quite materialistic?

You love your children and your relatives love them too. The difference is between the values you hold and your relatives. You may be vegetarian, and you’ve brought your child up that way, too. But on visits to aunties and uncles you may not be able to guarantee your child won’t be served meat. You can discuss your concerns with your relatives – who may naturally disagree with you – and you can discuss it with your child. You cannot tell them “no” without providing them with good reasons. Your children are clever and can understand things that are explained to them. Just keep a dialogue with your children and they will appreciate it. Of course, if relatives give love and support, and they want to help nurture your children in a way that you suggest, despite not agreeing with all your ideas, that is wonderful.

## My children were little devotees once but have now rejected it. What should I do - keep trying or back off?

This is a common question from parents. You don’t want to force them, or create any resentment, and you hope they’ll come back to Krishna when they’re older. What should you do for the time being? When children get to a certain age, they have to make the spiritual journey in their own way and in their own time and develop their personal faith independently of what they are being told, by questioning, challenging and sometimes rebelling. They need to come to their own conclusions and realisations through their life experiences. Some will simply hear and understand, and others may have to dip their feet into the material energy to come to their own personal understanding.

It’s best if you give them the space to do that, but also stay firm and fixed in what you are doing and remain loving towards them. Some things take time and during the life of an adolescent with academic work, hormones and peer pressure, spirituality may not be on the top of their priority list. However, give it some time and it will come. Another thing worth mentioning is that children also need different styles and ages of role models and leaders as they grow. Although they’ll always love you as parents, they will begin to look to others for answers to their questions about life. The person who inspires a child of eight may not inspire them by the time they’re twelve.

## What do children practically need to become more spiritually aware?

They need to be given many more opportunities to come together with others their age and engage in fun activities with a spiritual focus. It is possible to introduce a spiritual element in every game or fun activity. Sometimes just good wholesome activities like treasure hunts, cooperative games, physical challenges, debates, drama plays and singing choirs - all of these can be inspiring and fun for them. The fun element is essential for children. It is the company of others that is crucial in making spirituality for children a reality.

## My child was raised a vegetarian but, due to outside influences, has now decided to eat meat. I don’t want to alienate him, what should I do?

Many children and adults are becoming vegan and vegetarian at present. It is not at all unfashionable or unusual. Your child may have made this decision as a demonstration of independence. Of course, falling in with the choices of others may help them to feel they belong to a group, but it won’t develop their inner strength and their own sense of identity. The best thing is for your child to understand all the arguments and reasons why anyone becomes a vegetarian. If they know that or see videos of animals being mistreated, chemicals added to meat products, or other leading celebrities who are promoting vegetarianism, this will go further to convince them than you could. Help them to see that being a vegetarian means making a stand, not following the crowd. It may then be more appealing to them. The other element, of course, is that vegetarian food tastes better than meat, and they may simply need to experience tastier cooking.

## I am not used to the idea of having a teacher or guide, but should I adopt someone for this new stage of life?

At every stage of life, whether as a newly married person or a new parent, there’s always someone who’s been through it before you. It’s important that when challenges and questions arise, as they most certainly will, that you take help from them. They’ve already ‘walked the path’ and it will be very beneficial for you to learn from them. So why not ‘adopt’ them? They’ll be able to share with you their experiences and realisations and relieve your worries.

Just one piece of advice, one tip or trick, will be enough to really help you. At the very least, you won’t feel that you must face this new situation in life alone. If you don’t feel comfortable depending on one person for everything, have three of four ‘guides’ to answer your questions. The more the better. You could also join a group. There are many people in the same circumstances as you: newly-weds, new mums and toddlers, parents of teenagers – and quite often they’ve already started a group you can join. You’ll find the knowledge from the other members very helpful. If you don’t immediately find the right group for you, keep looking until you find a group you feel comfortable in. Occasionally, you may have to organise a group yourself – and then join it!

## How can I really care for myself, all through marriage, raising children, in my spiritual practice and temple involvement as well?

It’s essential to find a work/home/life/spiritual balance. You must think of all your roles and try to ensure that you invest in all of them. A good exercise is to write a list of your roles, and goals within those roles, and see what you’ll need to do to be them/reach them. A lot can be done by combining activities, and by eliminating any time-wasting. In terms of raising a family or spending time with your spouse, you should understand that this is also part of your service to Krishna, not something separate.

Srila Prabhupada made many statements as to how important it is to give our children a good start in life. And for children to have a strong mental and emotional foundation in their future they also need to see you and your partner modelling a loving, sound and caring relationship. So, all elements in your life are important and need to be balanced, not separated or sacrificed.

## I have heard that some families do the songs and arati – how do they keep the kids involved?

For all the family to come together in the home for a spiritual programme is a wonderful thing. Spirituality in the home is a precious time and is very healthy and enlivening for children. They retain the effect of it throughout their lives. Most families would not try to reproduce the temple schedule though, they’d perhaps set aside 15 or 20 minutes. This is short enough that children can take part and enjoy it but a worthwhile addition to the day. You must see what is manageable for your family with work and school commitments.

They say that a family that prays together stays together, so learning songs, chanting japa, kirtan and some stories and philosophy that’s on their level is fun for them. Parents can draw their own interpretation and inspiration from any stories from scripture, too. The main thing is that whatever you do must be relevant to the children in their lives, otherwise they will see it as an artificial imposition rather than an integrated aspect of their life and understanding.

## Defending your marriage

## A couple we’ve known for a long time has just got divorced. Why does this happen so much?

A marriage is like a house you both build together. First the foundations, then the roof and rooms, then the decorations – in that order. Over the years, thousands of small problems can build up and cause stress. Fire can destroy a house very quickly, but woodworm – just thousands of tiny bites – can do it over the years. Therefore, minor problems should be dealt with. These tiny bites often come in the form of unkind words, unhelpful comments, criticisms and complaints, and repeated conflict situations. Conflict means that one partner proposes something, the other objects, an argument ensues. It doesn’t have to be a flaming row, just enough for it to be one more tiny bite.

## But aren’t disagreements and arguments all part of married life?

Yes, they are. Conflict is a part of how we get to understand each other in any relationship. So, we should never imagine that the relationship is over because there’s been an argument. But it’s how we recover from arguments, what we learn from them, and how we try to be more understanding of each other the next time round that matters. We shouldn’t keep having the same argument every time, and the arguments should be shorter and softer each time, too. It is better to understand how conflict situations develop, how to avoid them, but also how to prepare for them anyway. The couple that doesn’t learn from their last argument will have a bit more resentment in the next one, and that’s what builds up over the years, to the point where they can no longer live together.

## Isn’t every couple different in what they argue about?

Every couple is unique – but their arguments are very similar! The unkind comments gradually escalate until the other person retaliates with heated responses, and an argument ensues. Believe it or not, there are experts at household arguments, and they say there are four stages of comments before an argument takes place. Imagine a man and his wife, around dinner time. The man is unhappy because he sees that his wife has again cooked some khichari , which he doesn’t particularly like:

1\. Challenge – an expression of dissatisfaction – “Khichari again?”

2\. Complaint – the challenge is focused into specifics – “I think we have khichari too often.”

3\. Criticism – Accusation of wrongdoing / suggestion through ‘always’ and ‘never’ that it’s a recurring problem – “You always make khichari!” “You never make new things!”

4\. Contempt – dismissive and hurtful remarks – “My friend’s wife cooks nicely for her husband, why don’t you learn some more recipes?”

Anger is a ‘momentary madness’ that threatens any relationship. The Bhagavad-gita explains that by focusing too much on our selfish desires we become attached to certain outcomes. Gradually we lose our intelligence and become bewildered. Anger then makes us temporarily forget the love we have for our spouse. It’s a downward spiral.

In our example above, the husband’s cravings of the tongue made him want to enjoy a variety of palatable dishes and craving for respect made him imagine he deserved pandering from his wife. When this was frustrated, he became angry. His thoughts became words of anger that led to angry actions.

## So, what you’re saying is that disagreement on almost anything can produce a conflict?

Yes, all it takes is two opinions, self-absorption and an obstinate attitude. The most common flash points for dispute are anything to do with work, the in-laws, children, money, sex, housework (because men tend to be less hygienic) personal habits (untidiness, punctuality, reliability). Then there are areas of contention arising from factors we can’t change, such as ill-health and ageing, loss of physical looks, stamina, and diminishing enthusiasm for physical pursuits.

## How can we avoid it?

It is better to resolve a dispute than have a fight. You need to recognise the danger conflict poses to your marriage. Your marriage is a fragile, vulnerable thing you have created, so even unconsciously we create rules to prevent the destruction of our marriages. We speak softly, refrain from talking about hurtful matters, calm issues rather than inflame them, and never, ever, bring up previous arguments or historical mistakes your partner made. Unfortunately, even your own rule book tends to get ripped up during conflict.

The main points of preventing or lessening the effect of marriagebreaking arguments seem to be these:

Develop your inner, spiritual strength – it’s a real super-power, and just waiting for you to discover. Meditation, developing new perspectives on life, practising gratitude, prayer, are all ways to discover the real you inside. That you is the soul, the conscious self, and is free from the pettiness that usually starts the arguments.

Work at Conciliation - conciliation is the antidote to conflict, the renewing of trust or agreement between parties and the restoration of relationships. The chain of events mentioned in the Bhagavad-gita – cravings, attachment, loss of memory, anger - is a ladder downwards. You need to work up the ladder not down:

\- gentle and non-threatening language

\- turn negative disapproval into positive encouragement

\- use ‘we’ instead of ‘you’

\- make a sacrifice in order to change things – ‘look, I will do the cooking.’

Conciliation can be brought in at any stage, but it isn’t easy, and one person may run the risk of losing face and looking foolish. So, it’s risky. If the criticism is unjustified then the conciliation must be balanced with tackling the issues.

Realise that some conflict is inevitable - conflict can be a sign of life and can help. There’s always a cycle in any new situation or a change of circumstances. It takes time for you both to go through this. First there is the new situation, then there’s a period of adjustment where changes of responsibilities are either accepted or resisted; then comes a normalisation period, then progress within the marriage as you again move towards your goals. This is sometimes termed: Forming, storming, norming, and performing. Some conflicts must happen but make it a rule to avoid conflict unless it is absolutely necessary. When conflict happens, always remember that there are worse things than conflict.

Understand the principles of forgiveness - forgiveness is one of the most important tools in defusing conflict; it stops the momentum of a dispute. Forgiveness is:

\- Not an emotion but a decision.

\- It can be a lengthy process.

\- You’ll need to overlook your personal anger and hatred, but not the behaviour.

\- Something we need to give so that we can receive it.

\- Ideally the other person should feel sorry for what they said or did and offer repentance. This completes the healing. But don’t always expect them to repent.

\- It may be costly. You’ll have to abandon any thoughts of revenge and people may think you are weak.

\- But the alternative is to be poisoned by your own bitterness.

Set rules for handling conflict

\- No physical violence or threats / no verbal abuse. Domestic violence is on the increase. Don’t add to the statistics.

\- Agree that certain issues will not be brought up.

\- Set space and time limits on your conflict.

\- Never threaten to walk out.

\- Agree to postpone the conflict.

\- Agree to let each other speak.

## That’s a lot to remember. Can you summarise it?

Alright, here’s a few more tips for handing conflict:

1\. Be aware of the main areas of stress as stated before and clarify each other’s expectations in these areas.

2\. Put the relationship first. There’s both of you, and then there’s the relationship. Although we tend to think of our own needs first, or the other person’s needs first, the main question is always: What’s the best for this relationship?

3\. Seek to understand. Ask questions for clarity and respond to show that you have understood. You don’t have to reply with your views, just re-state your spouse’s views to show you were listening.

4\. Aim for a permanent resolution that cures, not a temporary solution that calms . There’s no point in giving a painkiller for a broken leg, so go for a more permanent cure. And don’t examine the issues without recognising the emotions you and they are feeling.

5\. Keep it cool . Try not to use the word ‘should’ – it sounds like a parent speaking, not a partner. And leave out the ‘you always…’ and ‘you never…’ because this turns the challenge into a criticism. Never shout at each other unless the house is on fire.

6\. Be fair and honest . Don’t give the excuse that you are stressed unless you are willing to excuse your partner too. Never exaggerate or lie: ‘I told you...’ or ‘You knew I…’ Never put the worst possible interpretation on what your partner says: ‘Oh, so now you think I’m…’

7\. Don’t expand the conflict . Stay focused on the issue. Don’t say: ‘and another thing…’

8\. Avoid allegations, insults, and ridicule . People in modern, western culture say that it’s good to express your feelings. But it’s far better to hold your tongue and to think how to most sensitively and tactfully convey your opinion later. If you must criticise don’t do it in the heat of an argument – it can be misidentified as a hurtful insult. This may fester for the rest of the marriage. In marriage you are far better off losing an argument than winning it with insults.

9\. Be prompt in seeking and giving forgiveness . We have a built-in reluctance to admit that we have done wrong. ‘I’m sorry’ is the hardest thing to say. Be quick to say sorry, but also quick to give forgiveness when your partner says sorry.

10\. Set space and time limits on any conflict . Don’t argue publicly, and don’t do it in a sacred space like your local temple, your home puja room, and not in the bedroom. In fact, don’t argue after your normal bedtime. Never part from each other with a conflict unresolved. Unresolved conflicts are a major factor in making extra-marital affairs attractive. If a partner dies with something unresolved it is a double tragedy.

11\. End it properly. Don’t revive an issue. Review what happened, with your spouse if possible. Ask whether it could have been avoided, and if there are any lessons for the future. A conflict is a sudden, large withdrawal from the emotional bank account, and you must now put ‘money’ into the account

## To be honest with you, we seem to argue a lot. Maybe I married the wrong person?

When you argue, it’s very tempting to think like that. Modern mythology is that there is ‘the right person’ or a ‘soul mate’ out there for all of us. The difficulty is that many times that special person is the person you didn’t marry (or the one you’ve never met). That’s why it’s a hopeful myth created by the mind. You should thank God for giving you the one you did marry. Many lessons will be learned by being married to the one you married. You simply need to see them with a fresh pair of eyes.

The problem is that when we’re hurting, we are tempted to blame someone else for our marital storms. Instead of admitting that we ourselves are failing to make the marriage work, we may blame the friends who encouraged us, the astrologer or the elderly relatives who urged us on at the time. We’ll blame our youthful, head-strong nature, or our ‘karma.’ If we get desperately philosophical, we may even feel that the second wife might possibly be a much better choice since so many lessons have been learned with the first one! But second marriages do fail, and third. All that happens is that you simply transfer your problems to the next partner. And they are your problems, not theirs. In all of this, don’t regret your decision. That would be a terrible and painful mistake. Remember that each day can be an entirely new start with the same partner.

## Challenges

## I’m a devotee, but my husband isn’t. We don’t look at life the same way. What can I do?

Don’t panic. Men and women view many things differently anyway, and what you’re reacting to may simply be because he’s a man. You are not alone. But when one partner in a marriage is a devotee of Krishna and the other isn’t it does create the need for a greater level of mutual understanding.

It’s quite common that the wife is a devotee of Krishna, but the husband isn’t, or the husband is a devotee and the wife is not. People become interested in spiritual life when the time is right for them; sometimes that’s before they get married but many times it happens afterwards. This can cause confusion in the relationship when one partner wants to introduce changes in diet or domestic routines.

But there are many couples where, with a bit of give-and-take, they can both live together quite happily. Where there is a strong bond between husband and wife, changes can be negotiated without too much discomfort. But there are a minority of couples where the spiritual life of one of them becomes a nagging problem for the other. The simple solution is to talk about it, come to some compromise, and remain peaceful. The question is not: what can I do, but what can we do?

## I’ve recently become a vegetarian, but my wife hasn’t, so she still cooks meat in the house.

Up and down the country the same scene is playing out. One member of the family chooses to become a vegetarian, and the rest of the family then have to adjust in some way. Families have a variety of solutions: in some there’s more vegetarian options on the table, in others, the whole family goes meat-free on Mondays. In some families the vegetarian doesn’t eat at the same time as the others, and in others, the meat-eaters agree to eat meat only outside the home. Whatever way they make their adjustments, the family members will only do it if they have mutual respect and love. Without that, the vegetarian will be made to feel guilty, or the other members will be reprimanded for being ‘carnivores.’

With the number of vegans in Britain quadrupling over the last four years, and the number of people eating animal-free standing at nearly 2 million, there are any number of homes that have had to make some changes in eating habits. To be sure, when one member of the family announces that they’ll only eat vegetarian food because they are now a devotee of Krishna, it highlights ‘religion’ as the cause of the inconvenient changes that must now be made. Between husband and wife that can cause more than a little tension. Gone are the candle-lit dinners at the romantic restaurant. And if the family is a traditional one where the mother is the main cook, it can mean some favourite recipes being rejected.

But just because one partner turns vegetarian it doesn’t mean it’s the end of romance. Life has never been better for those who want to eat meat-free, and compared to a few decades ago, there are now unlimited places to eat out. It’s not easy for a meat-eating wife to adjust to the newly converted man in her life, especially when cooking for her husband has been one way she showed her love. But if the man sits down with his wife and very gently explains how it makes him feel to have meat cooking in the house, there’s no loving wife that wouldn’t at least consider making some changes.

## I have read that a wife should always try to please her husband, even if what he wants is not that good – isn’t that demeaning and sexist?

The word ‘sexist’ means prejudice or discrimination against women based on their sex. But identifying the strengths and tendencies of men and women – the so-called ‘Mars and Venus’ characteristics – and how they contrast and complement each other is not. Rather, knowing how men and women act and think differently can help establish and maintain psychological balance in the relationship. It isn’t that we must equalise absolutely everything between men and women, neutralising all differences, for there to be harmony. In a marriage, both partners are called upon to serve each other. There is no question of the wife being the servant of the husband if it is not reciprocated.

Love and service must flow in both directions, and yet they may be differently expressed by man and a woman, according to their nature. For peace in the home, it is certainly helpful if the wife thinks of pleasing her husband, but if he is unreasonable, demanding and unappreciative, that desire to please will gradually dry up.

## What do you mean by ‘illicit sex?’

The word illicit means illegal, so sex outside lawful and religious marriage is considered illicit. The other meaning - and it’s an expression used only by devotees of Krishna - is any sex that is not spiritually progressive, that makes the development of latent spirituality any more troublesome than it already is. The idea is that enjoyment of sex tends to intensify the self-serving instinct and root the mind very firmly in the conception of physical identity. This is a problem, because life is about regaining awareness of our innate spiritual identity. There’s a constant struggle between the two identities. The Vedic conception offers an opportunity for establishing a balance between our physical needs and our deepest spiritual aspirations.

## Do you mean sex only within marriage?

Sex within a committed relationship certainly helps to confirm and strengthen the relationship, but only up to a point. The same sex that strengthened the commitment can also serve to weaken it, and even committed relationships can be haunted by routine and boredom.

So, the idea is that we moderate sexual activity by not indulging in it every time the desire arises. That way mental strength and determination increases, and sexual energy can be used as desired. We control it – it doesn’t control us. Dedicated devotees therefore regulate their sex to the times most suitable for conception – around once a month.

## Why should we abstain from sex if we’re married? That doesn’t seem natural.

The pleasure of sex can both make and break a relationship. Like all pleasures, too much can lead to diminishing returns and ill health. Ancient Indian texts had much to say about sex (The Kama Sutra) but also the benefits of reducing it (The Grihya Sutra) as well as giving it up completely (The Yoga Sutra) .

The main lesson from all three books is that you should control it, not be controlled by it. Sex is indeed natural, but too much of it can block you off from gaining other pleasures, such as spiritual experiences. And spiritual experiences can not only help you personally but make your marriage unbreakable.

## We’d like to follow the ‘sex for procreation’ principle, but we’ve already had our children. Does this mean no sex at all?

If you are both beyond ideal childbearing ages, then Mother Nature is probably already giving you cues that your best sexually active years are now behind you. Some people mistakenly think that sex is the entire happiness of marriage and thinking of marriage without sex horrifies them. For this reason, they try to remain young for as long as possible.

But you have discovered the joy of Krishna consciousness, and by now you should know that there can be all kinds of married life, with different kinds of happiness available to you at all stages. Sexual craving, or ‘lust,’ may still come and go, but you really don’t need to act on it. Why not make the best attempt at a married life where the lust is diminished, and the love increased? It will take your marriage forward and make it stronger.

## We are both following that principle, but from time to time we slip. Do you have any advice for us?

The Hare Krishna guru, Srila Prabhupada, said that material life and spiritual life were like opposite ends of a see-saw in a children’s playground. When one end goes up, the other goes down. When spiritual life is on the rise in your life, the material desires based on physical attraction will fall. Married couples always deal with the material energy, and there may be many reasons for the ‘see-saw’ of life to fluctuate. Eating more food than required, consuming highly spiced or sweetened items, engaging with other couples or hearing loose conversations at the workplace – can all change your inner environment and mental resolve from time to time.

Inattentive meditation is the main cause of a gradual sapping of spiritual focus. When attempting to re-balance your spiritual life, you should not allow guilt or shame to motivate you, but instead press on with rededicated enthusiasm. It is a noble ideal that you’ve chosen, and if you develop determination then Krishna will give you the necessary inner strength. You’ll find the basic spiritual practices, such as chanting the Hare Krishna mantra, to be an invaluable source of spiritual strength.

## What about contraception? Is it wrong?

Contraception is an artificial interruption in the natural order of things. Contraceptives are not ‘natural.’ They neither grow on trees nor can they be harvested from a field or garden. They are devices or chemicals invented in order to prevent conception. To have the enjoyment of sex without the natural consequences is a contrivance against nature. It is not a ‘health choice’ but a ‘reproduction choice.’ In these difficult times we need to work with nature rather than against her.

## I understand that no religion favours divorce, but what if the couple just don’t love each other?

Divorce doesn’t solve anything at all and can only result in pain. That is a fact. It has nothing to do with religion or religious beliefs. The teachings of the Vedas are practical wisdom, not arbitrary ideas from an extinct culture. They are as relevant today as they were then. The difficulty for us is that they deal with long-term benefit, not short-term happiness, and they deal with the welfare of society over multigenerations, not just the convenience of one couple, at the present time.

When we encounter the teachings of the Vedas, they may sound too sacrificial for us today; we may feel we are being asked to be better people than we are. We can become better than we are, and the teaching provides us with exactly the techniques of long-distance character development we need.

## Is divorce ever sanctioned in the Vedas?

Surprisingly, there are some circumstances in which a husband or wife are permitted to dissolve the bonds between them, but these are exceptional. Although there are different opinions from numerous sages of the ancient world, the general counsel is to be patient, exercise tolerance and understanding, and to remain together.

Marriage is a sacrament, sanctified in the presence of God and gods. During the marriage ceremony a couple vow to stay together and uphold traditional family values in accordance with Dharma. The bride is given to the bridegroom as a gift from the gods, whom he can never abandon, without incurring the sin of violating the marriage vows. Whatever difficulties the couple may have, society and the scriptures expect them to take them in their stride, as a part of their destiny, and continue their journey together. In exceptional cases, they may live separately, but cannot throw away their marriage relationship without incurring negative karmic consequences for themselves and their children.

If a husband leaves his wife and ‘goes abroad’ for work or education, she is to wait for him for several years, provided she is maintained financially during this time. If only recently married, then the waiting period is reduced to seven months. That a wife does not become pregnant is insufficient grounds for divorce. The husband must wait for eight years. In order to procure a son, he can take a second wife, but he must fully maintain the first wife.

Yet in the 59th chapter of the Arthashastra of Kautilya it is written: ‘If a husband is of bad character or is long gone abroad (and has failed to return), or is likely to endanger the life of his wife, or has fallen from his caste or has lost his virility, he may be abandoned by his wife.’ It is further written:

‘A woman, hating her husband, cannot dissolve her marriage with him against his will. Nor can a man dissolve his marriage with his wife against her will. But from mutual enmity, divorce may be obtained (parasparam dveshán-mokshah ). If either partner, fearing imminent danger from the other, thinks their life may be at risk, they may separate from the other.’

## Is it wrong to have friends of the opposite sex?

We are all in daily working situations which put us together with attractive and capable men and women. It’s natural in those situations to engage in conversations which may not be work-related. Because we spend most of our day at work, if ever there’s a problem, it’s a workmate we turn to. We voice our concerns, fears and hopes. In this situation we receive sympathetic listening, kind words, friendship and even much-needed emotional support. It’s not surprising then that many friendships do develop between men and women in the workplace. The danger is when it starts to become much more than this. There is a clear line between friendship and infatuation, and protecting your marriage is a matter of knowing the difference and acting appropriately.

## My friend had an affair with someone at work, but I don’t think either of us would ever do that.

I’m glad to hear it, but if you think it wouldn’t happen to you, I’d suggest you be extra cautious. It isn’t realistic to assume that because your relationship is strong right now, it will always be so. In today’s world, where men and women spend more time with each other at work than they do with their partners, the temptation is always there. It has never been easier to engage in extra-marital affairs. In some circles it is almost expected after a few years of marriage, with some so-called ‘experts’ claiming that it can rejuvenate the relationship. Nothing could be further from the truth. Affairs are damaging, and adultery is something from which many marriages do not recover.

## What’s the difference between an affair and adultery?

An adulterous affair is where a sexual liaison has taken place. The non-adulterous affair is where, although there is no sexual act, the psychological, social or even spiritual boundaries of a marriage are broken by one party’s relationship with another person. Both an adulterous and a non-adulterous affair damage a marriage in different ways. There are two extreme forms of adultery. One is a sudden, impulsive, hot-blooded sexual encounter. The other is the coldly planned and pursued affair. To add deliberate deceit to adultery is to make things far worse.

## But I have a strong marriage. Are you saying that affairs are inevitable?

Not at all, but affairs are like fires breaking out, they can happen almost anywhere, although we can never predict them. And just as you might take precautions against fire in your home or place of work, you would do well to fire-proof your marriage. An affair is not impossible, even for a couple whose marriage is otherwise happy. Affair-proofing is not a strategy needed only by those with weak marriages; it is needed by everybody who is married.

## So how do I ‘affair-proof’ my marriage?

The first thing is to be conscious of what conditions lead to affairs, and the second thing is to avoid them. Number one would be the oldest condition we all have: Kama or ‘Lust,’ described in the Bhagavad-gita as ‘the all-devouring sinful enemy of the world,’ and rather colourfully as ‘one of the three gates leading to hell’ (the other two being Greed and Anger).

The word may sound old-fashioned to us now, but lust means intense craving. And of all the things we might crave, lust becomes most ardently focused as a desire for sex. In normal circumstances, that lust is directed to our spouse only, and thereby moderated by love, but such is the state of the world that stimulation surrounds us on all sides. Whether the non-stop exploitation of bodily attraction in advertising, the relentless pushing towards the fulfilment of all and every lust in social media, or the attractive dressing and office banter of the workplace, we are all in an environment that provokes lust, rather than reduces it.

Too much stimulation of lust in marriage can have the opposite of the intended effect. Instead of sexual fulfilment, too much indulgence of every craving leads to diminishing enjoyment, fuels dissatisfaction, distorts the relationship between husband and wife, and leads on to the tendency to become attracted elsewhere.

## But what can I do about sex desire? It’s just the way I am.

One of the fallacies about lust is that being married will completely take care of it. Unfortunately, the Bhagavad-gita tells us that lust ‘burns like fire,’ and it’s a fire that can’t be put out by ordinary means. The more sex you have, the more you want. It’s an insatiable desire.

There’s an old story about an Indian king who had delightful sex for many, many years. At the end of his life he was still unhappy because his passions had been like flames into which he’d been pouring melted butter. We are each born with a certain amount of lust – that much is sure – and it has its purpose. But if we moderate it by not indulging in every passing desire, we’ll find that it becomes tamed – and tamed is good for your marriage.

## You mentioned there were other elements dangerous to my marriage?

Yes, the next one is loneliness. Men may contemplate breaking their marriage vows when they’re on extended business trips, or when they go to work early and come home late. Added to that physical loneliness is the psychological loneliness that comes when one partner doesn’t feel understood or loved. Loneliness is a fertile soil for an affair, and communication – verbal, emotional and physical, is the best antidote for loneliness. To treat loneliness by having an affair is as sensible as burning your house down to keep warm. You need to make friends, not have an affair.

## Lust and Loneliness – what about plain old boredom?

Yes, boredom is a big one. When the novelty of being married has worn off and the initial infatuation has faded away, you can both enter a slump of being bored with each other. The same conversations, same food, same clothes, same routines. Then at work, every day brings the same mindless, repetitive tasks and there is nothing to look forward to. Added to this can be the insecurity of the mid-life crisis .

Beginning around the age of 35, a nagging anxiety can arise that life has not, yet, delivered to you everything you expected. Your anticipated promotion has not yet materialised, and you realise that you need to change things about yourself in order to achieve your dreams. It’s at this time that a new car is purchased, new house, the job is changed, and the ensuing anxiety then is how to pay for everything. That leads to arguments about money.

## You’re painting a very bleak picture for me. Can you cheer me up?

Oh, I haven’t quite finished yet! I’m giving you this list of danger zones so that you’ll be able to protect your marriage. It doesn’t mean that it’s all going to happen to you. Forewarned is forearmed, they say, and prevention is better than a cure. The next thing is just plain Laziness . When things don’t go well in a marriage, the opportunity of another relationship appears, and then it may seem to you to offer a far easier option than working at your marriage.

Wherever there’s a successful marriage, I will show you one where there has been a great deal of hard work. Laziness is often coupled with Escapism , to seek consciously or subconsciously an escape route from a situation where a difficult action is required. You should beware the temptation to dodge hard issues. Face up to the things you must do and tackle them head on. To dodge every challenge and difficulty is no way to run life and is certainly no way to mature.

## My friend who had the affair, he said it all just crept up on him. He didn’t realise it was happening until it was too late.

Yes, that happens, even to people who have been married for twenty-five years. By slow degrees, when several of the above elements are in place, a person may become infatuated with a friend, work colleague or even someone they met accidentally. Infatuation is an irresistible attraction which feels like an intense love. It doesn’t last longer than a few months but is usually long enough to heavily damage a marriage. Here’s twelve signs that you may have become infatuated with someone:

1\. You find that you are sharing things with them that you do not share with your spouse.

2\. You choose to spend excessive amounts of time with them.

3\. You enjoy being with them more than being with your spouse and you feel excited about meeting them.

4\. You feel they understand you better than your spouse does.

5\. You feel exhilarated or ‘young again’ when you are with them.

6\. You take more care over your appearance when you meet them than when you are with your spouse.

7\. You refuse to listen to warnings or advice about your relationship with them.

8\. You feel defensive about the relationship so that you end up making excuses or justifying it using such phrases as ‘she’s really part of the family’, or ‘he’s going through some problems and I’m helping him’ or ‘it’s purely a platonic relationship.’

9\. You adjust your schedule or your travel arrangements so that you meet up with them, or more seriously – so that you can meet up alone.

10\. You find that, for no special reason, you are thinking about them, and especially about their looks or charm.

11\. You have fantasies - not necessarily sexual – about them in which this person replaces your spouse. For instance, you imagine what it would be like to come home after work to be met by them.

12\. You find yourself comparing your spouse negatively to them. You discover yourself thinking ‘if only my wife had her charm and wit’ or ‘if only my husband had his good humour and looks.’

## That sounds like a bad situation. How can I prevent it happening to me?

Beware of the risks: remember, infatuation can wreck a marriage faster than anything can. The non-adulterous affair that I talked about earlier is still an affair and still breaks the exclusivity of the marriage bond. You need to take preventative action at the first hint of infatuation. Distance yourself and quickly. Above all, don’t act on your feelings. If possible, take the matter to a trustworthy and reliable friend. And before, during and after – pray.

## I suppose that early prevention is better than a lastminute cure?

Yes, always. You need to understand the warning signs long before anything else happens, and act immediately. Even before that, just do all you can to strengthen your marriage. Here are some main points to remember:

**Resolve to put your marriage first**

Resolve that your marriage, not you, not your career, not your friends, not your hobbies, comes first.

**Recognise and act on the risks**

Risks in a marriage alter with time. For instance, from having young children to having teenagers at home. As the relationship changes, so new weaknesses may develop and new risks surface. Be vigilant in changing circumstances.

**See through the lies about affairs**

Unfortunately, there are a lot of lies about affairs. From newspaper and magazine to television dramas and cinema, having an affair is almost always portrayed as more passionate, exciting and ultimately more

‘real.’ When did you last see a positive portrayal of a happy and passionate marriage on the small or big screen? The prevailing attitude is that you can try marriage if you want – just don’t expect it to last. There’s negative lies about marriage as well as positive lies about affairs. For instance, the idea that to have an affair is a sign of maturity and liberty, and of breaking loose from inhibitions.

**See to your own needs**

All the research and all the experience of thousands of couples point to the same thing: affair-free marriages are generally happier, healthier, more stable and, in every way better than affairs. To have a much happier life, you should avoid affairs. You’ll be much happier.

## You spoke about ‘warning signs’ – what are they?

• You wake up still angry with them after a row the previous night

• You don’t spend as much time with them as you could

• You make excuses to do different things to them

• You wish you could spend more time away from them

• You find some aspect of their personality repeatedly irritates you

• You have regrets about marrying them

• You accept the idea that your marriage is not going anywhere

• You realise that you can’t remember when you last laughed together

• You find that some small weakness of your spouse is more important than their major strengths

• You let secrets build up between you

• You are aware that your only physical contact is driven by sexual desire

• You realise that you can’t remember when you last praised them or they last praised you

• You recognise that although you’re married you still feel single

• You decide that your marriage is something that you endure rather than enjoy

• You find that you are saying harsh things about your spouse to friends

• You either use, or think harsh words about your spouse

• You notice that one or both of you regularly dig up past failures in arguments

## Quick tips if I find myself in a situation?

I would say that you need to remember the five ‘Rs’:

1\. Respond decisively

2\. Run from danger

3\. Resist fantasies

4\. Remember your promises

5\. Reflect the cost

## But what about if it’s my partner on the brink of an affair?

Don’t panic.

Don’t make things worse.

Try to understand.

Ask if you must take any blame.

Try to communicate about the issues.

Try to agree to see a qualified marriage counsellor.

Pray.

## You’ve given me some great advice. How about giving me your top ten?

**Seek help early.**

The average couple waits six years before seeking help for relationship problems. And keep in mind, half of all marriages that end do so in the first seven years. This means the average couple lives with unhappiness for far too long. If you feel there’s any sign of trouble in your marriage early on, seek help.

**Express interest.**

Learn what is happening in your partner’s world. Ask questions that show you are interested in their day-to-day life. We sometimes forget to check in with our partner or fail to respond to their attempts to connect. Over time this can create serious damage to the relationship. It can be as simple as asking, “How was your day?” Deeper levels of connection are possible when you ask open-ended questions about your partner’s internal world of thoughts, feelings, hopes and concerns.

**Edit yourself.**

The most successful couples are kind to each other. They avoid saying every critical thought when discussing touchy topics, and they will find ways to express their needs and concerns respectfully without criticizing or blaming their partner.

**Soften your ‘start up.’**

Arguments often start because one partner escalates the conflict by making a critical or contemptuous remark. Bringing up problems gently and without blame works much better and allows couples to calmly engage in conflict.

**Accept influence from your partner.**

Research shows that women are already well practiced at accepting influence from men. A true partnership only occurs when a husband can do the same thing.

**Have high standards.**

Happy couples have high standards for each other. The most successful couples are those who, even as newlyweds, refused to accept hurtful behaviour from one another. Low levels of tolerance for bad behaviour in the beginning of a relationship equals a happier couple down the road.

**Learn to repair and exit an argument.**

Happy couples have learned how to exit an argument, or how to repair the situation before an argument gets completely out of control.

Examples of repair attempts: using humour; offering a caring remark (“I understand that this is hard for you”); making it clear you’re on common ground (“We’ll tackle this problem together”); backing down and, in general, offering signs of appreciation for your partner and their feelings along the way. If an argument gets too heated, take a 20-minute break, and agree to approach the topic again when you are both calm.

**Focus on the positives.**

In a happy marriage, while discussing problems, couples make at least five times as many positive statements to and about each other and their relationship as negative ones. For example, a happy couple will say “We laugh a lot” instead of “We never have any fun.” A good marriage must have a rich climate of positivity. Make regular deposits to your emotional bank accounts.

**Make friends with spiritual people.**

By including spiritually progressive people in your circle of friends you’ll increase your chances of being on the receiving end of good advice and support. Their influence may help you to overcome any early problems and help you make your marriage strong. You can invite them to your home and make a point of keeping in touch with them.

**Have strong spiritual practice.**

In addition to having spiritual friends, you can ensure that your own spiritual strength is developing by taking time each morning to focus, meditate, study and discuss wisdom, or make a small act of worship and prayer at your home altar. Mantra meditation works quickly and effectively.
