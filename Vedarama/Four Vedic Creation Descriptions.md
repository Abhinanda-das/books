# Four Vedic Creation Descriptions

## Four Vedic Creation Descriptions

JANUARY 19, 2024

Reading Time: 5 minutes

In several Vedic texts, living entities are classified into four categories based on the type of birth: (a) Andaj, born from an egg, (b) Svedaja, born from sweat, (c) Udbhija, born from a seed, and (d) Jarāyuja, born from a womb. These four descriptions are simultaneously employed for the creation of the universe so the universe is created as four kinds of birth.

In Bhagavad-Gita [14.3](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/14.3), Kṛṣṇa states that the material energy is the womb and He is the seed-giving father, implying that the creation is a birth from a womb. In Bhagavad Gita [7.10](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/7.10), He states that all the living entities are His seeds, implying that the universal creation is a birth from a seed. The universe is described as a Brahmānda or Brahma’s egg in various places in Vedic texts, into which Garbhodakaśāyi Viṣṇu enters to produce life beginning with Brahma, implying that this is a birth from an egg. It is stated that Kāraṇodakaśāyī Visnu lies in an ocean of sweat produced from His body and numerous universes emerge from the pores of His skin along with sweat, implying that is a birth from sweat.

These four descriptions pertain to four different ways of understanding the creation.

The living entity or the soul is the seed of creation. It is covered by material energy, like soil, water, air, and sunlight cover an ordinary seed. The world is produced from the seed when it combines with its covering. Hence, the world is said to be produced from a seed.

The covering is like an egg, with two parts. The inner part is the egg’s nucleus contributed by Prakṛti. The outer part, like an albumen, is contributed by Śakti. Thus, if we consider the egg in which the seed fructifies into a tree, then we can call it a creation from an egg.

The egg is produced from sweat, also called Māyā, which is produced from the body of Kāraṇodakaśāyī Viṣṇu. Māyā is the negation of Viṣṇu and She becomes Śiva’s consort. Thus, if we invoke the source of the egg, then we can call the universe a creation from sweat.

Māyā is animated by kāla or time, also called Śiva. The living entity or soul is a part of Śiva, i.e., under the control of time. He impregnates Māyā by injecting the soul into Māyā. Since Māyā is impregnated by Śiva, hence, the universe is called birth from a womb.

The whole situation can be visualized as comprising three realities—the soul, matter, and time. Matter is itself divided into Māyā, Prakṛti, and Śakti. Viṣṇu is the source of all these realities. Hence, they are considered three aspects of Viṣṇu. The tree of the universe develops within the egg which is also called Brahmānda. The soul remains trapped inside the egg and doesn’t see what exists outside of it. Prakṛti and Śakti are the egg. Māyā is the sweat. The soul is the seed. And Śiva is the father. Viṣṇu is the source of all four of them.

In this way, four descriptions are applied to the universe: (a) Andaj, born from an egg, (b) Svedaja, born from sweat, (c) Udbhija, born from a seed, and (d) Jarāyuja, born from a womb. Each of these is a partial truth. The whole truth is the four descriptions together. Śiva, Māyā, Śakti, Prakṛti, and the soul are necessary but insufficient causes of creation. Viṣṇu is the source of these but they remain inactive within Him. Their activation and mixing occur outside Viṣṇu. When the five causes combine, then the causality is necessary and sufficient. Since the soul’s desire is responsible for it being injected into the universe, hence, suffering is ultimately attributed to the soul, rather than to Viṣṇu, Śiva, Māyā, Śakti, or Prakṛti who are called facilitators of the desires in the soul.

The different contributions of Māyā, Śakti, Prakṛti, Śiva, and soul must be understood to describe the creation correctly and completely. Nārāyana is accorded the greatest emphasis in this entire description of the universe because He is the source of all. It is not a rejection of the other realities or personalities. It is simply a summarized description. Those who want a complete understanding study the contributions of each of these actors.

The reason for the existence of the material world is individualism in the soul. It is the desire to be free or independent of Viṣṇu and create a personal world in which the soul is the lord and master of all it surveys. Many words are used to describe this individualism. Sometimes, it is called Pradhāna which means the ruler or master. Sometimes, it is called Ahaṃkāra, which is the egotism of an individual. Sometimes, it is called Māyā, which means what Viṣṇu is not, or sovereign from Viṣṇu. Sometimes, it is called Bahiranga Śakti, which means considering oneself external to Viṣṇu. All these descriptions are correct. They just pertain to the different aspects and features of individualism and its consequences.

Individualism also entails loneliness. The entire burden of life rests on an individual. Since this individual is small, hence, individualism creates fear. To fight this fear, the soul tries to accumulate wealth, power, fame, knowledge, and beauty. Sometimes, it tries to renounce these and pretends to be independent. The power to fight this fear is called Śakti and the things accumulated by the power are called Prakṛti. Māyā, or the sense of separation, gives birth to the power to fight and the things acquired by this struggle. Hence, the ultimate solution for all material problems is giving up individualism.

This individualism is called the Kāraṇa or the root cause of the material existence. It is produced as an ocean of sweat from the body of Kāraṇodakaśāyī Viṣṇu. The different places in the ocean are different kinds of individualism. They are transformed into different universes. Kāraṇodakaśāyī Viṣṇu is a non-individualistic individual. The individualistic creation is called His dream. The dream is temporary. Hence, it is not truth or reality which must be eternal. The material world is also therefore called a delusion. It is real from the standpoint of the soul but a delusion from the standpoint of Kāraṇodakaśāyī Viṣṇu.

All ideologies can therefore be characterized based on the single idea of individualism. If any ideology espouses the separateness of individuals, then it is the byproduct of the material nature. The worst individualism is the exploitation of others for one’s self-interest. A slightly better individualism is working for one’s own selfish betterment. An even better individualism is renunciation of the world for emancipation. These are respectively the guna of tamas, rajas, and sattva. They are different kinds of individualism. The non-individualistic philosophy is serving the whole through which every part of the whole is served.
