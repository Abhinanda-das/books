# ISKCON’s Views on Science and Vaisnavism: comparing the 1979 Life Comes from Life project with the archival transcripts of the recorded conversations

## Introduction

*Stewart Kreitzer, University of Florida*

In 1979, The Bhaktivedanta Book Trust (BBT) published the English edition of Life Comes From Life: A Startling Challenge to the Modern Scientific Theory of the Origin of Life and the Universe. The publishers described the book’s sixteen chapters as comprising morning walk discussions that offer “an impromptu but brilliant critique of some of the dominant policies, theories and presuppositions of modern science and scientists by one of the greatest philosophers and scholars of the century, His Divine Grace A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada.” The Forward explained that the book’s contents are “based on taped morning-walk conversations that Srila Prabhupada had with some of his disciples during 1973, in the Los Angeles area.”1 Perhaps more than any single project, this book has played a prominent role representing Bhaktivedanta Swami’s views on modern science.

Fortunately, in addition, the Bhaktivedanta Archives produced archival recording transcripts for most of these discussions, and then made them widely available beginning in 1989. Furthermore, many of the recordings also remain available, and they suggest the transcribers did an accurate job. Yet these transcriptions proved significantly different from the text offered in Life Comes From Life. Whereas the archival transcripts offered a comparatively nuanced and complex exchange that the publishers considered an “impromptu but brilliant critique,” the actual published volume presented a textual representation so different, it could be considered a condensed stylized revision. Though a close examination confirms a relationship between the book and the source recordings, it is difficult finding even a short phrase in the book that accurately replicates the original conversation. Since the 1979 English edition of Life Comes From Life is popularly understood within ISKCON as an accurate representation of these historical discussions, this situation presents a challenge both to scholars, as well as Bhaktivedanta Swami’s followers, who wish to develop a comprehensive appreciation of Bhaktivedanta Swami’s views.

This paper will describe some of the history behind the publication of Life Comes From Life (LCFL). While the published texts are not historically accurate, the reasons for this can be sympathetically appreciated when considering various book publication challenges experienced during ISKCON’s formative years. This paper will also analyze a few key textual selections to help illustrate the difference between the original recording transcripts, and the two “original” published accounts – the first, the 1974 German edition, followed by an English edition a few years later. The argument will be made that the original recordings and archival transcripts are well worth studying not just for their authenticity, but also for their content, as they offer a significantly more substantive portrayal of Bhaktivedanta Swami’s concerns involving Gaudiya Vaisnavism, professional science, and the modern world.

## Publication History

The morning walks that became Life Comes From Life took place in Los Angeles, during May and December of 1973. It is possible they would have been seen as another set of spontaneous discussions much like previous morning walk conversations, if not for the participation of a young disciple, Svarupa Damodara, who was completing his Ph.D. studies in Physical Organic Chemistry at the University of California, Irvine.For example, one published account noted it was only after these walks that regularly bringing along recording equipment became a priority.Further, the devotees attending these walks soon expressed appreciation that Bhaktivedanta Swami was opening a new initiative.He would also encourage his disciples to participate in his conversations with Svarupa Damodara.

An initial interest to publish these dialogues occurred early the following year, when the young German BBT editors working with Hansadutta dasa, the regional Governing Body Commissioner (GBC) at the German Schloss Rettershof center, listened to a set of tapes. At the time, Golden Avatar Studios in Los Angeles, a forerunner to the Bhaktivedanta Archives, served as a repository for Bhaktivedanta Swami’s recordings.They offered subscription options for purchasing cassette tapes in their advertisements in ISKCON’s monthly journal, Back To Godhead magazine. It appears recordings were made available as they arrived at the studios from devotees travelling with Bhaktivedanta Swami. Likely the German BBT editors received their recordings through this subscription service not long after the walks occurred.

In a published historical account titled, Srila Prabhupada and His Disciples in Germany (1996), and coincidently written by one of the first German BBT editors, the author recalls, “at the beginning of 1974, when we first heard the tapes of Prabhupada’s conversations with the devotee-scientist Svarupa Damodara about the origin of life, we convinced Hansadutta to ask Srila Prabhupada whether we could publish those talks in a book entitled Life Comes From Life. Germans are generally scientifically-minded, and we knew it would be great for preaching.”7

As GBC leader in Germany, Hansadutta proved himself a driving force for book publication and distribution. Bhaktivedanta Swami replied to Hansadutta’s letter on April 11, 1974: “Yes, by all means you may print my morning walk speaking on “Life come from Life” into a small book; this argument should be spread, as any intelligent man will be convinced that our discussion is thoroughly scientific and exposes the so called materialistic scientists as rascals. So go on printing and distributing as many books as possible; this is your real work and your personal success.”

Life Come From Life, titled in German as Leben kommt von Leben, became one of the BBT’s earliest German hardback publications, along with a translation of the first volume of Bhaktivedanta Swami’s Krishna Book (a summary study of the Tenth Canto of the Bhagavata Purana), and the Teachings of Lord Caitanya (a summary study of the Chaitanya Charitamrita).The following October, Hansadutta sent published copies to Bhaktivedanta Swami in India. Bhaktivedanta Swami inquired in his reply: “And what about “Leben Kommt Von Leben?” Are they also selling? I have never studied science, but I am challenging them. They may take me as crazy, but I am not crazy, I am right.”

Worth noting about Bhaktivedanta Swami’s argument is that while he readily admitted to not being a scientist himself, he felt qualified to present a challenge based on what he considered essential Gaudiya Vaisnava principles, to wit, his life comes from life manifesto that consciousness is not an epiphenomenon of matter. Bhaktivedanta Swami’s interactions on these walks helped to illustrate to his young disciples what he considered philosophical fallibilities popularly identified with the professional sciences. He appeared to consider much of the prestige afforded the natural sciences to promote a naturalistic agenda, inflated. That was something he became fond of referring to as “so-called science.”

As for the production of the German book, the text only loosely followed the original recordings. Still, it is easy to imagine young enthusiastic followers eagerly translating the English recordings into German as they listened to them, and then editing and condensing their German into a presentation that appeared dynamic as a book. Undoubtedly, when translating, and especially if translating from a live foreign language recording, it is not always possible to offer a wordfor- word, point-by-point exchange. As it turns out, these conversations became dramatically rearranged in a manner that did not likely appear problematic at the time to Bhaktivedanta Swami’s young followers, particularly given the pressure to publish quickly and the relative limitation of editorial resources available to them during this pioneering phase. At the very least, the new book offered an introductory exposure to Bhaktivedanta Swami’s views, whereas prior there was none.

Hansadutta immediately inquired about publishing an English version, and to that Bhaktivedanta Swami replied: “Regarding publishing the Life from Life in English it should be grammatically correct because it is written book. Yes, it will be very good if you publish a book of lectures.”Of note, Bhaktivedanta Swami mentioned the need for proper grammar in a printed edition. But other than that, he welcomed turning his lectures into book form. It is not clear if he ever read the German edition, and thus realized the breadth of the adjustments made in the publication.

Unfortunately, a raid by the local police the following month disrupted editorial work. In his historical account, the BBT editor recollected that early one morning, “a well-dressed man in a dark coat knocked on the front door. He was the state prosecutor . . . and he was not alone. Accompanied by eighty policemen, he entered . . . and presented an order of investigation.” For “many months,” the prosecutor had “gathered apparently incriminating evidence of fraud” involving fund raising done in the name of a food relief program in India, along with other transactions that attracted his attention.

Though Hansadutta had vigorously pioneered book distribution, his business acumen may not have been as focused. Bhaktivedanta Swami commented in a letter to one of his European leaders: “Regarding dishonest means being used, I have never advised or taught anyone like that. That is not my idea.”He confided to another, “This incident in Germany has caused havoc all over the world. It is hampering our reputation everywhere.”While discussing concerns with a GBC manger in America, Bhaktivedanta Swami stated, “Our occupation must be honest. Everyone should adore our members as honest.”The disruptive events in Germany suggest that a supportive environment for rigorous, scholarly editorial work may not have been ideal during this period. Suffice it to say, the full English edition would need to wait.

In the meantime, the American BBT editors at Back to Godhead (BTG) magazine published extracts from the morning walk series. One dialogue, published in BTG in early 1974, offered the title, “The Tiny World of Modern Science.”It presented a morning walk held on April 16, 1973, but that date does not appear in the Archives’ database. This could suggest that the conversation recording had been misplaced sometime prior to the establishment of the Bhaktivedanta Archives in the late 1970s. Still, the article also appeared a second time as a chapter in another BBT project, the Science of Self Realization (1977). Since this conversation was published prior to the German devotees requesting permission to translate the recordings, and subsequently not included in the German presentation, the 1974 BTG essay could represent a direct transcription of an original English language recording.

Meanwhile, a second BTG article published in late 1975 shared the title “Life Comes From Life.” It appears to be a compilation of extracts from a number of different sections of what would later become part of the 1979 English edition, based on the earlier German publication.Though the text is similar, it does not match exactly, suggesting the possibility that portions of the essay could have been derived from the German translation at a different time by different editors. One senior editor presently working with the BBT mentioned that back in the day, utilizing a sense of license to help illustrate pertinent arguments could appear more of a norm.

Though legal questions involving German finances proved a serious challenge, Hansadutta’s reputation as an ISKCON pioneer soon rebounded. During August and September of 1977, he received favorable press coverage in Sri Lanka for a series of exchanges that were part of a vigorous debate with a well known leader of the South Asian rationalist movement, Dr. Abraham Kovoor.In the end, Hansadutta gained an upper hand when he challenged Kovoor to produce life from matter, and if he could not, then to desist from challenging religious figures to produce definitive empirical proof for God. Hansadutta even offered a $60,000 prize if Kovoor could deliver. A BBT publication reported on the result: “Having been challenged \[by Hansadutta\] to demonstrate that he could create life at a public meeting, Dr. Kovoor declined to attend, saying that he would not feel obliged to demonstrate that he could create life, unless God Himself were to appear in person to demonstrate the same. The \[Colombo\] Sunday Times reported this with the headline ‘Flummoxed Kovoor Passes the Buck.’”

Hansadutta regularly updated Bhaktivedanta Swami on these events, and though he was gravely ill during this period, Bhaktivedanta Swami expressed effusive enthusiasm for Hansadutta’s challenge to the atheist rationalist. At the time, Tamal Krishna Maharaja served as Bhaktivedanta Swami’s secretary, and on his behalf he wrote to Hansadutta, “I read your reply \[to Kovoor,\] and Srila Prabhupada was very pleased . . . Srila Prabhupada stated, ‘Hansadutta has become famous now. He is putting very strong arguments. This is preaching.’”In a follow up exchange, Tamal Krishna Maharaja reported to Hansadutta: “Prabhupada liked very much your idea to print the talks between His Divine Grace and Svarupa Damodara with an introduction consisting of the correspondence with Dr. Kovoor and yourself. Prabhupada said, ‘It is very good idea. Do it immediately. This will certainly finish the rascal.’”Coincidently, Kovoor passed away a year later.

As in the previous discussions held on the morning walks, Hansadutta’s argument with Kovoor focused on life coming from life, as compared to it being an occurrence entirely reducible to chemicals and thus an epiphenomenon of matter. In addition, since Kovoor had long since retired as a professional biologist, he now indulged in evangelizing atheistic rationalism, albeit with an overlay of respectability drawn from his past career as a practicing scientist. In the end, Hansadutta’s successful experience debating Kovoor rekindled interest in publishing an English edition of Life Comes from Life, and now with the added feature of excerpts from Hansadutta’s interactions with Kovoor.

At this point the Los Angeles BBT began to pursue production of the classic 1979 English volume. The format of the book reflected the earlier German edition: same chapters, same dates, and many of the same chapter subtitles. In addition, when comparing the English text with the German text, the two are far closer to each other, than either to the recordings. This strongly suggests that the English edition is a translation of the German edition, which in turn took creative liberties as a translation of the original English language morning walk conversations. Editorial drafts utilized for the English project suggest the same.This could help explain why the text in the 1979 English publication is so difficult to match with the 1973 English language recordings.

In defense of this set of circumstances, recording transcripts had not been regularly produced until the late 1970s, at around the time research commenced on the Prabhupada Lilamrta biography project shortly after Bhaktivedanta Swami’s departure. An account of the history of the Bhaktivedanta Archives published in the ISKCON Communications Journal, describes how at that point, it was “decided that the BBT would fund the transcribing and proofreading of all of Srila Prabhupada’s tapes. This would meet two needs: providing material for the Lilamrta and creating an archival record. It was further proposed to create a microfiche of these transcripts, \[in\] a project named Operation Vani. A massive typing and proofreading process took place over more than four years, during which devotees typed 40,000 pages of transcripts and finally photographed them onto microfiche.”In 1987, these records were scanned and digitized, along with copies of Bhaktivedanta Swami’s books. These materials subsequently became the foundation for what became known as the digital Bhaktivedanta VedaBase, initially produced in 1991.

None of this was available during the 1970s when the precursor to the Bhaktivedanta Tape Ministry, Golden Avatar Studios, managed a cassette tape subscription service. Neither was a catalogue of back titles mentioned in their advertisements. In fact, the Journal account reported that during this period, tapes could be seen “sitting in an open room and largely unsupervised,” with recordings “in large boxes, many unmarked. Many bore no indication of when or where they were recorded.” But in 1977, the BBT established the Bhaktivedanta Tape Ministry, to provide a systematic structure and “catalogue \[of\] the tapes . . . \[which\] over the following fifteen years . . . gradually increased” to over 700 recordings.Suffice it to say that during the 1970s, it would have been a challenge locating specific recordings on specific themes for comprehensive research. Thus the relative lack of research tools available made it difficult to appreciate to what degree, if any, there could have been a significant difference between these early publications, and the original recordings.

But even if the editors of the English edition had been familiar with these concerns, it may not have been seen as critical since at the time since there lacked a formal collection of authorized transcripts to review. Things as they were, the “original” German publication appeared to work quite well as a book, so why not use it as a basis for an English manuscript? To get a quality hardback into publication would offer something substantial, whereas before, there was hardly anything at all. Over the decades, the English language Life Comes From Life became a huge publication success. For example, the 2007 Australian BBT edition listed nearly one million copies in print.

## A Comparative Textual Analysis

Viewing the 1974 German and 1979 English editions alongside the archival recording transcript helps to illustrate the extent of difference between them.Toward that end, the second part of this study will examine specific selections from the morning walk discussion published in the second chapter of both the German and English editions. The significance for using this sample is that the Bhaktivedanta Archives holds both a recording, and transcript, that match the date identified with this chapter – April 19, 1973. This analysis will particularly focus on selections from the beginning and end of the chapter. Images of textual sections are included at the end of the essay, along with a list of relevant primary source documents with addresses for viewing online.

The introductory page from the four sets of texts used for analyzing the second chapter are: (1) the 1974 German language translation of the English recordings \[Figure 1\]; (2) a late 1970s editorial draft from Los Angeles subsequently used for the 1979 English edition \[Figure 2\]; (3) the 1979 English language edition \[Figure 3\], and; (4) the original recording transcript \[Figure 4\], initially published in 1989 by the Bhaktivedanta Archives as part of an encyclopedic set. The Archives subsequently distributed the recording transcriptions in a digital “VedaBase” program, beginning in the early 1990s. Complete samples of these documents are also available at a variety of Krishna related websites. Internet addresses are listed both separately in individual footnotes, as well as in a comprehensive list in the Appendix.

First under consideration, is the beginning of the second chapter published in the 1974 German edition Leben Kommt Leben \[Figure 1\]. Please note the date at the top of the page (19, April 1973), along with the spelling “Shevot-Hills Park” identifying location. Also note that the first line of text begins with “Srila Prabhupada: Archāologen ...” Then a little over ten lines down is the phrase: “Srila Prabhupada: Die Drei Erscheinungsweisen der Natur – sattva, raja und tama.” This Sanskrit phrase refers to the modes of nature – goodness, passion and ignorance, and it is easily identified in all the texts used for publication.

The second image \[Figure 2\] is a sample from an editorial draft used for the 1979 English edition. It came from the files of one of the Los Angeles offices affiliated with BBT production.Please note the same date, and the same incorrect spelling for “Shevot-Hills Park.” Also note in the first line of the initial crossed-out section are the words: “Srila Prabhupada: Archeologists ...” This compares to the first line of the German edition. Then a few lines down, after the crossed-out section, is the beginning of the phrase: “Srila Prabhupada: Three qualities, sattvas, rajas and tamas.” This matches the Sanskrit terms found approximately ten lines from the beginning of the German edition sample for the second chapter.

Now compare these two textual samples with the first page of the second chapter \[Figure 3\] of the 1979 English edition of Life Comes From Life. Please note once again the same date (April 19, 1973), but now with the correct English spelling for Cheviot Hills Park. Also note that the section crossed out in the editorial draft is now absent. The text now rather begins with the phrase: “Srila Prabhupada: This material world is a composition of three qualities – sattva, rajas and tamas.” This are the same Sanskrit terms found in the German edition, just after the crossed out section in the editorial draft.

These three samples of textual evidence from the beginning of the second morning walk discussion presentations with the date, April 19, 1973, suggest that the German publication was used as a basis for the English edition. First, there is the incorrect spelling for Cheviot Hills Park in the German text (Shevot-Hills Park) carried over to the English editorial draft. Then there is the initial section of the second chapter of the 1974 German edition crossed out in the English editorial draft and subsequently deleted from the 1979 English edition. Finally, the second chapter of the English edition begins with a phrase that includes the Sanskrit terms sattva, raja and tama. This phrase, found in all three editions, opens the chapter for the 1979 English edition, just after the crossed out section in the editorial draft that corresponds to the section deleted from the 1974 German publication. Of interest, though this Sanskrit phrase describing the “three qualities” is found in all the publication materials, it is not found in the recording or archival transcript for the actual morning walk with the same date.

These examples suggest that the original 1973 English language recordings were translated into what became the 1974 German edition, which in turn was translated back into English to provide an editorial manuscript for what became the classic 1979 English publication. Suffice it to say, an editorial and publication track record of this quality does not lend itself well to creating a historically accurate account.

Now compare these three examples of text used for publication, to the first page of the archival transcript for the morning walk conversation transcript with the same date, April 19, 1973 \[Figure 4\]. The Archives and the BBT initially published this particular copy of the transcript in 1989 as part of an encyclopedia set.Please note that the archival transcript reads as if it could be a different conversation altogether when compared to the three variants used for publication.

A useful way to identify similarities is by locating and matching Sanskrit terms and verse selection reference numbers. The primary evidence offered in the opening sequence of all the texts is a reference to Bhagavad-gita 9.10: “Under My superintendence, nature is working.”This verse citation is found in every text as follows: (1) In the archival record, in the first paragraph of the transcription; (2) in the 1974 German edition, in the middle of the second page of the second chapter (p.18); (3) in the editorial manuscript, in the middle of the second page of “Morning Walk II” (p.12), and; (4) at the top of the second page of “The Second Morning Walk” in the 1979 English edition (p.8). Other than the direct citation reference to Bhagavad-gita 9.10, similarities are not that obvious in the opening section when comparing the published editions with the original recording.

Though a comprehensive examination can find samples of text of a similar quality suggesting correspondence, the wording is generally never exact or found in the same relative position within the conversation.This can be explained by the publication history described in this essay – the English edition was not taken directly from the English language recordings, but rather is based on a German translation that took extensive liberties.

Next under consideration is the concluding section to the second chapter of both the German and English publications, as well as the late 1970s English editorial manuscript. These will be compared to the recording and archival transcript that shares the same date, April 19, 1973. In the published texts as well as the editorial draft, the last paragraph offers an analogy of a croaking frog inviting death.35 But whereas the editorial draft and published texts end at that point, the recording transcript from the Bhaktivedanta Archives presents six more minutes of conversation. Of note, it is after the frog metaphor that concludes the published editions that Bhaktivedanta Swami presented an overarching theme motivating his critique.

In the conversation that continued after the published account, Bhaktivedanta Swami expressed the view that no matter the degree that material science prided itself advanced, the basic elements of conditional experience remained intact. In particular, the quest for lasting youth remained elusive, while the death rate remained steady at one hundred percent. He also poked fun at those who felt material arrangements alone, could effectively counteract these persistent dilemmas of human experience.

Bhaktivedanta Swami next prodded his disciples: “So you all write very strongly, vibrantly... Yes. They’re misleading. Godlessness.” Please note Bhaktivedanta Swami spoke the word “vibrantly” in the audio recording (47:30 minute mark) and not “vehemently” as misheard by the transcribers.Bhaktivedanta Swami continued: “As soon as you say ‘God created,’ immediately they become arrogant. That is our protest.” With this, Bhaktivedanta Swami asserted his challenge to those who blatantly propagate atheism in the name of modern science. Still, Bhaktivedanta Swami also proposed, “If they accept God, then we give them all credit. That’s all right. Otherwise zero. We don’t deprecate their intention of advancement of knowledge.” He then concluded, “We simply protest against their defying the authority of God. That is our point.”

In this statement, Bhaktivedanta Swami focused his analysis on natural philosophy, rather than on the methodological standards of natural science. It is also interesting to note that within this protest, Bhaktivedanta Swami expressed a respect for scientific aspirations to advance in learning in his statement to his disciples, do not “deprecate their intention of advancement of knowledge.” Rather, he focused his critique specifically on an atheistic interpretation of natural philosophy that proposed necessary and certain knowledge for “defying the authority of God.” He argued that this was his “simple protest.” He further acknowledged that his disciples could cross a line when challenging this attitude frequently promoted in the name of professional science, “even \[if\] it is a little offensive.”

Copies of this audio recording are widely available. The following excerpt from the recording transcript for this section under consideration, yet missing from the published books, begins at 46:25 mark of the conversation recording:

*All these scientists, they discover so many things. Why they did not discover something that he would not die? He would not become old? Where is that discovery?*

*They will say: “Yes, in future.” \[But\] one man is kicking on your face \[now\], and you are saying: “Yes, in future, when I shall become strong, I shall kick him \[back\].” But you are, my dear sir, being kicked now. What you are doing now? “Yes, I'm getting strength by your kicking.”*

*So you all write very strongly, vibrantly. Even it is little offensive, still these rascals should be taught good lesson. Yes. They're misleading. Godlessness. As soon as you say, “God created,” immediately they become arrogant. That is our protest.*

*If they accept God, then we give them all credit. That's all right. Otherwise zero. We don't deprecate their intention of advancement in knowledge. But we simply protest against their defying the authority of God. That is our point*.

In this selection, Bhaktivedanta Swami twice used a phrase with the word “protest,” and then concluded with the phrase “That is our point.” This statement appears to succinctly articulate Bhaktivedanta Swami’s protest against a philosophical attitude frequently promoted on the strength of the reputation of contemporary natural science. It is also useful to point out that this protest focused on metaphysical issues about which scientific methodology lacks disciplinary authority. In this exchange, it appears that Bhaktivedanta Swami’s challenge to modern science is specifically aimed at evangelical atheists propagating personal philosophical views on the strength of the professional reputation of the discipline.

And yet, this section is missing from the formal publications, produced first as a German edition based on the English recordings, and then as an English edition based on the liberal German translation, and intended for broad dissemination. When considering scholarly concerns involving the publications, it is worthwhile to consider the original recordings and archival transcripts as an integral resource necessary for developing a comprehensive appreciation of Bhaktivedanta Swami’s views. For that purpose, the published editions of Life Comes From Life fall short as a historically accurate account of how Bhaktivedanta Swami formulated his arguments concerning Gaudiya Vaisnavism, professional science, and the modern world.

The author found these documents on a website belonging to Nori Muster, the author of the exposī Betrayal of Spirit: My Life Behind the Headlines of the Hare Krishna Movement (1997). Muster described the circumstances by which she came to hold these documents in an email:

“After some time I decided to post the scans under the theory that something is better than nothing. That manuscript came to me because it was in the file cabinets at the ISKCON p.r. office in Los Angeles. I worked there for ten years, as described in my book. When our office broke apart in about 1987, everyone went in different directions. I ended holding the bag with all the files somehow. Some paper\[s\] I returned to people still involved in ISKCON, but the rest I kept. Eventually, I turned it all over to the J. Gordon Melton Collection at the UCSB Special Collections Library.”

Muster held the impression that the raw editorial manuscript was a transcription of the 1973 conversation recordings, as compared to a translation of the 1974 German edition, Leben Kommt Von Leben. She continues: “The LCFL manuscript shows how heavily they edited Srila Prabhupada’s dictation tapes. From my experience working with the p.r. department and L.A. BBT, they would make copies of manuscripts and pass them around to an editorial committee. In this case, I would guess they may have given a copy to Ramesvara, Radha-vallabha, Munkunda, and anyone else mentioned in the acknowledgements. The editing marks could have already been there when they made the copies. The copy I got was probably what was given to Mukunda, since the p.r. department was his office, and most of the paper in the files was his.” (Muster to Kreitzer, 22 March 2014).
