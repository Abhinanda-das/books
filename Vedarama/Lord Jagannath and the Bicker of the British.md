# Lord Jagannath and the Bicker of the British

## Jagannatha &amp; the British

During 19th Century

Jasmine Patnaik

From the mists of historical antiquity and through the aura of futurism and the fringes of eternity, the Sanatana Dharma engulfs the succulence of human existence. Millenia before the birth of Prophet Mohammed and before the supposed tutelage of Jesus in the Thera-vada institutions of West Asia set up by the emigrant Buddhist missionaries traversing from India to that part of the world through the Silk-Route, well before the times of Gautama Buddha, Socrates, Confucius and Plato and much before the word 'Hindu' has been coined the Sanatana Dharma pervaded the Indian scene asserting the unity of mankind and resolving all perplexities of existence through the mystic rhythm of its philosophical lores. Human body is made of perishable matter, but human consciousness transcends the time and space. This dichotomy and the mutual conflicts between the two serve as a fountain-head of all cultural dynamics. All arts, creativity and spirituality culminate from this source.

Though history has as yet, failed to determine the antiquity of the Jagannath Dharma with any amount of specificity or exactitude, the Puranic and Vedic mentions and references notwithstanding, the historical heritage of Purusottam Jagannath of the last twelve centuries reiterate that Jagannath Dharma has been a continuum over a cycle of centuries, if not millenia. Turning or returning to this holistic 'Sanatana' emblem called Jagannath Dharma amidst all its classical and archaic traditions becomes a moral imperative because it is as culture-specific as it is contemporary. The impatients who have tried in vain to confine it to a cult may cynically explain such a conclusion as a random reflection, though for any serious thinker or scholar a minor scrutiny would reveal the gross approximation of Jagannath to the Vedic concepts of Sanata Dharma. Its Primary concern is the well-being, a cerebral challenge in a positive manner - the 'is-ness' and now-ness' of life vis-a-vis the eternal. The way we think affects the way we feel. Cognitive traits develop as a result of cognitive distortions or thinking right. The legacy of the questioning of faith is inherent in Sanatan Dharma and answers are forthcoming as per the knowledge of the age, with a modicum of value - specificity and paradigms of pragmatism. Today, as it careens merrily down the rapids of modernity, what is simple and obvious is its supposed crowd of paradoxes, like the supernaturalism and irrationality of the era of humanity's childhood to the resilience of the faith to withstand and endorse the multi-faceted monolith of modernism.

Even as the modern secularist takes the oath in judicial courts in God's name, he deludes himself to believing that belief in God by the professed majority is cynical and pretentious. The discernible symptoms of such a crisis of conscience was ever reflected in the attitudes of the aliens and colonialists towards Jagannath, though it was not so in case of Benares or the institutions - galore of South India.

The European, particularly the **British** attitude to Jagannath offers an interesting study of oscillating attitudes ranging from awe and admiration to sequential disillusionment. Viewing it initially as an amorphous idol, they made it into a despicable metaphor - **Juggernaut** - but soon got boomeranged by it in their evangelical endeavours. Vilifying the immense importance of the car-festival of the Lord and ascribing to the festival a genocidal contour, they wrote from time to time about the pilgrims throwing "themselves under the chariot so that its wheels may go over them ...... And the car passes over them and crushes them and cuts them asunder, and so they perish on the spot." Conti even supplements by adding that to be "crushed to death" in such a manner was "a mode of death which they say is very acceptable to their god." Gasparo Balbi (c.1581) also repeats this preposterous nonsense. Gladwin is more objective in his description of the festival :

"In the town of Pursotem off the banks of the sea stands the temple of Jag naut, near to which are the images of Kishen, his brother, and their sister, made of sandal-wood, which are said to be 4000 years old ... The Brahmins ... at certain times carry the image in procession upon a carriage of sixteen wheels, which in the Hindooee language is called Rahth; and they believe that whoever assists in drawing it along obtains remissions of all his sins."

In Bruton's aspersions (of 1632) the same insularity of vision is reflected :

"Vnto this Pagoda or house of Satan ... doe belong 9000 Brammines or Priests, which doe dayly offer sacrifice vnto their great God Jaggarnat, from which Idoll the city is so called .... And when it (chariot) is going along the city, there are many that will offer themselves a sacrifice to this idoll, and desperately lye down on the ground, that the Chariot wheeles may runne over them, whereby they are killed outright; some get broken armes, some broken legges, so that many of them are destroyed and by this meanes they thinke to merit Heauen." Bernier competes with Bruton in describing the festival in despicable terms :

"In the town of Jagannat, which is seated upon the Gulf of Bengala, and where is that famous Temple of the Idol of the same name, there is yearly celebrated a certain Feast .... The first day that they shew this Idol with Ceremony in the Temple, the crowd is usually so great to see it, that there is not a year, but some of those poor pilgrims that come afar off, tired and harassed, are suffocated there; all the people blessing them for having been so happy ... And when this Hellish Triumphant Chariot marcheth, there are found (which is no Fable) persons so foolishly credulous and superstitious as to throw themselves with their bellies under those large and heavy wheels, which bruise them to death."

And Hamilton echoed, in 1727, in the following vein :

"His (Jagarynat's) Effigy is often carried abroad in procession, mounted on a coach four stories high .... they fasten small ropes to the cable, two or three Fathoms long, so that upwards of 2000 people have room enough to draw the coach and some old Zealots, as it passes through the street, fall flat on the ground, to have the honour to be crushed to pieces by the coach-wheels."

The poem "Curse of Kehama" (XIV 5), in its rapturous ecstacy of ignorance, sings, in 1809, "The ponderous car rolls on, and crushes all, through flesh and bones it ploughs its dreadful path. Groans rise unheard; the dying cry." The loss of three lives in such manner during the car-festival of 1814 as reported in Asiatic Journal of 1814 is indicative of a tendency to make a mountain of an issue out of its mole-hill proportions. Analogically, not a whimper of any national protest was ever heard in England when thousands of **British** soldiers would die over the issue of a key to a church in Turkey in 1854. Poynder in the E.I. Court of proprietors session of 1827, would persist with such castigations about the car festival; Forster and Renan would persist with this derogatory description in their own individual styles.

Thus, the much-maligned issue of the car-festival, started by Sebastian Manrique in 1636, continued as a harangue and against the backdrop of abortive attempts at evangelization at **Puri** by the Christian missionaries, came to give them a sadistic sense of "hellish triumph". Stirling, the Collector of **Puri**, wrote in 1818 to say that such so-called "excess of fanaticism" reflected in and through instance of immolation were too few far between and that invariably these were results of accident or euthanasia. Chevers would endorse such a view. This naturally would give credence to the view about "that ecstatic mockery of martyrdom" as figments of the alien flights of fancy. If that be the case then the barbarity of commercializing the temple and its traditions in the name of seeking insulation from pollutions of superstitions is as preposterous as it is mock rhetoric. Perhaps a pseudo-reformatory zeal for missionary purposes lay at root, which raised a hullabaloo about frivolous accidents but found soon, to its dismay, that the Jagannath-culture is not so fragile as to succumb to such susceptibilities and insinuations.

A few of such insinuations galore can be mentioned. Jagannath was described in an editorial as the 'Moloch' of India. Moloch is a semite God who accepts child-sacrifice with glee. Woodhouse, the English novelist,is no exception to this flippancy when he says, "I felt like some unfortunate Hindu beneath the wheels of **Juggernaut**." Robert Southey's fictional poem on Kaiyal paints an equally ghastly sight. David Smith's report of 1847 on "Pilgrimage to **Juggernaut**" would talk of such suicide in moments of maddening excitement', but the bubble of gross exaggeration as inflated by Reverend Ward was pricked by the investigating Commissioner, A.J. Mills. Prior to Mills, W.W.Hunter, Collector of **Puri** had also done the same. Some of them even described Jagannath as" a figure that resembled nothing in the heavens above or the earth beneath, or in the waters under the earth. This had impelled Maj. Thorn to visit the place and see the amiable object of veneration by millions of pilgrims, though he found the deity "disgusting".

To Laurie, Orissa was a land of superstitions; to Kaye, the "real character of Hindooism was repulsive. Way back in June 1806, Rev. Claudius Buchanan had written about the "atrocities" at **Puri**, This clergy had even suggested then for the establishment of a Christian institution to undermine the impact of Jagannath. In 1813 the wolf came out of the sheep's clothing when he wrote to the court of Directors to draw their attention to the promising prospects for evangelization and conversion "into the pure religion of Jesus Christ" most of the "heathens" who gather for the "annual carnival" of car-festival in 1823 a thousand copies of the Gospel, translated to Oriya were distributed by William Bampton and James Peggs. The effort drew a blank. Reinforced though in their ranks by Charles Lacey and Amos Sutton, they could hardly make any inroads. Bampton's pamphlet, "Folly of the Worship of Jagannath" only brought the initially promising but finally spiteful proposition of Sadhu Sundar Das. The missionaries had forgotten that it is better to be irresponsibly right than responsibly wrong.

The **British** had come to know about **Puri** before they had developed any idea about Orissa. This city was famous for its temple and, so "The Chief City (IS) called Jekanat" (Sir T.Roe, Hak. Soc, ii, 1616, 538). The **British**, nation of shop-keepers' as Napoleon has rightly described them, 'true to our national character' settled in Orissa "as merchants long before we made our appearance as rulers", wrote Hunter. The 'district of Orissa' was an unnecessary buffer between Calcatta and Madras. But in their marches through Orissa in 1781 and 1790 they had come to be acquainted with Orissa's topography as well as the genial nature of its people for whom Jagannath was the be-all and end-all of life. So Wellesley's militancy against the Maratha power in Orissa was anachronistically punctuated by a policy to "conciliate the inhabitants"; and he admonished Lt. Col. Campbell to "employ every precaution to preserve the respect due to the pagoda and to the religious prejudices of the Brahmini and Pilgirims". The institution of Jagannath being integral to all aspects of Orissan life, they came to adopt a maticulous policy whereby they would neither give up advantages nor retain disadvantages. They came to abolish the 'Pilgrim Tax' after the oscillations and vacillations as reflected in Charles Grome's Report of 1805 and George Webb's report of 1807. The pilgrim Tax, purportedly "a state sanction to idolatry" in John Richardson's words, was also abolished under the pressure of the Christian missionaries for the obvious reason that part of its surplus was allotted to them for their use. They fixed the expenditure of the temple at Ar Rs.56342.9 per annum, provided for the government to bear it as well as the 484 yards of cloth per annum from the government's warehouse and in return, the government to collect land revenue of the temple-lands. This was approved by Governor General Minto on 5 Feb. 1808. They decided to vest proprietory rights on the Raja of Khurda who was now to act as the superintendent of the temple and relinquished direct responsibility in the administration of the Temple, though they were prepared to lend "as much support of the temple as the Marathas had done". The self-alienation of the government from temple-administration was largely accentuated by missionaries disavowal of any government subsidy to the temple. One such example is that of the London Missionary Society and Free Church of Scotland.

What had irked the Christian Missionaries about Jagannath was that here, at **Puri**, "is the national temple whither the people flock for worship from every province of India. Here is the Swarga Dwara, the Gate of Heaven where thousands of pilgrims come to die, lulled to their last sleep by the roar of the eternal ocean. The sight of singular devotion to a single god by millions who are unwavered in their allegiance in spite of the best efforts of the missionaries rendered the perspective of mirage to the missionaries. Grapes became sour and disillusioned the likes of Peggs and Bampton. Bampton's "forlorn hope" to plant the banner of the Cross within the precincts of **Juggernaut's** temple is vengefully vented by Peggs to whom "**Juggernaut**, the grat, the obscene, the bloody **Juggernaut**, must fall; Long perhaps will be the struggle and fierce the conflict but he must fall; and the place which knows him now will know him no more for ever." History scoffs at Peggs today when, in the heart of his country, thousands throng to celebrate a miniature replica, Lord Jagannath's car-festival and the Lord seems to permeate the will and intellect of an ever-increasing number of his followers in the Anglo-Saxon and Teutonic races. It seems the missionaries outnumbered the converts. Stray are instances of conversion, as of 'Lokhun' Das who tore his sacred-thread to declare his conversion. While the list of missionary's along with the 'date of their embarkation' is quite a long one. The missionaries skepticism found a scape-goat in the government; "if the government does not forsake **Juggernaut** how can you expect that we should ? , was someone's curt reply to Bampton and the acrimony was the result of a vision blurred, perhaps, by the crocodile tears over the issue. The spectacle of thousands thronging the car-festival irrespective of caste-class distinctions paled into obscurity the Christian missionary message that God was the father of all mankind and that therefore there was a brotherhood of man. So lamented Sutton, out of typical Christian disillusionment, "your prayers can not pierce this gloom nor your labours open an avenue sufficient to let down a ray of heaven's light on this idolatrous province."

Mention here may be made of the fact that not all of the **British** lacked an objective approach to the issue. One such was Charles Buller, a Member of Parliament who was an ex-member of he Board of Revenue and Settlement Commissioner of Cuttack. His argument in the House of Commons was a bid "to correct the erroneous opinion which seems to prevail in regard to the conduct of our Government with reference to the Temple and to remove the exaggerated ideas entertained in respect of the atrocities said to be practised there." Many like Winternitz endorsed Buller's views and rejected it as too insignificant to be worth reckoning. Buller had witnessed only one case of self-immolation during the car- festival of 1809 at **Puri**. W.W. Hunter also brushes the alleged issues aside; on the contrary he waxed eloquent on the tradition at **Puri** of preventing widow-burning on her deceased husband's pyre much before the Government had contemplated on any such action.

Buchanan had described Orissa as "the wide and extended empire of the Moloch in the heathen world." Peggs was more candid in his admission, "Orissa presented a challenge to the missionaries since it contained the temple of Jagannath, the Mecca of Hindustan, the Sebastapol of Hindu idolatry" (J.Peggs, A Brief Sketch of the Rise and Progress of Cuttack, London, 1854, p-4). The strong bond of "Mahaprasad brotherhood" among the Oriyas was deliberated upon in the **British** Parliament in January, 1873. In 1817, Robert Ker, in his report, described the Oriyas as rude and ignorant in the eyes of the **British**. Wilkins (Hindu Mythology) had described Jagannath- **Puri** as "one of the greatest religious centres of the world." Charles Grome regarded it as "probably the most celebrated temple in India to which resort pilgrims from all parts of the country" (Collector of Pilgrim Tax to Board of Revenue : 11th Nov.1806, JTC Pt-I). Dunn refers to the temple as "Jagrenot Pagoda" (Diary of Dunn, 1780) while Reynolds says "the **Juggernaut** Pagoda is an excellent sea mark on a coast which exhibits continued sameness." Be that as it may, "Pooree is the principal seat of Hindooism" (Collector of **Puri** to Commissioner, 31 December 1838, JTC Pt.II).

An interesting aspect of the Temple- administration by the East India Company was the issue of the Pilgrim Tax. The rates of the tax, as continued from the preceding periods, were stream-lined by Grome (1805) and authenticated by Webb (1807) as Collector before submitting for approval to Lord Minto, the then Governor General. The collection of the tax was decried by the missionaries as wages of idolatry which should be stopped. But when this principal source of income was stopped and the government decided to subsidize the annual expenditure of the Temple, the same missionaries criticised it as something the Government had not done after the repeal of 'Corn Laws' in England. The Company government was, thus in manger over the administration of the temple from time to time due to 'apposition for opposition sake' attitude of the missionaries.

Obstinacy had denied the **British** the realisation that the Jagannath-triad was no mere illustration of objects known from nature but a divine presence that is beyond the frontiers of ordinary experience. The central theme of the Jagannath Dharma is that the macrocosm manifest in the Triad is the same as the microcosm or the individual self. This cumulative Upanishadic substance deified in the triad and the transcendental state of gnosis intrinsic to it were beyond the cane of evangelical comprehension. The obsidian eyes continue to notoriously glitter without a flicker !
