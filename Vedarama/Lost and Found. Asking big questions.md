# Lost and Found. Asking big questions

## Introduction

Over the course of the last forty years, people have asked me many questions about spiritual life, particularly the subject of bhakti-yoga. I have collected their questions here, together with my answers, and I hope you find them useful.

The first section is more general questions on eastern philosophy; the second more about the bhakti tradition and culture; and the third covers the practical aspects of spiritual life in today's world.

Presented first as three small volumes, they are bound together in this digital edition.

## Volume 1: Asking big questions

*Since the beginning of time, philosophers, theologians and scientists have endeavoured the understand the world in a more profound way. We look up at the stars and wonder what lies beyond. When did it all begin? What happens when I die? Why does love never seem to last? Why do bad things happen to people who are good and decent? Explore the fresh perspectives, insights and answers from the Krishna tradition.*

## Krishna Wisdom

*For as far back as we can remember, philosophers, theologians, scientists and great thinkers have discussed and debated with a view to understanding the world in a more profound way. The sages of the East were no different. In the ancient body of literature known as the Vedas, they documented a spiritual understanding of the self, the universe and the deeper purpose of life. This is ‘Krishna Wisdom’ – essential principles that underpin universal reality.*

**The Krishna people talk of philosophy. What do you mean by philosophy?**

Philosophy means ‘the love of knowledge’ and it’s a rational investigation of what’s true and what’s not. Philosophy is about solving problems and creating greater happiness through better principles of living. Something is philosophy when the thought process involves the removal of illusion or deception, and the discovery of a higher meaning of life. The word is often used to describe the search for an ideal way of relating to others, or of discovering an ultimate reality.

**Surely practical action is more important than philosophy for solving the world’s problems?**

You’re right that change in the world takes place because of action, especially when people join together in collective action. But even before action there must be a value system on which we base our opinions and choices of action. Life is given meaning when we decide what is most important to us. Philosophy is the search for what is the most important, and therefore the basis of any value system. When you know what you love, you can speak and act with conviction.

**So is Hare Krishna a philosophy, a spiritual path, a religion or a movement?**

I don’t want to confuse you, but it’s all of them. It is certainly a philosophy, because it contains a system of logical thought that analyses what knowledge actually is, the parameters of knowledge, how we ‘know’ something to be true or real, why we suffer and how we can experience a greater happiness, both individually and collectively. Philosophy leads to action and a sense of a goal to be reached through that action. Wherever you have both action and a goal, you have a path; and when the attainment of a spiritual goal is at the end, you have a spiritual path. When you join together with others to follow certain practises based on your common philosophical ideas you can describe that as a religion. And when that helps to bring about social change and an improvement in people’s lives you could describe it as a movement. So it’s a ‘yes’ to all of them.

**I like the idea of a spiritual path, but I don’t like religion.**

Religion without philosophy is just an unsubstantiated belief. It can easily descend into sentimentality or fanaticism. But philosophy without religion is mental speculation, and easily becomes dry intellectualism followed by inertia. You need both for balance. Religion needs an underpinning of rationalism, and philosophy needs to be expressed in ways that create happiness, morality, and an experience of a higher reality.

**You’re an eastern religion, right?**

It depends where you live. The concept of ‘eastern’ and ‘western’ religions date back to British Empire times. Back in the 1880s the world adopted a system of measuring longitude based on the calculations made by the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, near London. That meant that anything east of Greenwich became ‘East,’ and anything to the west of it became ‘West.’ As a consequence, China became the ‘Far East’ and Arabia became the ‘Middle East.’ The descriptions are purely notional – and based on the concept that London, at a notional zero degrees of longitude, is in the centre of the world. In China, for instance, Buddhism is known as a ‘western religion,’ since it came out of India, which is to the west of China. The world is a globe, so no-one owns the right to be called the centre of the world. True wisdom, like the sunshine, has no national, geographic or ethnic boundaries.

**OK, but it’s a religion originally from India?**

What we call India today was once known as Bharata, and its cultural influence once extended all the way from Persia through to Cambodia. Even in present day Greece and Russia, archaeologists are discovering artefacts dating back thousands of years to the Himalayan culture. India has seen the origins of many world religions, including Buddhism, Hinduism, Jainism and Sikhism. Some people even say that strands of ‘Hindu thought’ ended up in the writings of the Essenes, the community that wrote the Dead Sea Scrolls in what is now Israel. There was a lot of deep thinking in ancient India!

**So if Buddha is the founder of Buddhism, who’s the founder of Hare Krishna?**

Krishna’s conversation with Arjuna around 3,000 BC is one of the principal texts we follow. It’s known as the Bhagavad-gita , or ‘The Song of God.’ However, 5,000 years ago ‘Hare Krishna’ was already very old, since the same teachings go back thousands of years before that. The name for those teachings is The Vedas, which simply means ‘The Knowledge.’ Although many Himalayan sages preserved the knowledge by teaching it verbally to their disciples, who then taught it to their own disciples, and so on, it was eventually written down in the Sanskrit language by ‘the original guru’ whose name was Vyasa. He divided The Vedas into portions to make it easy for humanity to preserve.

**Do you have to read teachings that are so old – can’t you read modern spiritual books?**

Of course we can - writing about spirituality is not limited to ancient sages and prophets. There are many modern authors who write on spirituality. But almost all of them are drawing on the ancient wisdom for their inspiration, too. So why not begin with reading the spiritual classics? If people are still reading a book five thousand years after it was written, you could rightly conclude that it has passed the test of time, and that it contains perennial wisdom. Besides, the spiritual nature and experience of it is the same today as it ever was, so there’s really no such thing as ‘modern spirituality,’ only spirituality addressed to modern needs, in modern language.

**So what is your main book – your Krishna Bible?**

There’s more than one ‘Bible,’ actually, and they are all part of a vast library of ancient wisdom. The entire library is known as the Vedas, or ‘the knowledge,’ and the deeper, philosophical parts are known as the Upanishads . The word means ‘sit down closer.’ More than one hundred Upanishads contain a very pithy analysis of the difference between reality and illusion, and the Vedanta Sutra summarises and rearranges their essence into a logical sequence of arguments. The Bhagavad-gita is the record of a one-hour conversation between Krishna and His warrior friend and disciple Arjuna. It contains all the major concepts of the Upanishads in dialogue form and has been read by millions for thousands of years. Then there’s another book known as the Bhagavata Purana , or Srimad Bhagavatam . It’s both an ancient history book and an exposition of many different levels of reality. In 18,000 verses it analyses matter and the quantum of consciousness known as the atma , or inner self and further explores the reality of an unlimited, eternal world beyond the limits of our perception. All the Vedas offer knowledge of how we can be healthy and happy in this life, and be free from problems in the next.

**What is the relationship between Hinduism and Hare Krishna?**

Hinduism is a collective term for the religions of India. At one point in history, the land to the east of the River Indus became known as Hindustan and the people as Hindus. The British added the suffix ‘-ism’ creating the term Hindu-ism. Although the word is not found in any sacred text, the term Hinduism is now widely accepted. Hinduism is not one belief system but a family of religions with thousands of members. Just as all the branches of a large tree can be traced back to the trunk, so all branches of Hinduism can be traced to the ancient texts known as the Vedas.

The largest branch of Hinduism is known as Vaishnavism, which holds that God is one, an all-pervasive Deity and a supreme person, addressed as Vishnu or Krishna. Five thousand years ago Krishna spoke the Bhagavad-gita, and five hundred years ago reappeared as a great teacher named Sri Chaitanya. The members of Hare Krishna follow the teachings of the Gita, and the path of life taught by Chaitanya.

**What does Hare Krishna mean?**

Hare and Krishna (Hare is pronounced Huh-ray) are the first two words of a great mantra mentioned in the Vedas, and the mantra recited by Krishna people each day. It goes like this:

*Hare Krishna Hare Krishna*  
*Krishna Krishna Hare Hare*  
*Hare Rama Hare Rama*  
*Rama Rama Hare Hare*

Krishna means ‘all attractive,’ and Hare means ‘the eternal energy.’ The other word is Rama (rhymes with drama) which means ‘supreme pleasure.’ A mantra is a sound formula with the potency to uplift and enlighten those who repeat it. The word is derived from mana (the mind) and trayate (to protect and free). The ultimate reality is described in the Vedas by many names, and Hare, Krishna and Rama are three of those names. When the first Americans and Europeans saw the devotees of Krishna in the 1960s they termed them the ‘Hare Krishna Movement’ or ‘The Hare Krishnas,’ and the name has stuck.

**Why do you sing in public?**

Because the mantra elevates the mind and allows anyone to tap into the natural, spiritual happiness that lies within them. So it’s good to share. Even if someone doesn’t understand the language of the mantra or its meaning, there’ll be a beneficial spiritual effect. When a mantra is recited softly it benefits only the person who’s repeating it, but when the mantra is said out loud it can benefit anyone who hears it. If the mantra is sung to a melody then the recitation is known as kirtan , and when sung in a public place, the mantra singing becomes sankirtan. Musical kirtan is an enjoyable way to meditate – even in the middle of a busy street!

**How does a mantra work?**

Nowadays the word mantra has been borrowed by journalists to describe any repetitive slogan or phrase. They sometimes talk of a politician’s ‘mantra,’ meaning a much repeated claim on the campaign trail. But it’s not just any word that can be a mantra. A mantra is an authentic word or phrase taken only from the Vedas. It has intrinsic spiritual power, and when employed correctly, at a time of deep meditation or prayer, will have the desired effect. The repetition of the mantra calms and quietens the mind, sets it free from unhelpful cycles of desire and aversion, and focuses it on transcendence. Through mantra meditation we rediscover our authentic self deep within the heart, and experience an awakening to joyful inner happiness. The fact that mantras work can be validated by the many thousands who chant them each morning. Genuine mantras are given freely, but come with a set of guidelines so that the maximum effect can be gained.

**Don’t you just zone out when you chant mantras so much?**

Not at all. There are so many ways to ‘zone out’ but meditation isn’t one of them. With digital technology, the opportunities for placing the mind in a semi-stupefied state have greatly increased. Virtual reality of different kinds has only resulted in a lack of clarity. The mind is made to oscillate between hankering for something in the future and lamenting for something lost in the past. Very rarely are we in the present reality. As a result, many people spend hours each day in unhelpful states of mental confusion. For people on a spiritual path, the present moment is extremely important since that is substantial. Mantra meditation places you in that present moment, and then transports you to a timeless state where the mind can be fully refreshed and enlivened. So actually, mantra meditation is a ‘zoning in.’

**Don’t you lose your individuality by becoming a Hare Krishna?**

That’s a question we used to be asked thirty years ago when almost all of our members lived in communities – sometimes in the same house -and wore the same clothing. The success of the movement has meant that we have multiplied in number many times and the diversity in the lives of our membership has broadened as a consequence. These days, you would be hard-pressed to know who was a devotee of Krishna just by looking at them. Although the philosophy and theology of the movement are common to all, there is a striking variety of ethical and political opinions expressed by our members.

**What are your basic teachings?**

In a very small nutshell they are as follows: The soul is spirit and the body is matter. The body is a vehicle for the soul. The body is temporary but the soul endures. The soul is never born and never dies. It is unchanging, primeval and a spiritual spark of life within the body. The soul is conscious and blissful by nature. When the soul identifies with matter it mistakenly imagines that the body is the self. In its original state the soul is unlimitedly happy, but when it forgets its divine nature it struggles to achieve even temporary pleasure. The soul is looking for the happiness it once had but in the wrong place.

Just as the sunshine comes from the Sun, so the souls emanate from the supreme source. That ultimate reality is known in the Vedas as Brahman, Narayana or Krishna. That Godhead is not merely an impersonal energy, but the energetic source of all existence. The souls are infinite and dependant on Godhead, just as the rays depend on the Sun. God has attributes such as a form and beauty. God has compassion and love. All souls emanate from the Supreme Being and share His loving nature, as sparks emanating from a fire share, in a very small degree, the fire’s heat and light. Some souls leave their relationship with the Supreme to experience life apart in the world of matter.

Over countless births, layers of psychic conditioning cover their remembrance of their original nature and the hearts of these forgetful or ‘conditioned’ souls grow hard. By hearing the spiritual messages such as in the Bhagavad-gita, and applying them in their lives, the hearts of those souls will once again become soft and they will return to their original home with the Supreme.

**Isn’t the Supreme Being you speak of more of a spiritual oneness we merge into?**

A great sage once said that ‘when a green bird flies into a green tree it may look as if it has disappeared, but it remains an individual bird.’ The idea of merging into a divine substance is a popular idea. Individuality seems to come along with anxiety and pain, and things don’t improve when we share our lives with others - relationships can be a major cause of disappointment.

So when we think of existential freedom, the idea of a divine white light seems immediately attractive. But freedom from problems isn’t the highest happiness, it’s merely a negation. Real spiritual positivity lies in discovering our true spiritual individuality and enjoying it, and that can be best done by experiencing the bliss of our original relationship with the Supreme.

**Buddhism speaks of a nirvana, an extinction of all desire and consequently the illusion of individuality. What do you say to that?**

The Sanskrit term nirvana first appears in the Vedic literature, which pre-date Buddhism, so its meaning was originally something slightly different. Vana means ‘wind’ as in being pushed around on a gusty day, or of waves on the sea. Nir means a cessation of, so nirvana means ‘no more wind.’

The word is used to describe the freedom from being pushed around by the winds of selfish desire and rebirth in material existence. But it is grammatically negative and, by itself, the word does not signify a positive state beyond the vana . So in the Bhagavad-gita, where the word appears five times, it is prefaced by the word Brahma, a supremely positive word meaning the absolute platform of spirit. Krishna explains that brahmanirvana means not an impersonal absolute but a mystical union of the soul in devotion, where both God and the individual soul retain their individuality.

## Spirit Matters

*In a world where short-lived physical enjoyment is the goal, and accumulation of material goods the means, we should expect that people are at least happy some of the time. Yet the reverse seems true – restlessness and frustration are all too common and the world now seems unable to produce sufficient resources to support our increasingly materialistic lifestyle. Our existence as spiritual beings is an idea that stands in need of investigation. If true, our happiness may lie just beyond the range of the senses, and meditation may just provide the means to achieve it.*

**What do you mean by the word ‘soul?’**

The word ‘soul’ is an English version of the Sanskrit word atma , which means ‘the self.’ The atma is the source of consciousness in the body. The body is a complex arrangement of chemicals, none of which are conscious. The body is the unconscious, external covering of the conscious soul.

**Isn’t the brain the source of consciousness?**

The brain is an organ of soft nervous tissue contained in the skull and it functions as the coordinating centre of sensation and nervous activity. But since scientists cannot adequately explain how unconscious chemicals become conscious, they admit that consciousness, as a phenomenon, must be considered separately from the functions of the brain. That is why ‘Consciousness Studies’ has now become a distinct area of scientific research.

It has been shown that consciousness has the ability to observe distinct, remote phenomena without the intermediate brain and senses. In laboratory experiments, detailed drawings and designs were viewed and reproduced by the subjects at a distance of several miles from their targets. The results all point to a conscious observer that exists outside the brain. No one is quite sure how that works, but the phenomenon has been investigated for two decades now.

**So you’re saying that the soul is consciousness?**

Consciousness is a symptom of the soul, and the soul is conscious. The soul is the life within the body, and its power pervades the entire body. The soul is the ultimate level of the self and the real perceiver of the world. The soul enters a bundle of cells at conception and when it departs from the body – when the soul and the body separate - we call that death.

**Does everyone have a soul?**

Actually, it’s the other way round: you don’t have a soul. Rather, you are the soul and you have a body. You’re not a human being that has spiritual experiences; you’re a spiritual being that has human experiences. But to answer your question – yes, everyone is a soul within a body, including every animal and plant.

**What real difference does it make if I think of myself as a soul?**

If it’s true then it makes an enormous difference. Thinking of yourself as something you’re not is unhelpful, especially in the long term. It’s like turning left when you should have turned right: every mile further down the road you go you’re more lost. If you are actually much higher than the physical body and mind then you won’t be able to find physical pleasure satisfying. You’ll always be looking for something more, buying more and working harder to get money to buy it. With limited resources on the planet we could all do with less stuff – and more real happiness. So thinking of yourself as a soul could immediately help you and help the planet.

**How would you define ‘real’ happiness?**

When the pleasure is material it’s temporary, it has a beginning and an end, and it results in frustration. Because of this, material pleasure can never lead to a sense of fulfilment. We’ll always be left wanting more – and that’s a shame, because there’s a much better way to be happy. The Bhagavad-gita says that we’ll be happier enjoying a permanent pleasure that comes from deep within and which doesn’t depend on external things.

**But I’m generally happy enough as it is. Why should I try for more?**

Happiness is something we experience in different amounts, and much of what we call ‘happiness’ is simply the temporary relief of suffering. That’s a poor form of happiness. But there’s more, a happiness so great that it goes beyond any delights of this world – an ocean of happiness compared to a puddle – wouldn’t you like to experience that? The

Bhagavad-gita offers the alternative: a world beyond our perception, a world in which we can experience something called ananda or ‘bliss.’

**OK, so how would I ‘transform’ my consciousness?**

It begins with meditation, the gradual drawing of our consciousness away from the mundane and temporary to the transcendent and infinite. Meditation can be done at any time and in any place, but the easiest method is to begin each day with a dedicated period of meditation.

**What is meditation?**

Meditation means to hold one thought within the mind for a period of time; to deeply contemplate it, even to become absorbed in it to the exclusion of all other thoughts. The original yoga texts say that the mind is constantly fluctuating from thought to thought. One thought leads to another, and another – a chain of thoughts. These thoughts come from sensory stimulation from our current environment, or they can come, unexpectedly, from incidents in our past lives. The total effect is like ripples on a lake of water. The yogis say that the bottom of the lake is where you have dropped a precious golden ring, but the constant waves of thought prevent you from seeing any deeper than the surface. Meditation stills the waves of thought and allows you to see your deeper self.

**Why is it important to meditate?**

Our life is a spiritual journey. That’s what we’re made for. It’s not just about getting educated, working, having a family and then dying. So every day we need to take a few small steps along that journey. That way, as life goes by, we’ll become spiritually awakened. But our tendency is to do things that really don’t help us on that journey. Our minds are always dragging us here and there and we fall prey to bad habits. Meditation helps us to be peaceful and brings our lower self – the mind and senses - under the control of the higher self – the soul. When that is done we lessen the risk of committing unhelpful actions and we become spiritually stronger.

**Should I sit quietly and do silent meditation?**

You can do that, of course. You can find a method of meditation that works for you. In an increasingly noisy world, where every bleep and ringtone is a clamour for your attention, there’s great value in just switching everything off and listening to the silence. Your mind needs a break sometimes. You might even want to extend your silence over a weekend in the country.

The sages say that the sound of birds is very good for helping the mind be peaceful. However, you may be interested to know that the classic texts on meditation don’t actually recommend silence as an active meditation. Mantras are said to give us access to the greatest silence: the transcendental platform that is illuminating, all-blissful and permanently free from noisy distractions. That’s why classical meditation mostly prescribes mantras. They are not ordinary sounds, and they lead the mind to the greatest peace.

**How does it work?**

There’s an old poem that says that your mind is like a mirror. You look in the mirror but it’s grimy from many layers of dust, placed there by your actions over many lifetimes. You can just about see your own reflection, but only very dimly. The mantra works by gradually cleaning your mind of all that dust, enabling you to see yourself as you truly are. That’s why the process is called self-realisation.

## Karma &amp; Samsara

*If each of us is a quantum of consciousness inside a steadily changing physical body, a spiritual passenger in a DNA-engineered vehicle, what exactly determines our personal rollercoaster ride along the way? The materialistic worldview would have it that we are all helpless victims of some inherited biological coding, and that everything else is random coincidence. But perhaps the karma of life is simply a science waiting to be discovered.*

**I’ve heard the word karma before, does it mean bad luck?**

The word karma means ‘action.’ Everything we do, for any reason, is known as karma . Every action creates a reaction known as karma-phala or ‘the result of action.’ When we experience the results of our actions it creates further desires, which makes us act again. So there is an endless chain of action-result-desire-action. This chain of karmas and karma-phalas can be either helpful or a hindrance, according to whether our actions are good or bad.

**What would you describe as a ‘bad’ action?**

If our words or actions give anxiety, pain, fear or misery to others; or if we take something that doesn’t belong to us, that’s bad action. It affects our present, because doing bad things to others lowers our spiritual focus, and it affects our future, too, because all our actions – both bad and good – come back to us sooner or later and we’ll have to deal with the consequences. Bad action is also when we act in any other way that spoils our chances of transforming our consciousness and achieving higher, spiritual happiness.

**You mean like bad karma?**

Both good actions and bad actions – good or bad karma – will come back to us in the form of karmic reactions. Good deeds come back to us as good reactions, and when that happens we’ll experience pleasure. Bad reactions come back to us as anxiety or pain. The long chain of good and bad reactions is known as samsara .

**But aren’t ‘good’ and ‘bad’ relative? Isn’t it OK if we all just follow the laws of the country we live in?**

There are variations of good and bad, for sure. Driving on the left-hand side of the road is certainly a bad deed in the USA and will get you arrested, while over here it won’t. It’s not a matter of opinion. Similarly, having several wives is considered a crime in Great Britain, while in other countries it’s not. But there is a universal right and wrong, good and bad, that is understood the world over. That is because the laws of karma are universal and don’t always resemble man-made laws or popular opinions of what is good and bad. Like the law of gravity, these laws of karma are universally applicable.

**How do we know what are bad and good karmas?**

They are listed everywhere in the Vedas. For instance, a short list is given by the sage Manu : perseverance ( dhriti ), forgiveness ( kshama ), self control ( dama ), sanctity or purity ( saucam ), control of senses ( indriya - nigrah ), reason ( dhi ), knowledge or learning ( vidya ), truthfulness ( satya ) and non-violence ( ahimsa ). Lord Shiva echoes these and also adds the cultivation of compassion ( daya ), generosity ( dana ), and austerity ( tapasya ).

Another great yogi, Patanjali Muni, adds: ‘do not covet what others have’ ( aparigraha ), ‘do not steal anything’ ( asteya ), ‘cultivate satisfaction with what you have’ ( santosh ), ‘serve a teacher of sacred lore’ ( guru - sevanam ), and ‘surrender to God’ ( ishvara -pranidhan ). In the Bhagavad-gita Sri Krishna further explains that intense desire, greed and anger are the three gates that lead to darkness. To understand what a bad action is, you just imagine the exact opposite of any of the good actions. For instance, the opposite of forgiveness is vengeance, and the opposite of self-control is self-indulgence and so on. Good deeds are collectively known as dharma and bad deeds as adharma.

**That’s a long list! Is it possible to be such a good person?**

Bad actions begin with bad thoughts and feelings, and good actions begin with good thoughts and feelings, so it all begins with cultivating good thoughts and avoiding the bad ones. And holding on to good thoughts throughout the day means that you have to begin the day with good thoughts. That’s why people start the day with meditation. If you meditate before breakfast, and before you start work, you’ll have a very good chance of thinking, feeling, willing and acting good all day. It is said: ‘The mind is a very good servant, but a very bad master,’ so being in control of your mind, and not allowing it to wander as it tends to do, is essential.

**Do you believe in more than one life?**

Yes. If we have not exhausted our stock of karmic reaction before the end of one life, we take birth again and continue in another life. This is sometimes known as transmigration of the soul, or reincarnation. So the soul passes through one life after another. The soul is the passenger in the body, and the body is the vehicle. The soul remains the same but the bodies change.

In each life the soul passes through successive stages of birth, youth, old age, dwindling and death, and each time the soul mistakenly identifies with the body. In one life the soul thinks: “I am a man” or “I’m quite rich,” and in the next “I am a woman and a mother, and this is my husband.” In yet another, the soul thinks: “I am a horse,” and “I quite like this grass.” The soul may go down through many species of life before coming up again to the human body. It is like being pinned to a wheel, a wheel of changing bodies - a revolving wheel of reincarnation.

**So you believe that a man can become a horse in his next life?**

A soul can enter into the body of any species of life, according to the activities of his previous life. It is not that a man becomes a horse, merely that the soul temporarily inhabits the body of a horse. The soul never becomes a horse – the soul remains the same in every life. If the soul has performed a lot of good karma, then that soul may even take his next birth as a god in a heavenly region. But the human life is the crossroads where you decide where you want to go – and you must choose to act accordingly.

**I still don’t see why I would even want to become a horse though – or anything else. Can’t I just stay as me?**

You’re still you! In every lifetime you’re always you. You never change, and you’ll never be anyone else apart from you. Think of it this way: you wear a suit to work, and then you come home and change into something casual. You are not your clothes, you’re the person inside. Despite having changed your appearance you remain the same. Or another example: you drive an old, cheap car, and then you sell it and buy a new and more expensive model. You were the driver of the old vehicle, and now you drive the new one – but you remain the same person. Similarly you, the soul, is inside a vehicle made of flesh and blood. It lasts for some time, then you get a new one – but you always remain the same person.

**Why can’t I remember my last life – or any life?**

Sometimes it’s difficult to remember what we were doing yesterday, what to speak of many years ago. But the trauma of being squeezed through a birth canal has an effect on us, as does the identities provided by those around us, such as parents, siblings, relatives, teachers and other adults. Forgetfulness is relatively easy in such conditions, and our identification with our new body, and the drives to experience pleasure and avoid pain, all conspire to lock us in tight to this new physical and mental package. Sometimes children do remember their past life, usually before the age of eight, but if the parents don’t accept the reality of a previous existence the child also rejects its memories as nothing more than a dream.

**If I can’t remember my previous life, what can I learn from it?**

We may not always have recollections of specific incidents, but the aggregate of all previous experiences is accumulated in the form of impressions. This is known as samskara and creates strong tendencies in our present life. There are abilities passed from life to life, such as an aptitude for an area of knowledge or a particular physical skill. Then there are irrational fears that get transmitted due to previous life trauma. Thus one person appears wonderfully blessed as a child prodigy while another is crippled by an unexplained phobia. So although the learning does not come in the form of individual lessons learned from recollected incidents, it comes as unconscious internalised learning.

**How can anyone be sure there’s life after death?**

These days there is enough anecdotal evidence to suggest that consciousness can exist outside the brain. People are regularly reporting out-of-body experiences while under anaesthetic or at times of physical trauma. Experiments conducted at research laboratories suggest that ‘remote viewing’ - the ability to view detailed information at a distance of many miles - is a scientific fact. Other researchers have delved into accounts of ‘past lives’ from around the world. People have been sharing clear memories from their previous life, then having those facts checked – even in other countries. Most of these people did not believe in reincarnation, and had no religious affiliation. If consciousness can exist outside the brain, it can survive the ‘death’ experience.

**What’s at the end of the chain of lives?**

Each life is like a classroom in a long learning experience. Ultimately, the soul must learn all the lessons from an almost limitless number of experiences over thousands of lifetimes. The cumulative effect of this is that the soul becomes exasperated with repeated attempts to find lasting happiness in the world of matter. Only when this material exhaustion takes place does the soul begin to explore an alternative method of achieving a deep and lasting satisfaction. This gradual search for moksha , or freedom, is known as yoga. The philosophy taught by Sri Krishna in the Bhagavad-gita recommends a path of dedicating all one’s actions to the supreme as the most efficient means of speedily perfecting the human form of life.

**Yoga - is that a kind of meditation, or a set of postures?**

The Sanskrit word yoga means ‘to link’ and describes activities by which someone can awaken perception of their spiritual nature, and through that, bring about their gradual awareness of God. Since everyone has different inclinations and abilities, there are several kinds of yoga. Action-yoga is for the physically active; knowledge-yoga is for the scholarly; meditation-yoga is for the mystical. The Sanskrit terms for these are karmayoga, jnana-yoga and dhyana-yoga . The physical postures and breathing exercises taught as yoga in many modern cities is a part of dhyana-yoga .

**Which one do you practise?**

In the Bhagavad-gita the different forms of yoga are all described, compared and contrasted. Ultimately, Krishna sums everything up by describing a form of yoga that delivers the best results of all three. It is known as the ‘most secret of all secrets’ and ‘the perfection of yoga’ and is known as Bhakti-yoga. Bhakti means a life where the practitioner orients life so that the Absolute Truth is in the centre. A gradual awareness of one’s relationship with the supreme is achieved at the same time as self-realisation takes place.

The liberated soul is one who has reawakened the original love of the soul and this journey culminates in a deep feeling of being loved in return . This perfection of yogic practise is cultivated by the different aspects of mantra meditation, pure habits, study, ritual and personal disciplines. Bhakti-yoga thus means linking with God through all actions, gradually leading on to an uninterrupted consciousness of Krishna, or God.

**Does the soul have a form?**

The soul has an eternal form and identity, and a relationship with Krishna, but in the conditioned state the soul forgets this identity. Sometimes it is said that the soul in this forgetful period is only present in a ‘seed form’ and as it progresses it regains its identity. Other saints have explained that the individual soul in this forgetful stage has none of its eternal characteristics, and that they must again be given by Krishna in response to the devotion of the soul.

**What is the soul doing in this world?**

Souls possess free will, and that means they can decide to love God or to turn away from Him. God never forces the souls to love Him, for to do so would nullify their free will and render the love null and void. It is a fact that the souls within this world are in a state of rebellion against God, having used their minute free will to reject His company. Becoming enamoured with this world, they are born again and again until they realise that their true home lies elsewhere.

**What happens if you die and there’s actually nothing there?**

In modern western philosophy, betting that God actually exists because it involves less risk is called Pascal’s Wager. The argument is named after the seventeenth century French mathematician Blaise Pascal (1623-62) who said that every one of us is in fact betting that either God exists or that He doesn’t. He urges us to bet that He does, since if we do, and He does exist, it will lead to eternal life. We will have led a good life and only have lost a little income or perhaps some personal pleasure along the way. But if we use our life in denial of Him - in effect betting that He doesn’t exist - we may lose everything in the life after this one.

## Logic of the Absolute

*There is a notion that belief in God is intellectually immature or philosophically naïve, and that what we call religion is nothing more than the enactment of ritual and mythology. Faith in a transcendent reality is routinely challenged by those who claim that God is no longer relevant. The market has become flooded with elaborately worded and stylishly packaged books propounding the death of religion. Such propositions have entered mainstream culture without much robust challenge, heavily influencing the unassuming and trusting layman.*

*Spirituality, the Vedic teachers remind us, has an entirely logical and scientific basis. Whereas some spiritual paths culminate in faith, Bhagavad-gita explains that faith is only the beginning of one’s journey to Krishna. As one undertakes spiritual practices with enthusiasm, patience and determination, we can begin to directly perceive the spiritual reality. From the hypothesis we undertake the experiment, and eventually we arrive at an observation and conclusion.*

**You mentioned Krishna or God – do I need to believe in God to do yoga?**

‘God’ is an old English word coming from a German word Gute , meaning ‘that which is good.’ Ultimately it comes from the Sanskrit word Huta , which means ‘to whom we offer.’ Usually, when we say the word ‘God’ the image of the aged man with a white beard appears in our mind. That’s an artistic image but not a concept that stands up to philosophical discussion.

God is infinite and eternal, the controller of everything, and both the transcendent and the indwelling witness within us. God is the entirety of all existence, the origin of all but one who has no other origin. There are many names of God, in all the languages of the world, but ultimately God is one. Real religion, the process that helps us to be improved versions of ourselves, is not man-made but comes from the ultimate Source. So yoga and the concept of an ultimate personal God have always been intrinsically related. The author of the ancient yoga text Yoga Sutra , sage Patanjali Muni, says emphatically that the culmination of the yoga path is Ishvara-pranidhan or ‘surrender to God.’ Yoga actually means ‘union with God.’

**But why do I ultimately need a religion? Can I not just be a good person?**

Theoretically you don’t need a religion. You can be a good person without religion. But how would you know what is good and what is bad? You would have to sit with others and come to a joint decision as to what is good and bad. And this might change from one year to the next, as we often see. The result is that both good and bad become arbitrary values. It is hard to derive lasting virtues from endless discussions. The net result is that both virtue and vice become meaningless terms. That is why most cultures derive their understanding of ethics and morals from a scripture that is either God-given or God-inspired.

Even if there is no such thing as God, there is a great value in religious scripture because the moral principles contained within it have endured within human society and have been proved of great value to millions of people, generation after generation. And once you agree to share common values with others, and strive together to follow them, you have a religion of sorts, even though you wanted to avoid it.

**In your opinion, what should everyone be striving for?**

According to the Vedas, the goals of a progressive and civilised life are to follow moral and ethical principles (dharma), to work according to one’s occupational duties in order to secure everything necessary for health and happiness (artha), to enjoy the legitimate pleasures which flow from these (kama), and to move towards spiritual emancipation (moksha). The Puranas state that further than this, everything should be done in a spirit of service to God. When human beings fail to do this, life is ‘simply a waste of time’.

**How can we know that God exists?**

You can be convinced intellectually by studying the logical arguments given to explain His existence, but these in themselves are not proof. You won’t know for sure. You could be swayed by the attractive emotional features of a religious path, or be moved by the sentiments expressed by others, or the good works generated by those who say they believe in God. But you still won’t know for sure. The only real way is through your direct experience. Ultimately, it is only when you have direct experience of God that you can say that you ‘know.’ But along the way, you can feel your progress and receive assurance from Him that you are on the right path.

**But if God exists, why doesn’t He just show Himself to everyone?**

He is already showing Himself to you – but you need help to see Him. Don’t be surprised if you can’t see everything with your two eyes. For example, your brain exists – but you’ve never seen it. You have faith that it’s there, but you can’t actually prove it. The MRI machine at your local hospital can show it to you, of course, but with your unaided eyes you can’t prove that your own brain exists. So it’s more a question of your ability to see God rather than His willingness to show Himself.

**It would solve the question if He showed Himself to us though, wouldn’t it?**

The Queen exists, doesn’t she? But she reserves the right to show herself to whoever she pleases. You’ll need an invitation and an appointment to go and see her – you can’t just turn up and expect to see her. Similarly, the Upanishads state that: “The Supreme Reality reveals Himself to whom He chooses.”

**Why does God only show Himself to a select few?**

He shows Himself to everyone. No-one gets left out. God doesn’t have any chosen people or favourite tribes or countries. There’s no race or ethnicity that is specially blessed, and no particular religion, either. God is not a Christian, Jew, Muslim, Hindu, Sikh, Jain or Buddhist. He has no religion Himself, but is always ready to give Himself completely to whoever is interested, no matter who they are. In the Bhagavad-gita it is described that everyone – all souls – are on exactly the same spiritual path. The difference is in how much they choose to show themselves to God. It is not God that is not ready to show Himself, but we ourselves.

**Isn’t religion just a psychological crutch?**

If you mean to ask: ‘isn’t religion just a product of the human mind and God a product of human hopefulness, an idea to help us when we feel weak,’ then the answer is no. When you feel hungry, you’re not imagining it; you are actually hungry. When you eat, you are not using a ‘psychological crutch,’ – you’re doing the right thing. It isn’t your imagination that creates your hunger - you actually do need to eat! Similarly, when we feel hungry for spiritual nourishment we reach out for spiritual food. It is an entirely legitimate need – because spiritual nourishment is real food for the soul. The proof of this is that when we have it in our life we feel our spiritual hunger disappear, we enjoy spiritual feelings, and we become spiritually nourished. So although traditional religion may not be enjoying popularity, people still have spiritual needs. They become ‘spiritual’ instead of ‘religious,’ and may choose to refer to themselves as ‘spiritual seekers,’ but they still have the same aspirations.

**Can’t I just be spiritual and not religious?**

It is a phenomenon of our times. Over the last fifty years we’ve all become quite fatigued with the systems and structures of conventional religion. We point at religion as lacking in solid scientific foundation, as being the root cause of problems between communities; and as a product of a pre-modern world. We have concluded that we can now do without it, so we don’t bring our children up with religious education – even in schools it is fading away – and attendance at places of worship are at an all-time low.

But people are still drawn to spirituality. They read many books about it and take courses, go on retreats, form friendships with others and meet up regularly. Yoga is enjoying popularity as never before, and teachings such as the Bhagavad-gita are known by millions. So although people say they are not ‘religion-seekers’ they still have such a hunger for the same thing in new forms. So if you’d rather think of yourself as a ‘spiritual-seeker’ that’s fine. Don’t let the labels prevent you from moving forwards.

**But wait, aren’t we all striving to experience our own divine nature? Aren’t we all God, in essence?**

In quality yes, we are; in quantity no. We are eternal, never born and always existing; God is eternal, never born and always existing. So we share these important spiritual qualities. But we are spiritual sparks whereas God is the great fire. The sparks radiate from the fire, not the fire from the sparks. God is completely independent whereas our existence is dependent on His existence. A gold ring and a gold mine are the same in quality, namely pure gold; but they are hugely different in quantity – and the ring comes from the mine!

## Happiness &amp; Distress

*Many people decry the idea of God because of the suffering in the world. They ask why, if God is all love and compassion, should anyone have to suffer, especially the innocent? And why is there evil in the world at all, if God is all-powerful? This age-old question, known as theodicy in Christianity, receives an enlightening treatment in the Vedas. Vedic sources introduce us to the concept of justice and mercy applied over multiple lifetimes. Heaven and Hell are real places, the Vedas say, but not permanent. They are both merely temporary places for the onward soul to gain limited reward and punishment – and then move on.*

**Does the soul go to heaven? Is heaven a real place?**

Of course, but it doesn’t last forever. Both heaven and hell are places where we experience the accumulated reactions from our life and we enjoy or suffer according to our stock of good or bad deeds. Once having experienced this we then take birth in this world again with a fresh opportunity to choose our activities correctly and make spiritual progress.

**So if our soul doesn’t finally go to heaven, where does it go?**

The source of all heavenly experiences is the realm of God Himself. That place does not involve any coming back down to any form of life. It is the highest heavenly place. In Sanskrit it is known as Vaikuntha , the ‘place without anxiety.’ Once there, we never return to this world of birth and death. In the kingdom of God there is no pain or fear; there is only an unending, blissful awareness of His presence. In that Spiritual Sky we live with God and enjoy with Him eternally.

**That sounds very nice, but what about all the suffering in this world?**

The laws of nature are very stringent. When we break them there are consequences. When we stand in the rain we’ll get wet, and may catch a cold. And when we put our hand into the tiger’s cage there’s a very good chance we’ll be bitten - we can’t blame God for our foolish choices. In the same way, when we use our phone while driving we break the law. If we’re caught there’ll be consequences and we’ll be punished. Suffering comes as the inevitable consequence of not following the laws of nature, or the laws of the country. But what if we’re travellers and don’t know the laws of a country? There are no excuses; we should have known the laws before visiting that country. In the same way we are all travellers in a strange land. We may not know all the laws of karma, but still there will be consequences. We can make this world a heaven or a hell, just by choosing our response to it. Nothing belongs to us; it all belongs to the supreme source, the one who made it, so we can only take what we need, and no more. When we take more than we need, we suffer. Suffering may be regarded as a way for us wayward souls to be corrected.

We are constantly being put back on the straight and narrow by a compassionate higher power who wants us to return to Him. We can’t really change what happens to us in this life as most things are beyond our control. What we can change however, is how we react to it. Happiness and suffering are inevitable in this world, and our best approach to solving these problems is changing our attitude towards life. When we understand that both happiness and suffering are temporary and focus instead on cultivating our relationship with God that brings us eternal happiness, then we can rise above the problems in this world. The suffering of this world is a prompt for us to aspire to leave it. This is not our real home.

**But why do children suffer when they haven’t done anything wrong?**

Every soul creates its own future, action by action. Sometimes an entire life goes by with no reactions to bad deeds; that soul does many bad things, but he seems to escape any form of karmic reaction. Then, in the next life, from his very childhood, he begins to suffer. When we see that life we can’t understand why bad things are happening to an apparently innocent child. There seems to be no apparent cause or reason for their trouble. However, our response should never be a self-righteous one of attributing blame to anyone for their suffering; that is the prerogative for a much higher order. Rather we should be thinking compassionately: “How can I help this person?” So the reason for someone’s distress is their karma , but our response must always be dharma .

**It still doesn’t seem fair. My aunt was very religious but she died of cancer, why?**

We can never know all the intricacies of karma, samsara and the grace of God. Neither can we know what happened to your aunt in her next life. It is sad, but true, that life is indeed short, painful at times, and the remote causes of the things that happen to us – the detailed reasons we suffer loss and pain – often remain hidden. God has infinite compassion and helps each one of us in different ways. Ultimately He draws every soul towards Him along His own path.

**If we create our own karma and are forced to enjoy and suffer, how can we stop it?**

We are born with what is termed sanchit-karma , the aggregate of accumulated reactions to all previous deeds. The portion of that accumulation that has now manifested in our current bodily form and tendencies is known as prarabdha-karma . Our previous karmic reactions will gradually manifest as we pass through life, and at the same time, as we continue to act, we will generate fresh reaction. The results of previous activities appear in three concurrent stages. One is called bija (the seed), another is called kutastha (the desire), and another is called phalonmukha (about to fructify). The manifest stage is called prarabdha (already in action). Karmic reaction causes us to become attracted to certain activities, which then create fresh reactions. But the fact of the matter is that this chain of action and reaction can be interrupted by the process of bhakti-yoga.

**How does Bhakti-yoga interrupt the chain of action and reaction?**

Karma is a system of education over many lives. It continues for as long as it takes for us to re-orient our focus from selfish action to selfless activity. Yoga provides us a meditative experience and allows us to see the difference between our mental desires and our real selves, so that we can effectively control our thoughts and subsequent actions. Meditation and prayer also connects us immediately to God, the ultimate source of the cause and effect and creates a by-pass to the painful educational process. It is therefore a direct method of re-directing our future.

## Science &amp; Religion

*Someone may ask: “How can you prove that ‘God’ isn’t just some chemical in the brain? If the spiritual experience you speak of is real, why can’t everyone have it? Why does every religion say they’ve found the truth - and the others haven’t?” At the moment it might seem as if religion and science have always been opposed to each other – holding entirely conflicting world-views. Yet both have a common interest in achieving a deeper knowledge of life’s purpose, and both want to create a better world. The Vedic conviction is that they both need each other.*

**Are the devotees of Krishna against science?**

Not at all, in fact there are many eminent scientists who are also theists, and they find no difficulty in accepting the existence of a higher reality and an ultimate source of the universe. Some of them are devotees of Krishna. Science and religion do not have to be mutually opposed as they have become in the last two hundred years. Both disciplines come from a search for the truth. The only difference is that one is achieved through evidence that is observable with the senses, and the other from an internal adjustment of consciousness leading to revelation.

**But so few people can reach that meditative state - how can you prove anything is true when so few have ‘seen the light?’**

So few people have directly seen the tangible evidence produced from a sub-atomic particle accelerator hidden inside a mountain in Switzerland; and so few can actually understand the mathematical theories that provide evidence of those hidden realities. But those few are highly qualified specialists in their field and we trust them. Similarly, the seers, yogis and saints, are also highly qualified specialists. If we offer them the same level of trust, then we’ll be able to learn from them.

**Yes, but a scientist can prove something in a laboratory, and another scientist can reproduce the experiment and get the same result. What you’re talking about is just belief, isn’t it?**

Religion has faith, or trust, as a preliminary foundation. But at its heart it has to have direct experience in order to be substantial. Without that, it cannot endure. In that sense, the scientific laboratory of the spiritual researcher is his own body and mind. If we make a beginning of the practise of yoga – if we begin our experiment – we’ll quickly find that there is a direct, tangible experience that is observable. That experience can also be corroborated by other practitioners.

**Do you believe in the Big Bang?**

Actually, the explanation of the beginning of the universe given in the Vedic literature is not too dissimilar to that of modern science. But rather than a ‘big bang’ we have a ‘big breath’ as the universes (and there’s more than one), looking like bubbles, each one independent, come into being from a primordial ‘steady-state’ of non-material equilibrium. Eventually, after 311 trillion of our human years, the universes begin to drift back to the state in which they originated, each physical amalgamation separating into its constituent elements, and each element being condensed into the next, more rarefied element until only the foundational pre-evolute or pradhana exists. Explained another way, the universes expand and drift away from the cosmic body of Vishnu who lies in mystic slumber.

**Do you believe in Evolution?**

We accept the phenomenon of evolution, but do not believe that the mechanism offers either an explanation for the origin of species, or the seemingly random nature of species development. Rather than reverseextrapolating the principle of evolution to arrive at single-celled ancestors and their mathematically improbable destiny as currently proposed, we would accept the Vedic teaching of origin of species. That is, that the variety of living organisms show such a remarkable level of ‘intelligent design’ inherent in their information content and complexity, that to deny this element requires even more faith than religious belief itself. Evolution as a principle plays a large part in the general Vedic teaching of the beginnings of the universe, as it does when the progress of the soul through different species is examined.

**But everyone seems to have a different idea of how to reach the spiritual bliss you’re talking about; there are so many religions and they all say different things!**

There are always two factors in a spiritual path: the exoteric and the esoteric. The exoteric means the external things such as the language, the saint or prophet, the clothing and the particular writings of the tradition, the holy books and the detailed rules people follow. The internal, or esoteric, is the higher state of consciousness which is the aim of everything the followers do. If you analyse the highest aim of all religions you’d find they all have much more in common than they have differences.

**I don’t know about that. It’s a nice idea, but it seems that arguing and fighting between different religions these days is the cause of a lot of problems.**

That’s like saying your damp ceiling is the cause of a water leak. It’s not. The damp ceiling is the symptom not the cause. The real problem is a burst pipe in the attic. You have to go beyond the symptoms to find the cause. When a road breaks because of an earthquake, the cause is not the road itself, it’s due to a release of pressure deep down in the Earth. The road just happens to be the fault line, the weakest dividing line between two places. The externals of ethnicity, tribe, language and religious identity are often the ‘fault lines’ along which communities split when there’s a deeper disturbance.

**So you don’t agree that religious ideologies are the cause of many problems?**

Non-religious ideologies are an even greater ‘cause’ of warfare and mass casualties. Of the 174 million deaths attributed to ideologies in the 20th century, 92 million were due to non-religious ideas such as communism and fascism. But, may I say again, the problem is deeper than the surface.

**If we knew the real problem the world would be a more peaceful place. What’s your idea?**

The Bhagavad-gita says that there are three ‘gates’ leading to hellish consciousness from which hellish action flows: intense selfish desire, greed and anger. These three usually follow in a sequence. Rampant and uncontrolled desires to own, control, and enjoy objects or people, leads inevitably to greed for more of such things. When satisfaction proves unattainable – or when there’s not enough resources for everyone’s greed – there is frustration and subsequently anger. Once anger is in the system it has to be discharged, through thoughts, words or actions. Multiply that chain of events a thousand times and you’ll have a world without peace.

**But it seems that religions themselves are guilty of selfish desire, greed and anger!**

Well, the humans who follow those religions – or who say they follow them – are certainly guilty if they succumb to these three ancient enemies of peace. We have to know the signs of someone who is not overcome by these three. You don’t have to be a great philosopher to understand it, either. A person who is free from those will think, speak and act just the opposite. Instead of intense desire there will be contentment with whatever he has, and instead of greed he’ll have a generous spirit to share what little he has with others. Instead of anger there will be joy – and that, too, he’ll share.

**What’s your definition of a ‘tolerant religion?’**

Academics who study religion divide all traditions in two: inclusive and exclusive. Because following a particular religion involves sticking to a body of ideas and practises, religious people adopt different stances in order to relate to others who do not follow the same ideas and practises. So ‘exclusive’ is when a religious group considers that it has an exclusive access to the truth, a revelation from God which is superior to others. It prefers to regard others as a challenge to their ideas and practises and maintains a clear dividing line between themselves and others. The members might dress differently, minimise their social interactions and strive for ‘purity’ by separatism.

By contrast, the ‘inclusive’ religions regard God as one and His purpose as one, but His revelations countless. They tend more to see the commonality between different religions, regarding the similarities between them as evidence of the singularity of God. They do not feel threatened by the presence of others who do not share the same holy book, appearance or practises. As a result they tend to be more tolerant of others.

**Why are there so many religions if God is ultimately one?**

The Bhagavad-gita explains that the path of yoga is the one true religion through which we can know and ultimately see God. But there are predominating forces of nature which cover the soul’s vision of God. Sometimes a cloud covers our eyes so that we can’t see the Sun. We say that the cloud has covered the Sun, but it’s our eyes that are the problem. We can see the hazy sunlight coming through the clouds, but not the direct Sun. In the same way, the soul is covered by the haze of layers of egotistic selfishness that prevent us from seeing God. This state then causes us to imagine a God according to our own limited conception. We humans create many different religions due to our inability to see clearly. A multitude of spiritual teachers and leaders, saints, sages, and various ‘sons’ and ‘prophets’ of God come into the world. They appear at different places and times in history and start their teaching in the cultures that surround them.

Then there are political intrigues as kings and rulers grasp religious messages to effect control of their citizens, and change them in the process. So we end up with a wide variety of religions. Even the message of Jesus Christ – just one person - is now represented by no less than 47,000 different Christian sects. But remove many of the externals and a great similarity of concept and purpose is revealed.

**Do all paths lead to the same goal?**

Imagine asking that question at a train station – or an airport! They’d think you were slightly confused. But yes, in one sense there is only one path: the path to God. Everyone is on that path, Lord Krishna says, and everyone has an equal chance to discover Him according to their level of surrender. Yet there are many ways that we can become deviated from that one path and end up at different destinations. We can become side-tracked to the heavenly regions, or we can descend to the hellish worlds. We can take birth again on this earth, suffering or enjoying the results of our deeds. Or we can attain the ‘white light’ of Brahman or merge into the cosmic ocean surrounding Vishnu if we choose to deny our individual existence. That’s why it’s important to have a guru, or spiritual teacher – even if all paths lead to the same God.

## Volume 2: The Krishna connection

Vedic scriptures describe Krishna, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, as "the greatest artist", the source of all beauty and attraction. The real key to happiness and satisfaction is rekindling the eternal connection we have with Him. This is achieved through the practice of Bhakti-yoga. Our innate desire to love and be loved, is most fully experienced when directed toward the Supreme Person, Krishna, who can perfectly and completely reciprocate with everyone.

## Who is Krishna

*Although the name ‘Krishna’ may sound familiar to many, it is unlikely that most have had the opportunity to really comprehend the vastness that surrounds the term. Krishna is conceived by some as ‘God’ and by others as a ‘mythological’ character from eastern traditions. The ancient literature where Krishna features affirm that Krishna is the original source of manifestations - both spirit and matter.*

*Understanding why these wisdom books refer to Krishna not merely as ‘God,’ but as the ‘Supreme Personality of Godhead,’ can change our outlook greatly. Specifically, one can gain invaluable insight into how the world functions, both outside of us, and within.*

**Was Krishna human?**

No. A human being is a spirit soul inside a material body. Krishna’s body is spiritual, not material. Our body is made of material bones, muscles and blood, but the soul inside is spiritual. Our body and soul are completely different – one is composed of matter and the other is spirit. Krishna’s body is all spirit – all the way through. His body and soul is one and the same thing. Our body is born, lasts for 70-80 years, and then it disintegrates. That’s because every material thing is a temporary combination of molecules and is in a state of perpetual flux. It comes into being in this specific formation, remains for some time, and then disappears. Krishna’s form always remains the same. It never becomes old and never changes. It is eternal. So Krishna is eternally in that form of Krishna, whether on the Earth 5,000 years ago, today, or 5,000 years in the future. Those who think that He is like them don’t understand Him, He says, because they imagine Him to be a mere human being with a body of flesh like theirs.

**But Krishna has a human shape – doesn’t that mean that someone has imagined Him?**

It is in fact the other way around. We have a ‘Krishna shape.’ Krishna isn’t a creation of human beings who imagine a God shaped like them. Humans are a creation of a God shaped like Krishna. Imagining God to be like a human being is called anthropo-morphism; understanding that we are shaped like God is called theo-morphism – two very different ideas. Many people think that humans create God with their imagination, but it’s the other way round – God creates us with His.

**How do we really know what Krishna looks like?**

So many people saw Krishna while He was on this planet for more than one hundred years. There were millions of eyewitnesses. Images of Him were drawn and painted, and realistic sculptures created. These were later installed in temples. Other than this, there was poetry written and detailed descriptions of the measurements of His divine form. These were compiled and the measurements are still in use by members of the shilpi community, those who carve images from marble or mould them from bronze. But Krishna Himself has revealed His form and nature to sages and saints even before He appeared in this world. So the main way we know is by studying all of the descriptions that appear in scriptures such as the Srimad Bhagavatam and Brahma Samhita . In the pages of those texts there exist many detailed descriptions.

**There are many different religions in the world. Why do you say they all worship Krishna?**

Everyone who acknowledges the existence of God and worships Him actually worships Krishna, but some don’t know Him by that name and form. When we say ‘God’ we mean the origin of creation, the all- pervading, supremely sentient, most powerful, beautiful, all-knowing and attractive person. By definition there can only be one God because there can only be one origin of everything, one supreme source. Since everyone, by whatever name they call God, is indicating the same person, everyone actually worships Krishna. In the Bhagavad-gita, the Lord says that everyone is on the same path towards Him, but as they surrender to Him, He reveals Himself accordingly. Knowing that ‘God is great’ is one thing, knowing Him in truth is more, and serving Him with devotion is more satisfying to the soul. Those who abandon all selfish interest are able to understand Krishna as He is.

**But some religious people think of God as a ‘mystery,’ or as a ‘divine white light.’ Are they wrong?**

No, they are also right. If beauty, love, compassion, form and personality exist in this world, and if God is the origin of all of these, then surely these attributes must be present within Him. The rays of the Sun are made up of heat and light, and as such the Sun itself must possess heat and light. The closer you get towards the Sun, the more heat and light you encounter. Therefore, the closer you move towards God, the more you experience the divine attributes. If ones proposes that God is a ‘mystery,’ it indicates that one doesn’t know God, and if one sees God as a ‘divine white light,’ it means that one has begun the journey towards Him. The divine white light is the preliminary stage of God-realisation – the sunshine of the absolute Sun. As you progress towards Krishna, His personality is revealed.

**Don’t all religions take you to the same place?**

Although everyone is on the same path, it may take many lifetimes for a person to become completely free from selfish desires. Along the way, one may attain to the divine white light and spend thousands of years there before taking birth again. Or he may worship other powerful celestial beings - the demigods - and spend thousands of years with them before coming back down to this world. Taking birth in a rich family, one may become distracted by wealth and beauty. All of these obstacles delay a soul from finding its ultimate resting place.

**If Krishna is a God of love, why does He send people to lower planets?**

For the same reason that a government might send someone to prison; to safeguard the good people, and to reform the wayward. A prison is intended to be a place of reform, where people learn the error of their ways. The lower planets are also a place of learning lessons about our previous karmas. This situation is not permanent; it’s a short chapter in the otherwise long book of life. Once the soul has been reformed in the lower planets, he returns to the world to start again with a renewed perspective on life that is imprinted within the subconscious mind.

**Why is Krishna blue?**

Krishna has no cause. He is the cause of all causes. So there is no ‘why’ for anything that He is. Everything He is, and everything He does is perfect and complete – and if we adjust our perception to the reality of Krishna we can directly become attracted to Him and rapidly make progress on our spiritual journey.

**Why do some say that Krishna is immoral?**

During His time on Earth, Krishna performed many compassionate deeds such as protecting His devotees and subduing demons. But, He also stole butter and the clothing of maidens while they were bathing, and He danced with girls in the moonlight! But we would be wrong to conclude from this that Krishna is immoral, or that He is a hypocrite, asking us to follow rules that He doesn’t follow.

The nature of the soul is infinitesimal and the nature of God is infinite. His position is eternal and unchanging, while our position has changed. We imagine ourselves to be God and, trapped inside temporary bodies, attempt to recreate the eternal enjoyment of God in a world whose very nature is change. Krishna, or God, is the supreme enjoyer and we, the souls, are the enjoyed. Our attempts to change the parameters of this primeval relationship have been a failure. In order to regain our original position we have to accommodate a new set of realities. God does not have to conform to the morality He requires of us; neither does He need to demonstrate it for our upliftment. Krishna is unchanging – and we are the ones who are required to change.

**Krishna asks everyone to bow down to Him – that doesn’t seem to be humble.**

It may seem as if Krishna is a supreme egotist, demanding our offerings, our meditation on Him and His name, and asking us to engage in conversations about Him - even to bow down to Him. We would not tolerate any human being making these demands of us, rather, we should declare them to be a dictator – why then should we accede to Krishna’s requests? That would be a good question if Krishna was just another human being, or an historical or imaginary figure. The truth is that He is none of these.

Krishna is, in fact, the only person qualified to ask us to do these things, and He asks not for Himself, but for us. Krishna doesn’t need our bowing down, or food, or conversation, or the recitation of His name. Krishna only asks us to do anything at all because He is infinitely kind, and wants us to be completely free to be with Him and enjoy ourselves eternally. In order to qualify ourselves, we must train our self-centred consciousness to the level of liberated God consciousness. And when we do that we’ll find that Krishna is not an egotist but our best friend.

**I’ve heard we all have a specific relationship, or rasa, with Krishna. How can I know what rasa I have with Krishna?**

Everything comes in its own time, through the chanting of the Hare Krishna mantra:

*Hare Krishna Hare Krishna*  
*Krishna Krishna Hare Hare*  
*Hare Rama Hare Rama*  
*Rama Rama Hare Hare*

Although the maha-mantra is the mantra given to beginners on the path of bhakti, you should not think that it won’t take you all the way to the highest perfection. That is why it is known as the ‘Great Mantra,’ and it is a mantra for the most spiritually advanced saints. In it Krishna has invested all potencies.

If you chant diligently, avoiding any offenses and bad habits, all possible results will come to you, step by step. At a certain point in your life an intense pleasure will come as you chant. You won’t even be able to imagine not chanting. After that, you will hear the sounds of the spiritual world. Then the features and activities of Krishna will become visible to you, along with your particular service to one of His eternal devotees. In this way, your relationship with the Lord is gradually revealed. There is no need for any additional training or mantras.

**Has anyone seen God?**

Of course, there are so many who have seen God. Throughout history great persons such as Prahlada and Dhruva, Suka and Vyasa have all seen God face to face. Their careful directions reveal how they were able to have such transcendental visions – and how we can, too. Such visions are not the result of sentimentality or imagination, but rather the result of philosophical discrimination, disciplined eating, sleeping, breathing, rigorous discipline of the mind, and unalloyed devotional service. To see God means to take complete shelter of Him, understanding that He is your maintainer and protector. It entails doing all things that please Him and nothing that displeases Him; dedicating your very soul to His service. This is known as saranagati , or the path of attaining divine grace.

**Why do you worship the guru like you worship God?**

In our temple you will also see a sacred statue, or murti , of the spiritual teacher or guru of the devotees of Krishna. Our founding spiritual teacher’s full name is His Divine Grace Abhay Caranaravinda Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, or Srila Prabhupada for short. We do not worship him as God, but as the servant of God. The pure devotee is dear to Krishna, having completely surrendered to Him, and through his blessings we can come closer to Krishna. He is always with Krishna, and serves Him eternally. Just as we might pray to Krishna, so we also pray to the pure devotee of Krishna. Each morning, lamps, incense and flowers are also offered to him, as part of a daily ceremony known as guru puja.

## Gods &amp; demigods

*One of the biggest mysteries to a spiritual seeker may be this; if God is just one, why are there many different religions and beliefs? Doesn’t all this lead to mere confusion? And it gets even more complicated within the Vedic tradition itself, where one finds various ‘Gods’ and conflicts based on who’s the greatest. If that isn’t enough, there are even people who claim to be God. Actually, it’s really not at all complicated once we step back and take a bird’s eye view.*

**What is an incarnation?**

The word avatar means ‘one who descends,’ and refers to the forms that God takes when He enters this world to deliver the innocent and vanquish the evil. Although the English word ‘incarnation’ is used as a convenient translation, the Latin word actually means ‘the spirit that comes into flesh.’ This is not, however, how the Lord describes Himself. In the Bhagavad-gita He says that He is never born, is not made of flesh and blood, and that those who think Him to be just another human being are foolish. When Krishna comes to this world He comes in His eternal form, in all His power, with the entire spiritual kingdom accompanying Him.

**So, Krishna doesn’t reincarnate?**

No, it’s just us that have to be reincarnated! When we come into yet another womb, of yet another mother, we are being reincarnated. We’re being forced to take birth again, and we have absolutely no control in the matter. Due to our previous life’s actions we must enjoy and suffer once again. This is the real meaning of ‘incarnation,’ or the ‘spirit coming into the flesh.’ Krishna does come into this world time after time, but in every case He comes and goes as He pleases. He always remains in control of His destiny. Unlike us, He never becomes bewildered by the illusion of the world, even when He comes as a baby.

**Who are the avatars of Vishnu?**

There are as many avatars coming to this world as there are waves on a beach. There are lists of different incarnations of Vishnu but the most common is a list of ten, who are as follows: Matsya (The Fish), Kurma (Tortoise), Varaha (Boar), Nrsimha (Half Man, Half-Lion), Vamana (Dwarf), Parasurama (Warrior), Rama (Divine King), Balarama (Krishna’s Brother), Buddha (The Enlightened) and Kalki (The Destroyer). Every avatar displays activities and speaks according to the specific needs of the circumstances of the world. In all forms, Lord Vishnu uplifts the sadhus , the people of good behaviour, while diminishing the power of the duskritina , those who are rebellious against God.

**You say that Krishna is supreme, but surely Vishnu is supreme?**

Vishnu and Krishna are the same reality in two features. There is no competition between them. Only from our point of view do we consider it difficult to understand. Vishnu is God in the form of creator and upholder of the universe, the indwelling witness and the constant friend of the soul in the material world. Krishna is God who manifests His supremely attractive activities and beauty, and the One who invites all souls to come and join Him in numerous varieties of loving service. These extra features constitute Krishna’s superlative opulence, indicating His identity as the supreme, original personality of all Godhead. Just as an original candle flame can light many candles, so Krishna is the original source of all incarnations. Overall, it could be said that Vishnu acts as ‘God at work,’ and Krishna as ‘God at home.’

**Why do you ask me to worship Lord Chaitanya? Can I not just worship Krishna?**

Chaitanya is none other than Krishna, in the form of His own devotee. The Lord promises that He comes age after age to help establish dharma once again, and for the Kali Yuga, our present age, this is His form. So by worshipping Sri Chaitanya you are directly worshipping Lord Krishna, and you are doing it in a way that will attract even more blessings, since in the form of Chaitanya, Krishna is very kind to those who ask Him for help.

**Why are there so many gods?**

God is personal and the universe is personal. The gods - also known as ‘demigods’ - are extremely powerful human beings with extraordinarily long lives who are part of the universal administration. By themselves they can award material benefits but they can’t give a soul freedom from samsara , or reincarnation. In fact, their many gifts to their worshippers only serve to prolong the souls stay in the world. If we worship them at all, it is merely to acknowledge them as agents of the Supreme, Sri Krishna.

**Who is the strange looking God with the big, round eyes?**

That is also Krishna. There was once a king who was a great devotee, and he asked a wood-carver to fashion him statues of Krishna, Balarama and Their sister Subhadra as They appeared riding on chariots bound for a solar eclipse festival in Kurukshetra (the place where the Bhagavadgita was spoken). Due to being interrupted, the carver did not complete the forms, but the king worshipped Them anyway. They are painted black, white and yellow and stand some eight feet high. A large temple was built for Them in Jagannatha Puri, eastern India, and a large annual festival is held there. The three deities are placed on individual, brightly painted chariots, each with many wooden wheels. More than two million come to the festival and pull the chariots through the streets. It is said that the large, unblinking eyes of Jagannatha are always open to give blessings to His devotees. Sri Chaitanya lived in this seaside temple town for eighteen years.

**Why is God always born in India?**

It’s a very nice place. Although we might consider London or New York the most important places, it appears that God might have other ideas. The ancient scriptures talk of the Himalayan Mountains being the site of the beginnings of life on Earth, and the home of the greatest sages and yogis. India was certainly civilised at a time when human society was only sparsely distributed elsewhere. God can appear anywhere He likes, naturally, and He has certainly sent His sons and saints to other places on the planet. But due to the many appearances of Krishna in the land now known as India, there exist hundreds of places of pilgrimage where beautiful temples can be visited, and where saintly devotees gather. We can all take advantage of this by going on pilgrimage, or yatra , at least once in our lifetime.

## Holy Books &amp; Hinduism

*A lot of religions, including the Vedic tradition, seem to rely on information based on ancient books that were written many thousands of years ago. One may question the value of relying on sources whose authenticity we cannot even trace when it comes to tangible historical evidence. As it happens, many important books in different traditions have been interpolated. Couldn’t that be the case with the Vedic books too? Even if it were all true and faultless, why do so many books exist, and how do they fare in terms of authenticity on a spiritual level? Written for different audiences in order to help them relate to the spiritual contexts in an effective way, different books provide us with interesting insights into our relationship with the divine.*

**What are the Puranas?**

Purana means ‘ancient history’ and refers to the scriptures that give accounts of the beginnings of the universe, the origins of the gods, the dynasties of great kings and saints, and the many avatars of Vishnu and His wonderful deeds. There are eighteen major Puranas, the chief of which is the Bhagavata Purana, otherwise known as the Srimad Bhagavatam.

**Are the Mahabharata and Ramayana on the same level, some say they are not?**

These are known as Itihasa , another word for history, but they are constructed in a slightly different way. The Ramayana is an extended account only of the avatar of Lord Rama. It was compiled by the great sage Valmiki in 24,000 verses and is known as the first poem. The Mahabharata is a history of the major events surrounding two great dynasties. At its heart is the Battle of Kurukshetra with the dialogue between Krishna and Arjuna forming the philosophical centrepiece, spoken just before the great battle. The Mahabharata runs to 100,000 verses and was compiled by the greatest of all sages, Srila Vyasadeva. These two works are considered to be the ‘Fifth Veda.’

**I have heard there are two holy books, Sruti and Smriti – why are there two?**

Not two holy books, but one body of scripture from two sources. Sruti means ‘that which is heard,’ and refers to the knowledge that was heard from God Himself in the beginning of creation. The Lord revealed the original Veda to great gods and yogis. Sometime later in the creation, the Lord again spoke. His teachings and glorious actions were remembered by the greatest saints, who put the accounts of His deeds into their own words. So this body of work is known as smriti , meaning ‘that which is remembered.’ They are not in competition with each other, and both types of texts are considered equally sacred.

**Do you study any other books?**

Well, in addition to the two main texts of Bhagavad-gita and Srimad Bhagavatam , we also study the teachings of Krishna’s incarnation of Chaitanya Mahaprabhu. He is Krishna who came in the mood and form of His own devotee to teach the essence of religion. Five hundred years ago He had thousands of followers all over India. Some of them lived in Vrindavan, Krishna’s original home. The most famous were a group of six perfected devotees, known as the Goswamis. So we also study Chaitanya Mahaprabhu’s teachings and life, and the Six Goswami’s philosophical writings. Rupa Goswami wrote the Bhakti-Rasamrita-Sindhu , now translated into English as The Nectar of Devotion . Later on, a follower of the Six Goswamis, named Krishnadas, wrote a detailed biography of Chaitanya Mahaprabhu. He named it Chaitanya Caritamrta , and we also study this regularly.

**What are the other schools of philosophy in India?**

There are so many, it would take years to describe them all! The schools of philosophy are like the many branches of a tree. Although the branches may be many, spreading in different directions, they all receive nourishment from the same trunk and roots. Lord Krishna spoke to such great devotees as Brahma, Shiva, Narada Muni, Sri Laxmi and so on, and then those teachings were passed down through a chain of teachers and students. Over time, slight differences of emphasis arose in the teaching and so different schools of philosophy developed. But each group can trace their origins to the Vedas. In some cases, one or two schools have rejected the Vedas as their scriptural authority, preferring the mystical teaching of their particular saint. Both Jainism and Buddhism, also originating in India, are in this category.

**It seems very complex - how do we reconcile the different schools of philosophy?**

The questions you have to ask any school of philosophy are these: What is your ultimate goal? How will you get there? Who is the founder of your school of thought? The answers to these questions will help you understand the authenticity and spiritual authority of the community. There are three ultimate goals proposed by any of the schools: the extinction of the self; the merging of the self; and the eternal devotion of the self. Or to put it simply: 0-1-2. Thus all the schools of philosophy in India can be explained as The School of Zero, The School of Oneness, and The School of The Eternal Relationship. The other details will be of philosophical emphasis, technique, original teacher and culture.

**So you read the Vedas and worship Krishna - it sounds like you are Hindus?**

The actual word ‘Hindu’ doesn’t appear anywhere in the original scriptures of India. Nowhere in the Vedas or Bhagavad-gita does it mention ‘Hinduism.’ The word seems to have been given by Persians and Greeks who came into what is now India and encountered people living by the Indus River. The British then came and added the ‘-ism’ to the word ‘Hindu.’ But now the term has stuck.

**You don’t like the word ‘Hindu?’**

We prefer the term Sanatana Dharma , because it more accurately describes the eternal path spoken of in the scriptures. ‘Hinduism’ can also be used to describe the worship of many gods or not believing in any God at all. It’s a very broad term, and one that is often confusing because it means so many different things to different people. Historically speaking, from the time it was first used, it was a word that indicated those who had not submitted to Islam.

**Is there a ‘Hindu Trinity?’**

The expression ‘Hindu Trinity’ originally came from Christian missionaries in India. The Christian Trinity of ‘Father, Son and Holy Spirit’ is a theological concept indicating the One God who appears in three aspects. Looking for similarities in the concepts of the Hindus, the Christians looked at Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva and named them as a trinity accordingly. In actuality they got it wrong. In reality there is one Supreme God, Vishnu, and two demigods of differing powers, Brahma and Shiva. They are both generated from Vishnu and gain their powers from Him. However, these three do share the three aspects of universal creation, maintenance and destruction. In chronological sequence, Vishnu creates Brahma, and then Brahma generates Shiva. In the cosmic order, Brahma creates the universe, Vishnu as the all-pervading soul of the universe maintains it, and when the time is right, Shiva destroys it. For these functions, and for these functions only, they are known as the three purusha-avatars . But the term ‘Trinity’ tends to suggest that they are merely three aspects of one impersonal Supreme; a common misconception.

**What is meant by the word ‘moksha?’**

The word ‘ moksha’ means ‘release’ or ‘liberation,’ complete freedom from all suffering.

For the soul to be trapped in the never-ending chain of death followed by rebirth, followed again by death, is the worst form of suffering. Moksha means that the soul breaks free from this cycle. One form of moksha is to enter into the Brahman, the ‘white light,’ the great spiritual energy that permeates everything. Unfortunately, this is also temporary, although the freedom enjoyed by the soul lasts for many thousands of years. Ultimate moksha means to enter into the realm of Para-Brahman or Vaikuntha. There, having awakened our eternal relationship with the Supreme, we can reside forever.

**If the entire world was influenced by Vedic culture in ancient times, why did it disappear?**

The Vedic culture was based on the knowledge of the Veda, and modern research suggests that it was known from the present-day Mediterranean and Middle East through the Himalayan cultures and India to Burma, Thailand, Cambodia and Singapore. Architectural and linguistic evidence for worship of Vishnu has been found in Russia and China. Not all the other cultures which were influenced by it became fluent in the Sanskrit language. As time went by, different kings and rulers took aspects of the Vedic culture and applied it to their own citizens, in their own climatic and political circumstances.

In different parts of the world we can see how the Vedic language has lingered on, often disguised by thousands of years of history. For instance, the word veda meaning ‘to know’ gives us the word vidya or knowledge, which then becomes videre in Latin meaning ‘to know’, and video , meaning ‘I see.’ There’s a similar relationship with the Sanskrit word for God: deva . From this came the Latin deo , the Old French dieu and even the Welsh duw – all words meaning the supreme person. And the ancient practise of fire ceremonies described in the Vedas may also have entered our language. The Sanskrit for fire is agni , from which comes the Latin igneo , giving us the English word ignite . The name for gods is similarly related, the Sanskrit dyu pita or ‘father of the gods’ became the Latin Jupiter , the father of the gods.

**How did so many religions come about?**

According to the Veda, religion is composed of yagya , or the sacrifice of materially enjoyable things for a higher purpose. Dana refers to giving to others in charity; tapa , or penance, is the act of taking on voluntary restriction, or personal disciplines, so that the soul can break free from the mind’s urges and the demands of the senses. All religions contain these elements, although the founder of the religious tradition may have expressed it in different ways, and to different degrees, according to the time, place and circumstances of the people. If we examine the numerous religions of the world, we find that they all originated at an historical time when they were needed by the people of a particular region. Although the external elements may differ, such as clothing, language and leadership, the essential messages are very similar.

**Do I need to be a philosopher to join Hare Krishna?**

You don’t need any qualifications at all, apart from your enthusiasm. But philosophy will help you to clarify your thoughts, see what’s logical and sensible, and will give you the intellectual conviction to sustain your faith. As soon as you’ve become convinced, philosophy has done its initial job. Along the way though, it is essential to learn the details of Krishna and how your relationship with Him can be cultivated. This isn’t really philosophy as such, but theology, the study of God. This type of knowledge will keep you sharp and prevent your faith from fading.

**Does it bother you that you won’t have enough life to learn all the scriptures?**

There are as many scriptures as there are grains of sand on a beach. You would need many lifetimes to learn everything. But the essence of everything is contained in just one book, the Bhagavad-gita . In this one conversation the Lord teaches the conclusion of the four Vedas, the essence of the Upanishads and Vedanta , the very basics of Pancharatra , and the final word of the Puranas . In just 700 beautiful verses, everything can be understood.

**Why does it say ‘As It Is’ on your Bhagavad-gita?**

There are many translations of Bhagavad-gita, including hundreds in English. Very few of them provide the actual message of Krishna, and hardly any of them were written by a devotee of Krishna. Srila Prabhupada was a pure devotee of Krishna, who lived the life of devotion as Krishna taught it. He added the ‘As It Is’ to the title of his own translations and commentaries because he felt the world needed a trustworthy edition. The result was that, for the first time, people who read the Gita began to put Krishna’s instructions into practise in their lives. Now there are devotees of Krishna all over the world – devotees who learned of Krishna through this one version - and the Bhagavad-gita As It Is can be found in more than 80 languages. It is by far the most widely read edition of the Gita in the world.

**But how can you be sure it’s the correct understanding?**

They say that the proof of the pudding is in the eating, so the fact that people have become devotees in so many lands is proof that they have understood Krishna correctly. Aside from this, the Bhagavad-gita As It Is contains the full Devanagari Sanskrit script (the original language of the Gita), the word meanings and a detailed translation and full commentary. It is by far the most thorough and detailed version of the Gita available.

Besides this, the teachings of the Bhagavad-gita have been passed down through the centuries, from guru to disciple, so that the message has been preserved intact.

**The line of gurus given in the front pages of your Bhagavad-gita – is it complete?**

There are some historical gaps between several of the gurus. It sometimes happens in history that the sublime teachings of bhakti become lost. Even Lord Krishna says that He has to come to this world, from time to time, in order to restore dharma. So the line of gurus or teachers that represent Him occasionally becomes fractured for one reason or another. But just as a river may sometimes go underground, only to rise again miles away, so the pure stream of knowledge occasionally disappears, only to rise again some years later.

**But haven’t the teachings been gradually changed due to so many gurus in the chain?**

Luckily there are many ways in which teachings can be preserved. Because the Vedic teachings are based upon an oral tradition, there were extensive methods of correcting pronunciation and keeping the sounds of the mantras accurate. Later, Srila Vyasadeva saw that people’s memories in the future would grow weak, so he compiled everything and wrote it all down.

**Where is the original Bhagavad-gita – the manuscript?**

The oldest Bhagavad-gita in London is in the Wellcome Trust Museum. It is dated to 1488, which is not very old in Indian terms. We don’t know if there is an original manuscript anywhere, since they were written on leaves. It is also sad to report that in India many old manuscripts were destroyed by invading armies, and many more have been thrown away, simply because they were old! Now many Bhagavad-gitas have been inscribed on copper, buried in time capsules or sealed in bomb-proof mountain caves. We are taking no chances for the next generations!

**Aren’t the stories in the scriptures just helpful myths?**

It’s interesting how we regard events we believe to be true as ‘history,’ and those we can’t comprehend as ‘mythology.’ The two terms have more to say about our level of acceptance than the veracity of the incidents. There are many phenomena we have not personally witnessed, even in this world, and our experience of reality is therefore highly circumscribed. Infra-red and ultra-violet light are beyond our perception, making anything that emits them outside our spectrum of visible phenomena. Our eyes cannot even perceive the things that lesser creatures like insects can see. How then do we propose to claim to know what is ‘real’ and what is ‘myth?’ While people can, and do, create stories about men and women with super powers, we should be careful of what we deny. The reality of the universe may be more wonderful than we can presently conceive.

Scripture employs much metaphorical and poetic speech in order to illustrate philosophical points. But there is a distinction between these and accounts of historical events which may be beyond our conception. We have to know which is metaphor, and which is history, and the difference between them is usually explained in the text itself.

## Active spirituality

*Some of the most challenging questions spiritual practitioners face are those dealing with the application of scriptural knowledge in everyday life. Although the spiritual aphorisms contained within the books may hold great value, it is the implementation of these that actually qualifies the understanding behind it. Some aspects may even sound counterintuitive to everyday moral concerns. Yet, when we study them deeper and practice them with sincerity, we realise that the theory and practice only complement and support one another.*

**What are the main things that I need to do to be spiritual?**

There are thousands of things you could do to lead a more spiritual life. But if you want practical suggestions of the best things that will give you results quickly in a minimum time, then just remember these letters: A B C D E F G.

A is for Association . Whatever you keep company with – the food, music, books, movies, television, websites and newspapers you consume, your phone conversations, your work colleagues – are what you associate with. By choosing carefully you can create a favourable environment for your mind, a great foundation for spiritual life. Be with positive people who lift you up and give you positive, life-affirming thoughts. Avoid those who don’t.

B is for Books. Whatever you read becomes a voice in your head, and will affect how you see the world and make choices of direction. Books like the Gita and Bhagavatam are full of powerfully spiritual messages that will help you see the world around you from the soul’s point of view.

C is for Chanting. Although we all have to work late nights these days, it’s worth getting to bed earlier, waking up slightly earlier, and getting in some morning mantra meditation before breakfast. Chanting of the maha-mantra is the greatest way to experience a quite remarkable transformation of consciousness. Do it regularly and you’ll never look back.

D is for Diet. The path of Bhakti-yoga begins with the tongue, with chanting and sacred food. If you avoid three types of food you’ll feel better, create no karma, and become connected with Krishna:

(a)    Foods that are the product of violence, such as red meat, poultry, fish, lobster, crab and shellfish.

(b)    Foods that are considered impure, such as eggs (whether fertilised or not), and some fungi.

(c)    Foods that agitate the mind, such as onions, garlic, alcohol, chilli and foods with high sugar.

E is for Enthusiasm. You’ll do well on the spiritual path if, once you’ve decided it’s for you, you throw yourself into it with all your enthusiasm, without reservation. Don’t wobble or waver in your spiritual life – be determined and enthusiastic.

F is for Friends. We all have an intimate circle of friends. They’re the ones who’d support you through hard times, who’d visit you in hospital, and who care about what you’re into. For rapid spiritual growth, we’d recommend having at least two spiritual friends in this inner circle, and more if you can. Why? Because we tend to listen to our close friends, and if they are also practising bhakti-yoga, it’s a powerful friendship to have.

G is for Group. You can join a group for any interest under the sun. A weekly or monthly group meeting helps to keep your interest alive. There’s probably a bhakti-yoga group very near you. You’ll learn more, have fun, and find inspiration if you join it. You’ll be taking part in kirtan, talks and discussions, and if there’s a free meal at the end of it, that’s another problem solved.

**Why do you put such an emphasis on chanting?**

The main practise in awakening our spiritual nature is known as yagya – the turning around of our consciousness from self-centred to God-centred. It involves offering our words, wealth, time and intelligence to God. Some of this is done as part of daily life, and some is done in powerful ritualised practises. There are four main ingredients for yagya and they have been practised, say the Vedas, from the very dawn of creation. The universe has four seasons that revolve over many millions of years. Each one of the four yagya activities is most appropriate for a particular season.

The Spring of Satya Yuga requires meditation on Vishnu within the heart; for Treta Yuga fire sacrifices are most important; in the Dwapara Yuga opulent temple worship is the recommended process; but in the Winter Age of Kali Yuga the chanting of the ‘Names of Hari’ is the most effective yagya process. Indeed, the Brihan-Naradiya Purana says harer-namaiva kevalam : ‘the chanting of the holy name of Hari (Krishna) is the only way.’

When Sri Chaitanya appeared in India He promoted the chanting of the names of Krishna as both a personal practise and a collective, congregational and musical practise. The result of His teachings, and of those who followed Him, is the modern-day Hare Krishna movement.

**Why do you worship idols?**

The ‘idols,’ as you call them, are the essential point of focus within a temple. Although the Abrahamic faiths proscribe the worship of ‘graven images’ and subject anyone who does so to punishment, it is important to understand the reasoning behind this form of worship. Firstly, it should be noted that imaginary images, conceived in the fertile mind of a man and executed by an artisan in wood or stone, are also forbidden in the Vedic tradition. No good can come from worshipping an imaginary God. The devotees of Krishna are in complete agreement with Judaism, Christianity and Islam on this point. But God does have an actual form, and this revealed form can be worshipped.

The archa-vigraha is the form of God fashioned in a material element such as wood, stone or metal and installed with authorised rituals and mantras. Krishna can turn spirit into matter and matter into spirit, so His consecrated statue should never be mistakenly identified as being made of matter. The sacred image is one way that the invisible and untouchable God makes Himself available to the senses of the devotee.

**How can you revere human beings as gurus?**

Reverence should be given wherever reverence is due. Vedic culture is one of respect and gratitude. Parents and other elders are honoured, older siblings are given respect, and everyone offers gratitude to teachers of any kind. The highest level of respect and reverence is given to that teacher who gives the highest knowledge and guidance. When a guru, or spiritual teacher and guide, repeats the words of Krishna, the guru becomes as honoured as Krishna. Although the guru is a human being, since the devotee hears the words of Krishna from the guru, the devotee feels a connection with Krishna through those words. A police officer may be a mere human being, but is obeyed because he represents the law; similarly a guru is a mere human being – and always thinks of himself as such – but is revered because of who he represents.

**If you are spiritual, why do you use technology?**

We would much prefer that the messages of Krishna were already heard, understood and honoured all over the world. Our motto is ‘simple living, high thinking,’ and we much prefer sustainable, natural devices to technological ones. But since practically no-one knows about Krishna, we are taking the opportunity to use any invention to disseminate the information. Devotees of Krishna thus run radio stations, both analogue and digital, run television studios, and naturally produce millions of books – both in print and electronic formats. At the same time, we also create sustainable communities where members can live off the land, and horse and oxen provide the transport.

**Why do you spend so much money on temples? Why not use it to help others?**

Temples and the worship that goes on inside them have a powerful and positively uplifting effect on people. For this reason, in any major European city, you’ll find that the cathedral or church was right in the middle of the population. From the religious centre came education, medical care, hospice facilities, food distribution and help for the poor, morality, arts and culture, including music, writing, architecture and many other aspects of progressive society. In India it was the same; the temples performed unlimited amounts of social service.

**I always thought that meditation was done silently, with closed eyes?**

The most ancient forms of authentic meditation have always involved the recitation of sound formulas, or mantras, but they may be spoken out loud, murmured, or heard within the mind with no sound at all. The meditation known as japa – the repetition of the Hare Krishna mantra – is actually far more absorbing than silent meditation. Proof of this is that a regular chanter can meditate for two hours at a time. So-called silent meditation simply means that an observer cannot hear a sound, but the meditator can. After the advent of Buddhism in India, other forms of meditation developed, later spreading to Tibet, China and Japan. But even many of them involve focusing the mind on sound. It is difficult – almost impossible – to focus on nothing.

**Why is a mantra more purifying than any other sound?**

A mantra is a spiritual sound vibration. Its origins are not in the world of matter. All mantras come from God and have been passed down by a chain of rishis (seers) and gurus (teachers). Although when listening to a mantra we hear only syllables made by the tongue, and vibrated as sound waves, it would be incorrect to think that the syllables themselves are the sum total of the mantra. The sounds are invested with potency beyond our imagination. Their true power becomes evident upon chanting with attention.

**Do I have to give away all my money to be a devotee?**

The Isha Upanishad says that everything is controlled and owned by the Lord, but that we can use what we have been allotted as our quota. If we remember those facts and work in that consciousness we may live happily for one hundred years. So when we transform our God-given talents into hard work and success, we have a right to do that; but the fruits of the work belong to God. We are allowed to use them, being mindful of His ownership. Wealth and poverty are therefore neutral; you are not closer to God by being poor or further away from Him by being rich. You are closer to Him by using whatever you have in His service. You can give your money to a good cause, particularly a saintly person or a temple – or you can do God’s work in the world, and become a saintly person yourself!

**Why are there 108 beads?**

There are so many occurrences of the number 108 in ancient civilisation that it is difficult to know where to begin. In modern mathematics 108 is considered an ‘abundant’ number and one with which a small computation produces the Golden Ratio. In ancient mathematics it was considered an ‘auspicious number.’ In modern astronomy the ratio of the diameters of Sun, Moon and Earth are all calculated to be an average of 108. There are 108 pressure points in the body according to the Indian martial art Marma Adi . There are said to be 108 Upanishads. And of course, it is said that Lord Krishna had 16,108 queens. The number 108 also adds up to 9 in numerology, the number of the guru principle. There are 108 holy places in Vaishnavism and of course, 108 beads on a set of japa-mala. In today’s India the 999 emergency telephone number is also – 108!

## Food &amp; Diet

*It is said that what we eat affects not just how we look, but also how we think. What you eat, becomes you – as food is what we’re made of, at least on the bodily level. Therefore, it makes sense to be sensible about our dietary intake. Some people argue however, for the consumption of designated levels of alcohol and meat, in order to maintain the body’s needs for a balanced diet and the mind’s needs for sanity. However, the Vedic tradition looks at the physical, mental and spiritual needs of everyone and explains why certain things are not just unnecessary, but also counter-productive for wellbeing. To summarise, consumption of healthy, meat-free and intoxicant-free foods helps us to become nourished.*

**Why are you vegetarian?**

There are so many reasons! Where should we begin? Firstly, we practise ahimsa or nonviolence, so we don’t kill animals for food – and neither do we pay others to do it for us, such as butchers or fast-food chains. Eating meat is both cruel and unnecessary. There is no good reason to eat animals for food; there are so many other sources of protein and minerals. Good old-fashioned rice and dahl, for instance, when mixed together, can provide all the essential amino acids that make up protein in your body. The meat industry is responsible for wastage of precious land, water, transport fuel, and is the highest producer of greenhouse gases – things we can all do without. The main reason for devotees being vegetarian is that we only eat food that has been offered first to Krishna – prasadam – and Krishna does not want to eat dead bodies.

**I was raised as a Hindu, so I have never eaten beef.**

Well done, you are to be congratulated. However, sheep, goats, chickens and fish are all equally sentient creatures that feel pain. We have no right to take their life since we didn’t create that life. By taking part in an industry of mass-slaughter we create a dark future for ourselves, since every animal we kill – or pay for someone to kill for us – will result in sinful reactions that must be endured by us at some time in the future.

**You’re still killing plants when you’re vegetarian, no?**

Yes, killing is required with vegetables although not with fruits. Since life is sustained by life, and we must continue to live, we must take life from something. But in doing so, we must choose foods that involve the least amount of pain as possible. Plants are much less sentient than an animal with a spinal cord. In addition, plants can be offered to Krishna.

**Why do you not eat eggs?**

Eggs are a product of the menstrual cycle of the chicken. Whether they are fertilised or unfertilised, we do not eat them because they are not considered to be clean.

**What’s so bad about onions and garlic?**

Onions and garlic are certainly neither animals, nor the products of menstruation, but there is a separate reason for avoiding these foods. These two vegetables, although tasty for some tongues, contain oils that are not conducive to maintaining a fixed level of meditation. They make the mind wander – even when you are trying to fix it on one point. Consequently, anyone who’s job role requires a good level of concentration would do well to avoid them.

**What’s wrong with mushrooms?**

If you pick them yourself – and you make sure you’re picking the non-poisonous variety – there is no harm, although fungi are also considered to be in the food group that tend to cloud the mind and reduce the capability of clear thought.

**And what about alcohol - not even at weddings or celebrations?**

There was a time when no-one at a vedic wedding ceremony would even have dreamed of drinking alcohol. It wasn’t considered necessary for having a good time. Now all types of alcohol are widely available and served at functions, and being a non-drinker is considered something unusual. Just in one generation! Alcohol is always bad news and regularly wrecks families, destroys livers, and causes addictive behaviour resulting in loss of promotions and a successful career. Just make a promise to your children to avoid it and see what a difference it makes.

**Someone told me you don’t drink tea and coffee?**

That someone was correct! Although they are only mild intoxicants, they are unhelpful to the system, over-stimulate the brain, produce an acidic after-effect which is the foundation of disease, and their overcultivation in some countries destroys the fine balance of agriculture leading to food deprivation.

**Please don’t tell me that I can’t even eat chillies!**

Alright, I won’t. But I will tell you that over-consumption of chillies inflames the stomach and the blood circulation; leads to inattention and enhances the raja-guna – the passions – making you irritable and anxious at work and with your family. But I didn’t say you couldn’t eat them.

**Why do you drink cow’s milk if you don’t eat beef?**

Milk can give us all the ingredients of meat without ever having to harm the cow. We believe in a mutually helpful relationship between humans and cows. The bulls plough the fields and provide transport, and the cows provide milk which can be made into yogurt, butter and ghee. In return, humans can protect cows as they would their other pets. Milk is a wonder food with all health-giving properties.

**But most cows are factory-farmed and their calves killed. Why do you use commercial dairy milk if it’s the product of such cruelty?**

You are right about factory-farming and milk bought from the supermarket. Our response has been to create our own farms where our cows can live out their lives without any cruelty. Such milk is ahimsa , or non-violent. Of course, this way of caring for cows cannot be undertaken by everyone, but we would like to see it more widespread. We do produce some ahimsa milk for sale, but not enough that everyone can choose to have it. So some of our members have decided to avoid commercial milk altogether; others offer milk to Krishna in the understanding that both the giver and receiver of the milk will be purified by this devotional offering.

**What’s your message to the world?**

For us, human life is all about gradually waking up to our spiritual nature and working towards spiritual freedom. Everything else should serve that end. When spiritual needs are given priority, everything else can be achieved: peace, food, water, prosperity, education. We can all help each other towards that. We need to work on our compassion and respect for others. To do that we need to know that happiness can’t be gained from an incessant march towards greater levels of materialism. Without that basic understanding we’ll compete with others for limited resources, over-indulge at the expense of other communities, and eventually fight with them.

To overcome this tendency takes yagya – the spirit of sacrifice. Our message is that much of this can be accomplished if we join together in kirtan , the joyful singing of mantras. We humbly ask everyone: “Please chant Hare Krishna and be happy.” So self realisation, spiritual freedom, compassion, respect, personal restraint, sacrifice, and singing together – those are a few values we believe in, and that is our message to the world.

**What would you change, if you could?**

One of our principles is ‘simple living, high thinking.’ It’s based on the idea that when you simplify your basic needs you’ll find the time for more important activities. There is much about modern life that is unnecessarily complex. Finding somewhere to live has been made needlessly hard with planning and building restrictions and the ‘housing market,’ for example. Working at what you love is becoming difficult, and education often means training for a career that may not exist in a few years. Even eating has now been made dependent on factories and vast transportation networks. We believe that we’ve gone quite far enough with social complexity. The petrol-driven, industrialised society is now dangerously short term.

There are many practical and spiritual benefits if we can simplify our approach to basic essentials. Food can be grown locally and transported a minimal distance only. Smaller towns and villages can become the norm, and the growth of overcrowded, polluted cities can be reduced. Our animal-based diet and level of consumption is cruel and unsustainable, and a plant-based diet is much healthier. We’d keep food real, and do away with all the unnatural flavourings and additives, the fats and sugars, that make us all unhealthy. That would save money for our hospitals. A shift from sedentary, machine-based occupations to ones where we use our innate propensities and skills would keep us all happily engaged and rewarded.

## Appearance &amp; lifestyle

*People often question as to whether one needs to change one’s appearance in order to develop a deeper relationship with the divine. Although one’s spiritual connection with the divine may not depend upon adopting a particular attire, it definitely helps one feel part of the community of practitioners that may very much impact one’s spiritual practice. Being part of a community is essential for spiritual progress because we can learn, practice and grow, based on where we’re situated in society.*

*Vedic tradition has many different attires to facilitate one’s spiritual experience, and just understanding this can give us insights into the depth of devotional practice contained within. Philosophy and thinking are necessary, and now let’s talk a bit about some practicalities. On a day-to-day basis, what will help me in my practise of spiritual life? How much can I realistically do at home? Are there different times of day for spiritual practise? What about sleeping – I heard you all get up really early? Why is that?*

**Why do you wear the robes?**

Some male devotees wear the traditional robe, known as a dhoti . It’s one piece of cloth, five metres long, cotton or silk, sometimes jute, and can be folded in different ways. It’s worn for simplicity – both in the production of the garment and the wearing of it. It’s warmer than it looks, and comfortable too. There’s a matching shawl known as a chaddar . A thicker shawl made of wool is often worn on cooler days. The thigh-length shirt is known as a kurta .

**So do you have to be a priest to wear a dhoti?**

No, anyone can wear one. Those who live in the temple as monks, and the men who serve as priests on the main altar wear them, and it’s also worn in the home at times of private worship.

**Should I wear a dhoti when I visit the temple?**

You can wear whatever is clean and smart and which you feel comfortable in, but if you’d like to wear a dhoti - why not? At least you could try wearing one on special occasions; you might get to like it. In southern India, it’s very common to see men wearing them; in the north less so. But now they are worn all over the world. Please ask and someone will show you how to wear one.

**What is the meaning behind the haircut the men have?**

The lock of hair is known as the sikha . This translates as ‘summit’ or sometimes ‘flag,’ like the top of a temple dome with the flag flying. Shaving the head is said to reduce vanity, and the sikha reminds the wearer that his own body is itself a type of temple. The monks wear their hair like that, and some of our other members like to wear it too. The sikha is a requirement for conducting all types of sacred ritual.

**Why do you have that mark on your forehead?**

The mark is known as tilak or pundra , and it’s made of a kind of yellow clay known as gopi-chandan . The twin lines on the forehead are said to depict the outline of Krishna’s foot, and the mark on the nose represents a leaf of the sacred Tulasi tree. In India there are many different varieties of pundra , each revealing the religious community of the wearer. Tilak markings are another way the devotee is reminded that the body belongs to Krishna.

**What do women wear?**

Many women like to wear the traditional saree , a patterned cloth of some six metres in length – sometimes even longer. These come in all colours and designs, and are available in all types of fabric. Perhaps it’s slightly easier for a woman to wear a saree than for a man to wear a dhoti! But again, the main point is to dress in what you feel is appropriate for a sacred space.

**Some people wear orange. What does that mean?**

Those are the monks. After a period of preparation, some men, usually younger, opt to live a simple life of discipline including celibacy. They practise their spiritual life as a religious community, and they can often be seen working hard around the temple, performing worship on the main altar, or teaching classes.

**Can they get married?**

Yes, this form of celibate life comes with the option of getting married if that becomes more suitable. In that case the devotee goes from the life of the brahmacari , or monk, to the married life of the grihasta .

**I have seen a monk carrying an orange stick, what is that?**

That stick is actually a bundle of three bamboo sticks tied together then wrapped in saffron-coloured cloth. It is carried by a special type of monk known as a sannyasi : one who has opted for lifetime celibacy and an itinerant lifestyle, travelling to many places to give the teachings to others. The three sticks indicate that he has formally dedicated his actions, thoughts and words to Krishna.

**Why do women have to cover their hair?**

They are not obliged to, but it is customary in many traditional cultures for women to cover their most attractive features when in the presence of monks. Some women tie back their hair, and others choose to cover it with the end of their saree or a scarf. It is not a rule, however, merely an optional and additional observance.

**So if what the monks see is important, why do women show their bellies?**

Traditionally, the saree was worn either with a shirt or a shorter blouse known as a cholee . While this was cooling for a hot, tropical climate, it is not recommended as an all- weather garment, particularly in the colder months. It is not an obligatory item of clothing, and as you have suggested, there are times when it might even be considered to reveal a bit too much.

**You said that orange means a monk; what does white mean?**

White is simply the colour of bleached cotton. It has no other significance. Many married men wear white, but they are not restricted from wearing any other colour they choose. Single men who are new students in the community also wear white.

**Who can give classes or lead the singing in the temple?**

Anyone who is a committed follower can lead the singing, and anyone who is sufficiently knowledgeable, and who has adequately prepared, may be invited to deliver a class on the Bhagavad-gita in the evening. Giving the morning class is a service reserved principally for the senior members.

**I hear devotees talking about the ‘modes of nature.’ What are they?**

The word ‘mode’ is an English rendition of the Sanskrit word guna , meaning ‘rope.’ According to Vedic metaphysics, matter or prakriti is constantly in a state of flux, the elements combining and disintegrating through time and space. The primordial constituents of matter are three qualities known as sattva , rajas and tamas . Rajas or ‘passion’ pushes matter into fresh combinations, sattva , or goodness, holds matter in a steady state and tamas , ‘ignorance’ or ‘darkness’, slows down combinations and disintegrates. Correspondingly, these three influence the ego of an individual which affects the mental equilibrium drawing the thinking, feeling and willing into corresponding activity. All contact between the senses and their objects are also constituted of mixtures of these three modalities.

**How can we know what modes will influence us and when?**

The three modes of nature are as regular as clockwork – literally. In fact the cycle of the three modes are what make up each day. Twenty-four hours contain three periods of eight hours each. Within each period of eight hours the respective mode grows in intensity reaching a high point then fading as the next mode begins to predominate. The influence of the mode of goodness grows in intensity from midnight, peaks at 4.00 am and then diminishes to 8.00 am when passion takes over, growing in dominance until its mid-point around midday. It can be noted that tendencies toward creativity and energy expenditure take place at certain times as does relaxation and sleep – or night time enjoyment and indolence. Those who wish to take advantage of the best possible time for spiritual activity can rise at 4.00 am, to make the most of the ‘golden period’ for meditation (known as the brahmamuhurtha) which lasts for 48 minutes and begins 96 minutes before the astronomical sunrise.

**Why is it necessary to have a cold shower?**

First of all, having a quick shower first thing in the morning, or as the devotees say, ‘taking a bath,’ is an essential ingredient of the bhakti-yoga lifestyle. After sleeping for eight hours or so, washing off the toxins of the night helps you to collect your thoughts and get ready for meditation. A cold splash after your warm shower will not only close your pores (good for health) and completely wake you up, but will also allow you to stay awake through your all-important meditation hour.

**What else do the devotees do in the morning?**

A quite common preparation for the day is using the thuli , less attractively known as the ‘tongue-cleaner.’ It’s a one-centimetre wide, U-shaped band of steel which is drawn down over the extended tongue, cleaning it of the night’s toxins. Devotees also like to drink quantities of warm water in the morning hours, often with added lemon juice. This helps with hydration and ‘keeping things clockwork.’ You might find other devotees practising a few minutes of yoga asanas in the morning – in addition to all their regular chanting and reading, of course.

Some breathing exercises or pranayama are always helpful to focus the mind before chanting, removing any memories of dreams the night before. Things devotees don’t do in the early morning include checking their phones, listening to the radio or reading the newspaper. From the time they wake up (usually to the sound of an alarm clock or digital equivalent) right up until breakfast, devotees are engaged in devotion. They know that this is the most important part of the day, the part that prepares them for everything else the day will bring. The devotee’s ‘morning programme’ might run from before 4.00 am until 8.30 am. Those who commute to work or get children ready for school would have to adjust their schedule accordingly, but the same level of focus and commitment to setting aside sacred time is required of all serious practitioners.

## Vedic Perspectives

*Religion seems to be responsible for a tremendous amount of violence in the world. Could Krishna consciousness ever be violent? What about the inequalities of the caste system? Isn’t it better just to help people than to spread yet another religion? Why do many religions look down on women? Do you? What about racism, where do you stand on that? If I was lesbian or gay could I be a Hare Krishna devotee?*

*The world we live in may look like it’s in a state of dysfunction. More often than not, we are faced with situations that pose challenging questions to our spiritual practice. For a spiritual practitioner, it is imperative to be conversant with the philosophy and practice of Krishna consciousness in order to effectively deal with difficult circumstances. Even though the books that explain the philosophy may have been written centuries ago, the knowledge therein is applicable to all situations even now; it just depends on the correct judgement on the spiritualist’s part.*

**Is the Gita a book of peace or violence?**

Neither. It is the record of an enlightening, hour-long conversation between Krishna and His warrior friend Arjuna on the battlefield. Krishna urges His friend to fight the upcoming battle, reasoning that it is a legitimate fight and that it is his duty as a soldier to do so. But the inner revelation of the Bhagavad-gita is that whatever one does, if it is done purely for the satisfaction of God, at His request, then the actions taken are free from any question of immorality. Neither a self-serving peace nor a selfish violence are the subjects of the Gita.

**Does Krishna promote the caste system?**

What we call the ‘caste-system’ is more or less a debilitated version of the original varna-ashram social system described by Krishna. The modern form has not worked properly for a long time, and has given rise to much human suffering. Originally, it was meant to accommodate the psycho-physical propensity of every human being in work that was conducive to personal progress; in work that was peaceful and beneficial to all other members of society. Varna indicates the work-orientation and skill-set of a person, across all types of labour and profession. Ashram indicates the life-stage and commensurate duties, from youth to retired life. The ‘system’ is simply the way in which humans of all types can most beneficially work in cooperation with one another to advance the whole of society.

**Isn’t it better to help people instead of practicing religion?**

Helping others is important and is the duty of all human beings. For many different reasons people suffer - from poverty, abuse, war and disease – and it is in everyone’s interest to help wherever possible. Helping others relieves suffering, develops our compassion and contributes to a peaceful society. It also helps our destination after this life ends. A life devoted to helping others means that we will be re-born in fortunate circumstances – or even attain heaven.

Unfortunately, any promotion to the heavenly regions, or birth in a fortunate family in the next life, is still only temporary. Sadly, the relief of suffering we can provide in this world is also temporary. The soul is eternal, and only when we become reunited with God, in the eternal abode, will we or anyone else be free from suffering. Devotional service to Krishna is therefore the all-encompassing activity that will result in helping people in all conditions, in this life and the next.

**In Hare Krishna do the men look down on women?**

Not at all. In fact the Vedic scriptures are very direct in describing how women are to be respected by men, especially by men on a spiritual path. The following is taken from the Lakshmi Tantra, chapter 43. The goddess Lakshmi is speaking:

“A man of piety, free from sin, consistently adhering to the precepts of the sacred scriptures, performs those deeds that are not condemned by women and that please them. A yogi should never abuse a woman, either in deed, speech or thought. who abuses women, abuses me, He who abuses me abuses the entire three worlds.

Knowing women as my direct manifestation, how can a yogi refrain from revering them? One should never hurt women, and should never even think of wronging women. A yogi who wishes to attain the fulfilment of yoga should always act to please women. He should regard all women as mothers, as goddesses, as my very self.”

**Why does God allow evil?**

For the same reason that He allows gravity. The natural force of gravity is neither good nor bad, but you must understand it and be respectful of it, otherwise it can harm you. There are invisible laws of nature that must also be understood in order to protect ourselves from present and future harm. Just as a child may not know the rules of driving on the road, but may learn them as she grows up, so we must enquire as to what are the rules of ‘driving’ our own biological vehicle, the human body.

There are good and bad deeds and the reactions to these will come to us in the life after this one. Just as there is a consequence when we throw a stone high in the air, there is a result for every action we throw out into this world. We need to learn the laws and obey them. When we disobey God’s laws and act against them, that is the path to greater evil. Just as we immediately see a shadow when we turn away from the Sun, so we immediately see darkness and may commit evil actions when we turn away from God.

**Is blood donation OK? What do you say about organ donation?**

Yes. Blood donation is a great act of charity that may allow someone else to heal, or to survive an accident or operation. Organ donation is in the same category. Since we do not believe in resurrection of the body, our beliefs do not require that the entire body be buried as in the Abrahamic faiths. Therefore, the arrangement of donating one’s organs after death is a final act of charity enabling someone else’s life to be enhanced.

**Do I get the karma if they commit sins with my organs?**

No. No more than you would be charged with burglary if a burglar stole your car and used it one night.

**Can I have medicines with alcohol or gelatine capsules?**

There are often medicines available which do not come in a tincture of alcohol. It is best to use these if you can. Although you are using alcohol for medicinal use, there can always be room for unscrupulous use at some stage. It does happen. Regarding gelatine capsules, these are directly made from the results of animal slaughter so it is best to avoid them if you possibly can. There are many vegetarian capsules that can be taken as an alternative. Try always to find the ahimsa or non-violent alternative if you can. If it is not available, the more people ask, the more manufacturers will respond to customer demands. That’s the way the market works.

**Are leather and animal products OK if I’m a vegetarian?**

Not really. They are the products of extreme violence, mainly towards cows, and if you can avoid them please do it. Leather shoes and leather sofas are the two most common items found in the homes of otherwise strict Hindu vegetarians. There are always alternatives if we look a little harder and try to avoid the hidden violence in everything we buy. We don’t eat beef, and we call the cow our mother, so why do we wear her skin on our feet and sit on her skin in our homes? It is not logical and it is not free from karmic reaction.

**What do you think of racism?**

Our philosophy teaches that there are numberless souls, each unique but spiritually identical. Each soul lives inside a body with different characteristics. Like passengers inside different vehicles. Just as some of us have small or large cars, trucks or vans, coloured ones or metallic ones, new models or old bangers, English, German or Japanese ones, so we also have biological ‘vehicles’ or different bodies. If someone forgets they are the driver of a car and instead starts thinking they are that car, telling friends: “You know, I’m actually a Honda,” their friends would think they’d temporarily lost touch with reality.

Similarly, if someone says, “I am a young black man,” or “I’m an old Japanese woman,” they have also lost touch with the actual reality of their situation. The only real identity is: “I am a soul, and this body I’m in is temporary.” Thinking of oneself as belonging to a particular ethnicity, tribe or nationality is therefore an unhelpful designation. Either it is falsely imposed by others, or we impose it on ourselves. Whatever the situation, it is a case of wrongful labelling. Racism is where this mistaken identity is further compounded by negative discrimination directed towards someone because of those external labels. It produces only tension and conflict.

**So how would you solve the problem of racism?**

Although the reality is that everyone is eternal in nature and spiritual in identity, only a few can remember this at all times. The majority of people will identify very closely with their race. They will then join others to form collective identities such as community, tribe, nationality and religion. The culture of the Vedas describes that these temporary bodily and mental designations – which most people experience as real - can be best accommodated in a social system that fosters affinity between people of like temperament. Physical and psychological propensities, and their subsequent work-life preferences, can best be engaged in a system that recognises and honours differences, not artificially ignores them. This is known as the varna-ashram system. It conceives of society as being a body with different organs, each playing their own role, each with its own set of rules. The difficulty in modern society is that we’ve taken a spiritual idea – equality of souls – and are very busy trying to homogenise everyone into a one-size-fits-all society. The spirit behind this is noble, no doubt, but it’s not working. In varna-ashram society tolerance, compassion and spiritually upward mobility are all important. It doesn’t work properly without those vital elements.

**What are your views on homosexuality?**

Sexual orientation is another artificial designation of the soul. Each soul is born into the world with a stock of karma from the last life, and will enjoy a variety of happiness and distress accordingly. Some are born with ambivalent gender, and some experience passions for those of the same sex. Both of these are considered karmic complications which may make life harder for that individual. Homosexuality is not a new phenomenon, and thousands of years ago the Vedic culture named homosexuals as prakriti tritiya , or ‘the third sex.’ According to the Vedas it is not a major issue, merely a phenomenon that must be dealt with by the respective individuals. The difficulty for homosexuals in modern society is that they have been first persecuted and criminalised, then pushed forward for special treatment as fashionable victims. Neither approach is recommended by the Vedas. Homosexuals are to be treated with the respect and understanding afforded to everyone else.

**So would you perform a gay wedding at the temple?**

The vivaha-yajna – the wedding ceremony – is a ceremony witnessing the giving of a daughter by her father to a young man, not merely a consecrated union of two adults. It is for the blessing of procreation and creating the next generations in human society. Hence this would not be an appropriate form of wedding service to be conducted at the temple.

Traditionally, members of the prakriti-tritiya have their own ceremonies – and their own priests. Our respectful suggestion would be that this service of lifetime commitment is best conducted under the auspices of a homosexual priest at a venue of their mutual choice.

**Is there divorce in the Hindu tradition?**

Marriage is a sacred bond, witnessed by God, the family and the community during the wedding ceremony, and it is upheld by family, friends and the law during life. As for divorce, there are a number of commentators on ancient scripture and they have differing opinions. There does seem to be allowance for re-marriage under certain circumstances. While some say a widow should not re-marry, there are other authorities who say that it is permissible. The following circumstances also enjoy some favour: if the husband has disappeared from the family home for more than four years; if it is proved that husband can produce no children; if the husband has become an ascetic; and if the husband has been expelled from his varna.

**What about abortion?**

The codes of dharma hold that life should be respected from conception through to delivery of the child, and right through life up to a natural death. Abortion of a child is therefore considered very sinful. There may be extreme cases where the mother’s life can only be saved by such an act, and in those cases only it may be that a sinful act saves at least one life. However, it has been seen in Britain that many terminations are performed in the name of the ‘mental health’ of the young mother, resulting in the current situation of ‘abortion on demand.’ We condemn abortion on grounds of ‘sex-selection’ as practised in some communities, and disagree that abortion is the only option when pregnancy is the consequence of rape. We feel that with around 180,000 abortions per year in Britain, the practise has become simply another form of contraception.

**What are your views on suicide?**

The taking of any life is wrong, including your own. Suicide should never be an option for dealing with emotional pain. The growing suicide rate in the UK - one death every four hours - is just one effect of a depressive modern life. Striving for unrealistic goals, unsustainable working conditions and long hours, fractured relationships and a lack of community support, all contribute to the long-term stress that results in poor mental health and ultimately severe depression. Our body is on loan to us and we must take care of it while we use it for this one life. The lessons we learn as we cope with pain are exactly what we need. Suicide cuts short that learning process, and no matter how unloved we feel, the final act leaves behind many grieving family members and friends.

**What happens to someone who commits suicide?**

Suicide is the interruption of the course of life by one’s own actions. Death takes place not by accident, disease or any natural causes, but because of the intense desire to bring that life to a close. It is very unfortunate. Normally, someone who takes their own life does not immediately receive another birth but has to wait for some years for rebirth. The soul needs a body to interact and perform more good deeds for its own elevation. Suicide means that no new body is provided, since the last one was intensely rejected. The lesson to be learned by the soul is that the human body is a precious opportunity to make progress and should never be minimised. However, there is one clause: one who has performed bhakti-yoga and who perfected their life to some degree will immediately begin another human life at the same level. Why would someone who has perfected their life to any degree wish to commit suicide? We are all born with an accumulation of residual karmic reactions from scores of previous lives. Sometimes, despite taking to the practise of bhakti-yoga, the burden of this weight can prove too much to bear.

**What do you have to say about euthanasia?**

Euthanasia, or ‘mercy killing,’ may at first seem noble-spirited, as the idea is to bring a sick person’s life to an end by administering an overdose of painkillers. Death is hardly ever without pain, and family members may wish to bring about an end to pain for a loved one, especially if death is anyway imminent. But any form of intentionally bringing about the end of a life is killing, and is always wrong at any stage or condition of life.

**But what if a person has a terminal disease?**

According to ancient tradition, in the face of incurable illness, someone may choose to end his own life by choosing prayopavesh , or death by fasting. Although this is a form of suicide, it is neither abrupt nor an act of the desperation borne of depression. It is, rather, a choice which provides a gradual approach to death, and adequate time to prepare oneself. The difference is that this can only be chosen by oneself, not by others.

**Would you agree that overpopulation is a problem in the world today?**

We would not agree. The current population of the world is around 7.3 billion. Even if the population were 9 billion, you could fit everyone – with a house and a garden – into Alaska. There is an enormous amount of land available – we just don’t use it wisely.

**But how could everyone eat?**

Of course, it is not space for them to live that is the problem, but water and food. Yet currently there is enough land under cultivation that could supply enough food for the entire world – and more. The difficulty is that a huge percentage of land is set aside for cash crops such as coffee, or for growing grains for feeding to cows for the meat industry. This industry also takes millions of gallons of water. A far more sensible solution to ‘overpopulation’ is to fully use agricultural land for plantbased protein. This will result in millions more being fed adequately.

## Volume 3: Mechanics of spirituality

*The path of yoga incorporates an array of regulated rituals and practices which support our growth. One may question the necessity of such an instituted and rigid programme. Shouldn't our spiritual journey take a more natural course according to inspiration? While spirituality is indeed spontaneous and individual, certain processes and patterns of behaviour help uncover that dormant consciousness. Tried and tested external practices open up an internal world of bottomless depth.*

## Spirit + Ritual

Spiritual life is about internal development, a change in consciousness from matter to spirit. But the path of Krishna consciousness seems to involve a number of ritualised actions that appear to be external. One may question the need for ritualistic actions in everyday life and their relevance to one’s inner development. The truth of the matter is that although our external actions may not always be internally inspired, they nevertheless produce helpful adjustments of consciousness.

**What do I do if I find chanting boring?**

According to psychologists, there are several reasons for boredom. One is when you have lots of mental energy and there are many things you’d like to be doing but you can’t because you have to do something else. This can also result in you feeling you’re not in control of the situation, that you’ve somehow been forced to be there. Boredom also comes from an inability to focus your mind on the activity you’ve chosen to do. There’s another soundtrack playing in the back room of your mind and you can’t concentrate.

For all these situations here are some tips: (1) Remind yourself that the chanting is your own personal commitment; something you decided to do because you wanted to, because you know it will make you happier, and not because someone else asked you. Know that you’re working towards a future goal of a new, improved you. (2) Understand that in the beginning of your meditation period there will usually be some mental resistance. It normally takes some minutes before your roaming mind settles down and relaxes into the rhythm of the mantra. Don’t give up before this happens. (3) Minimise mental distraction by not eating late at night. Overnight digestion produces dreams which affect the mind the next morning. Spicy food also produces a restless ‘morning-after’ mind and disturbs concentration. (4) Try doing your meditation at the most advantageous time of the day, before the raja-guna begins around 8.00 am. Before sunrise is the best time. Give up a little sleep if you have to. (5) Don’t use your mind to make decisions or think of anything stressful just before you begin meditating – and don’t become deviated into anything else during meditation.

**How can I better understand the books and philosophy?**

A book is a written conversation, and as such it often needs more conversation to bring out the full meaning. Every sacred text usually comes with a bhasya , a commentary, and that also has an explanation known as a tika , which also has one called a tipani . You get the idea. The process is that the sacred conversation is made increasingly relevant and personal as it is expanded by qualified discussion. The ultimate point of this is when you sit with a guru who makes it all relevant to you. The principle is that you need to find someone who knows more about the text, put your questions to them, reveal your personal challenges and doubts, and listen carefully.

**You seem to have gone from purely spiritual to ritual – why all the rituals?**

There is a common misconception that ‘rituals’ are man-made; a set of behaviours artificially stuck on to something purely spiritual and ‘ritual-free.’ The facts of the matter are quite different. Ritual has always been a part of the spiritual path, since the very beginning. The Vedic Pancharatra texts describe ceremonial temple or home worship of the very same Vishnu mentioned in the Rig Veda, and the Yajur Veda describes elaborate fire ceremonies. Rituals such as these are an external manifestation of an evolved consciousness. If we perform the same rituals our consciousness will also evolve. Therefore they are not to be dispensed with as being superfluous to spiritual life – they are an intrinsic and time-tested element of it.

**What is it with all the coconuts and bananas? Why are they used in ceremonies?**

In Vedic science there is a hierarchy of plant life – poisonous or other harmful plants are considered to be at the bottom, and the most beneficial, useful and delicious plants at the top. Coconuts and bananas are right at the top. They can be used for a multitude of purposes; they grow in abundance and have health-giving properties. They are the gifts of God to humans and they are offered back to Him in ceremonies.

**What’s a fire sacrifice all about?**

It’s another way of making offerings to God. Fire is a natural element. Half is visible and half invisible, representing the transition between the world we can see with our eyes and the world we can’t. The grains and ghee we offer into the flames are transformed by fire and transferred to the world we cannot see. More than this though, it is a way of actually connecting to gods or God through the act of yagya or sacrifice. Traditionally, it was the chief way of making offerings, later supplanted by temple worship and most recently by the recitation of the names of God – the sacrifice of the Names, or Nama Yajna . But we still preserve the fire ceremonies for life-cycle events and important occasions.

**Aside from weddings, what other religious ceremonies are there?**

There are customs and ceremonies for each important stage of life. These are known as samskaras . Different numbers of ceremonies are given by various authors, but the main ones are ten in number and are as follows: The Wedding ( Vivaha ), Conception ( Garbhadana ), the Birth ceremony ( Jata ); the First Visit to the Temple ( Nishkramanam ) and the Naming ceremony ( Nama Karana ). These are followed by the giving of the First Grain Food ( Anna Prasana ), Haircutting ( Chuda Karana ) and the First day of School ( Vidya-rambha ). Before the age of sixteen comes the Vedic Initiation ( Upanayanam ) followed by, once again, the Wedding! At the end of life there are the funeral rites ( Antyesti ). All these ceremonies can be conducted by a brahmana and they make enjoyable and memorable life celebrations.

**Why do we put our two hands together in a gesture of ‘Namaste?’**

The universal greeting of placing the two palms together before the heart is the gesture of respect for others, for saints, for gods, and for God. Gestures like these are an embodiment of an inner thought, and just making the gesture helps to create the inner thought. It is one of the aspects of the science of ritual. The word Namaste, as many already know, means “I offer my respects to you,” and can be witnessed everywhere. The gesture can also be given as a hello, goodbye or a thank you.

**Why do the devotees at the temple say “Jaya!” and raise both their hands?**

This is a gesture of exultation or happy celebration. When we feel that something great has been achieved we raise both arms and cheer. This is a universal gesture, whether in sport, music or at times of great joy. It is also a gesture seen at a time of spiritual awakening, when all cares and anxieties have been relieved. The gesture can also be seen in the figures of the Pancha Tattva , the five associates who spread the teachings of Krishna five hundred years ago in India. They danced and sang together, raising their arms and crying out “Jaya!” The word jaya means ‘Victory!’

**Why do we not touch paper, books or other people with our feet?**

The feet are the lowest part of our person. In a dry, hot climate they are also unhygienic, carrying dirt and dust. The head is our highest part and is said to carry our pride. When we express our humility we bow down, bringing our head to the lowest level. This is particularly so when we bow down at the feet of God or a person who has dedicated their life to God. While the feet of a saint are said to be the place where we can lose our pride and become elevated in spirit, our own feet are considered to be quite the opposite. If we think highly of the spiritual credentials of others, it will be a natural consequence for us to consider our own feet to be not worthy of anyone else’s bowing. In this way, and out of respect, we do not touch anyone else with our feet, neither any holy article nor anything which is to be used in service to God.

**What is the ceremony with the waving of a peacock fan?**

Arati means ‘to greet,’ and is a festive ritual held six times every day. The service is a ceremonial presentation of a series of articles offered to the Lord on the altar. Such pleasing items as incense, lamps, flowers, fresh water, cloth, and cooling fans – including one made of a Tibetan yak’s tail and another of peacock feathers – are waved before the Deity to the accompaniment of the blowing of a conch-shell, the ringing of a bell and the chanting of prescribed mantras. The public can come and witness this and receive blessings in the form of the sanctified items as they are passed out from the altar. All the while, the congregation join in singing the kirtan being led with cymbals and drums.

**I saw some special plants in the greenhouse – what are they?**

That is Tulasi, a plant also known by the Latin names of Ocimum tenuiflorum or Ocimum sanctum . It is sacred to all those who worship Vishnu or Krishna. Every time a plate of food is offered on the altar, a leaf of the Tulasi plant is placed on the top. Garlands made of fresh, leafy Tulasi sprigs are offered to the Deity, and the buds and flowers of the plant are placed upon His feet. The devotees wear two or three strands of small wooden beads on their necks and these are made of the Tulasi stems. The devotee’s meditation beads, or japa-mala, are also often made of the wood from the Tulasi bush. You’ll see a plant or two decorating the altar, and every morning one of these plants is offered a short arati ceremony while devotees sing a special song describing the benefits of worship of the Tulasi plant. Because Tulasi grows best in a tropical climate, the greenhouses are always kept warm.

**What does the OM symbol mean?**

It’s not actually a symbol, although it is often used in that way as a generic indicator of Hinduism. OM, or AUM is actually a word made up of three letters of the Sanskrit alphabet. It is a kind of respectful address, or invocation, and is the first word in the many verses of the Vedas, the sacred scriptures of the tradition.

**Occasionally I’ve even seen a Swastika used in temples – I thought that was a Nazi symbol?**

Actually, it’s an ancient symbol of good fortune. The very words Swasti-Ka means “May good luck be here.” It was used all over the ancient world in slightly different forms – even here in Britain! Before the rise of the National Socialist party in Germany, the symbol was very common on greetings cards and lucky charms. Unfortunately it was misappropriated, and no-one can see it these days without thinking of the terrors of the Second World War.

## At the temple

*The term for a temple in ancient Sanskrit is ‘mandir,’ which literally means ‘where the mind becomes steady.’ Although one’s inner state of consciousness is the most important factor in one’s spiritual life, the temple with all its rituals and regulated ceremonies has a lot to do with achieving that state of consciousness. Understanding why we do what we do can add a lot of value to the activities we carry out at the temple, and thus accelerate our spiritual progress.*

**Why do I need to come to the temple? God is everywhere.**

Yes, that’s true, but it is not always easy to see Him everywhere. For this reason, it is helpful to go to a place set aside for that purpose. Many people find the forest, beach or mountain helpful for thinking of God because those places are relatively free from distraction. But the image of God, manifesting on the altar, is an important part of focusing your mind on Him, and according to the Pancharatra texts, God actually appears directly in His temple image. So you can directly perform sacred services to Him in that spot. The other benefits of coming to the temple are that you get the experience of shared worship – something unavailable by yourself, you get to enjoy the company of likeminded people, and you’ll also have enlightening conversation and delicious blessed food afterwards.

**Why do I have to learn Sanskrit prayers? Why can’t I pray in English?**

God understands every language spoken on Earth, including the voices of all the animals, so you don’t have to speak to Him in a special language during your personal prayer. But if you’d like to share in the hymns and songs spoken and sung by the greatest of His saints, as well as learn the words actually spoken by Him during His avatars, that opportunity is also available to you. Besides, Sanskrit is a beautifulsounding language, and the poetry composed by the great devotees is particularly pleasing. (The translations into native languages are also available so that you can know what you are saying!). These days, Sanskrit verses and expressions are a common way of communicating with devotees of Krishna all over the world. There is also a revival of Sanskrit right now, and it is spoken as a living language by around 200,000 people.

**Why do you have to give money? Is that part of the tradition?**

You don’t have to do anything that makes you feel uncomfortable, but if you feel grateful for what you’ve received at the temple, or if you’d like to support the good work carried out by the devotees, then a contribution is always very welcome. The temple runs on the kindness of our supporters, and we’d like to think that Krishna blesses those who give according to their means. Money is simply an easy way to give a small proportion of your day’s work, but you can also come and volunteer your time and skills to the cause of Krishna’s service. Certainly it is a part of the tradition to give something to support the worship, and the mission to reach out to others.

**Why can I not go to the temple during my monthly period?**

Menstruation is a rather vexed subject among western educated women in particular. Often questions are raised as to why there are restrictions on a woman’s activities during her period when it is a perfectly natural biological process and an integral part of womanhood. The simple answer lies in the matter of ritual purity, which does not conform to concepts of microbiology and is not the subject of negotiation based on the latest social politics! External cleanliness is an important factor. The presence of any exudates on the body is a disqualification for any ritual act, both in males and in females. In the presence of spittle, pus, urine, faeces, blood and so on, no-one is permitted to cook food for others or to enter a temple, or perform any ritual act whatsoever. So in the presence of the active flow of menstrual blood, women are relieved of the burden of cooking, cleaning or exerting themselves in any way. Of course, in modern times, methods of controlling blood flow have improved, but the culture has remained. In Indian society the entire burden of caring for the family and guests falls on women. Mothers in general, and in the west in particular, do not get days off! So, one of the aspects of this system of restriction is to give women three to four days of rest per month.

**When there is a death in the family, why can I not come to the temple?**

Mourning for a loved one, and the extra observations involved with a death and preparing for a funeral, all take time. According to tradition, during this period it is also considered proper that one restricts one’s socialising or going out. Instead, relatives and friends visit the home. The temple also visits the home in the form of the devotees who sing the names of Krishna and read from sacred books such as the Bhagavadgita. According to one’s family tradition the period may last from a few days up to one month. It is also customary that no cooking is done in the home, instead, friends take turns to cook simple dishes - at least for a few days. The mourners cannot serve food to those who visit their home, and visitors cannot eat in the home until the mourning period is over.

**What happens at a Hare Krishna funeral?**

A funeral has several purposes. It is a chance for mourners to come and express their own grief and offer condolences to the family and friends of the departed. It is also an opportunity to give thanks for the life of the person who has died, to recount some of their contributions and what a difference they made in the lives of others. Since it is the very last stage of the soul’s embodied journey for this life, prayers are said for the continued journey of the departed towards Krishna.

Finally, it is a time to say goodbye and to commit the mortal remains back to the elements. In our funerals, that element is fire. Sometimes the mourners will line up to scatter flower petals into the open coffin. Hare Krishna funerals take place in modern crematoria and usually last forty minutes. The ashes of the deceased are often taken to India for ceremonial dispersal in a holy river.

**Why do we walk in a circle around something?**

This is a ritual or ceremonial act of respect for a holy person or object, not limited to India or Hinduism. It is known as pradakshina or ‘circum-ambulation.’ It is performed in a clockwise direction, often walking in three circles. It is said that to walk in this way, keeping the right shoulder towards the holy centre, serves to bind the holiness to your heart. The opposite is to walk in the anti-clockwise direction, known as apradakshina , and this serves to distance the holiness from the heart. When Ravana was on the battlefield with Sri Rama, he circled Him in this way.

**Why do we regard trees and plants as sacred?**

All life is sacred, even trees and plants. To cut a tree, with no purpose, is considered a sin. But sometimes, if a particular tree is Swasti , or ‘auspicious,’ or if the plant gives us life, shade or blessings, we offer respects to it, considering it as a gift from God. In this way, even a common plant or tree may serve to remind us of God and His many blessings. In some rare cases the plant is the earthly manifestation of a higher, divine personality.

Such is the case with the plant known as Tulasi. In the higher realms, she is a divine female attendant of Sri Krishna. Her leaves are picked and placed upon every food offering taken to the shrine. Each morning the devotees assemble to sing a song dedicated to her and also to walk around her in respect. To water her is considered to be an act of devotion to Krishna. After she dies, her wood is taken to make the neck beads ( kanthi-mala ) that devotees wear, as well as the meditation beads ( japa-mala ).

**What is the significance of the lotus? Why do we say ‘lotus eyes’ and so on?**

Viewed from any angle, or at any stage of its growth, the lotus flower is quite the most beautiful of all flowers. While nothing in this world can actually compare to its divine creator, this has not prevented poets from comparing the Lord’s form to the most beautiful wonders of the natural world. Krishna’s face has thus been compared to a fully bloomed lotus in its radiance, and His eyes compared to the shape of the lotus petal. The palms of His hands and feet are compared to the pink lotus, and His bodily colour to the beauty of a blue lotus. The gradually expanding consciousness of the devotee is compared to the slowly blooming lotus.

**Why do we blow the conch?**

The conch is one of nature’s musical instruments. When the end of the empty shell is cut and air blown through the inner coils, it sounds like a sonorous trumpet. The deep and resonant note produced is also said to resemble the sound of OM. It attracts the attention of all who hear it, even from a distance, and especially when more than one is blown at the same time. It can be seen in the hand of Vishnu, and Sri Krishna, Arjuna and all the warriors blew their respective conches to herald their presence before the great battle of Kurukshetra. The conch, or shanka , is blown at the beginning and end of every arati ceremony and on special or ceremonial occasions.

**Why are women and men separated in the temple?**

That is a custom to help preserve the one-pointed spiritual focus of the temple. It helps to maintain the sanctity of the space, and the natural tendency to socialise before the altar is kept in check. Besides, sometimes the temple is extremely crowded and a little separation serves to protect women from being pushed. But it’s just in the temple room itself and other crowded spaces – there is no separation anywhere else.

**What is the significance of the burning lamps being passed over the hair and eyes?**

All items that are first offered to Krishna on the altar are afterwards offered to everyone else. The item is considered to be ‘non-different’ from Krishna and a blessing to be accepted by the grateful devotees. The flowers can be passed around and smelled, as can the fragrant incense; and the food – now prasada – can be tasted. The flame is simply touched, and the heat applied to the head. Some worshipper’s like to think of the flame as a protection, or that when the flame’s heat is transferred to their eyes, that they will perhaps begin to develop the inner vision to see the Lord as He is.

**Why do we show our left side when bowing?**

It is a tradition to show the right shoulder to a holy person when walking around them, and the left shoulder when bowing down. Service is always performed from the right hand side of the person you are serving, which means you will stand to the left as seen by others. Consequently the services are offered from the left of the altar, and the priest stands on this side.

**Why do people bow down to each other or touch the feet?**

It is said that ‘freedom from pride opens the gate to the eternal realm,’ and so the cultivation of humility is important for those on the path of bhakti-yoga. Humility is a voluntary acceptance of the worth of another, and is brought about by spiritual cultivation. It has nothing to do with humiliation, which is a negative emotional state. Offering our respect, gratitude and service to others helps to foster our humility, and bowing down immediately diminishes our pride. It is quite common throughout the eastern world to bow to elders and teachers, and in India touching the feet is another way of offering respect. However, touching of the feet is usually done with the permission of the other person, and is mostly done when that person is already one’s teacher or guide.

**Why do we take our shoes off before entering the temple? And why can’t we wear slippers?**

The temple is a clean and sanctified space, and shoes always tend to bring in the dirt from outside. Removing the shoes (and taking off one’s hat, or covering one’s head) are also ways of indicating that you’re coming into the presence of someone greater than yourself. Traditionally, shoes are left in a special room for that purpose. You may wear slippers inside the temple building, but not in the temple room itself. The only exception made is for the disabled.

**Do you have priests?**

We would define a priest as someone who conducts religious services on behalf of others. In the temple we have a system of training and initiation so that over four years a member can become qualified to conduct religious services. The priests serve on the altar and you will see them on the main shrine performing the arati ceremony. They also conduct lifepassage services such as birth ceremonies, weddings and funerals. We consider the priest to be a role and not a spiritual level; a person’s devotion is the real measure of their spirituality, not their ceremonial function. Both men and women serve as priests.

**Why is there a bell just inside the temple room door?**

You are entering the Lord’s private chamber, so to draw attention to your entrance, and that you are here only for service, you lightly ring the bell. Not loudly, just enough for Him to hear – and He has very acute ears! You have come for an audience ( darshan ) with God, so you can go immediately to the shrine, bow down and make your prayers. Taking photographs is not an immediate priority. Imagine you were with a very important person – you would probably speak with them before taking a picture! So it is with Krishna. The etiquette observed is not to talk in front of the altar, and not to eat anything directly in front.

**What are the curtains on the shrine for, and why can’t we have darshan all the time?**

The curtains are closed during the times when the Deities are being bathed and dressed, when meals are served to Them, and while They are resting. To avoid disappointment you can learn the times of darshan and arati services, six of which are held throughout the day, from 4.30 am through to 9.00 pm. There is always enough time to see the Deities if you plan ahead.

**Can I photograph the shrine?**

Yes you can, although the traditional way is to approach the Deity with respect and to first spend some time looking with your eyes. Begin by gazing at the feet of Krishna and then move up slowly to the face. You can then offer your prayers. Only after this can photos be taken. You should not take ‘selfies’ with the Deity as this involves turning your back to the shrine. There should be no gossiping before the Deity.

**Is all the food at the temple is cooked by the devotees?**

Yes, the cooking of food six times a day is an important part of the worship of Krishna. We have two kitchens; one is described as the ‘Deity Kitchen,’ and all the food cooked there is only for the altar offerings. When an offering is cooked, it is placed on silver dishes – one for each Deity – and it is known as naivedyam . After the offerings have been made there is a long period while the Lord eats and then the food is known as prasadam . It is not only delicious but full of blessing power. Devotees are very happy just to be served even a small portion of it.

**Why do you have a painting of a lion with lots of blood on the floor?**

That is Narasimha or Nrsimhadeva, the half-man, half-lion form of Vishnu. When the demon king Hiranyakashipu persecuted his saintly son Prahlada, it was more than the Lord could bear. He burst out of a pillar to do battle with the king, eventually dispatching him upon His knees. Although the image looks somewhat grisly, the devotees like it because it reminds them that no matter where they are, the Lord will always protect them.

**What is the painting of the five people with their hands in the air?**

Those five figures are collectively known as the Pancha-Tattva, or ‘The Absolute Truth in Five Features.’ Krishna comes into this world as His own devotee, and, accompanied by these four associates, travels and spreads the religious practise for the age: the chanting of the names of God. From left to right in the painting you will see Advaita-Acharya; Nityananda Prabhu; Caitanya Mahaprabhu; Gadadhara Pandit, and Srivasa Thakur. They have their hands up because they are dancing together in sankirtan, the group chanting of the Hare Krishna mantra.

**Why do people bow down?**

It is said that the head carries the pride of a man, and by lowering the head before God, we rise in humility. All religions practise some form of bending, kneeling or bowing before the altar. The version you’ll see in the temple will be either one of getting down on both knees and placing the hands in front of you, then touching the floor with your head. The other form is lying facedown on the floor with legs and arms outstretched. This is known as ‘becoming like a stick’ or dandavat .

**Do I have to sit cross-legged?**

We can provide a chair if you need one. We know that everyone’s knees can’t always manage sitting cross-legged for more than a few minutes. But it does slightly slow the blood flow to the lower body, and consequently there’s more blood that gets to the brain. All the better for your thinking about spiritual things! That posture is traditional, and some find it helpful, but if being comfortable is more important, any posture is good – apart from falling asleep!

**What about joining the palms together?**

That is an ancient form of respect from one person to another. It can be used to honour the highest good in a person you meet, or it can be used when you come in front of the altar to pray. It is also used as a goodbye. It is considered to be more respectful than a handshake, especially with those whom you do not know well.

**What are the yellow scarves with red writing on them?**

Those are cotton shawls with the Sanskrit words of the Hare Krishna mantra printed on them. They can help to keep you warm, and devotees use them at times of prayer or ceremony, or at any other time to show the ‘team’ they support! They are only intended for the upper body, however, and must never be used to wrap the legs since they have the divine names of God printed on them.

**What is the white liquid in a silver bowl by the altar?**

Krishna is bathed in the morning with yogurt, honey and Tulasi buds. Some of this is collected as it flows down to His feet and transferred to a bowl where three drops are taken in their right hands by temple visitors. This is a very special blessing, and is said to prevent untimely death. The drink is known as ‘the Nectar of the Feet’ or charana-amrita.

**What is the Gayatri?**

The Gayatri is the name given to a poetic metre of 24 syllables. There are many Gayatri mantras, but the term is most commonly used to indicate a particular mantra known as the Brahma Gayatri; the mantra given at the time of upanayanam , the initiation ( diksha ) ceremony. This mantra is accompanied by investiture with the sacred thread, given to young males, often between the ages of 8 and 16. The Brahma Gayatri was originally given by Lord Krishna to Brahma, who was then inspired to speak the Veda. The Gayatri is a secret mantra given by the guru to the disciple, and is not meant to be broadcasted as, for instance, in the form of singing it loudly.

## Regulated devotion

*Along with the rituals, the path of Krishna consciousness seems to place a great emphasis on counting things: there are numbers for rounds of japa and regulative principles, and lists for almost everything else.*

**There are also many other rules. How important are lists and rules, and why? If Krishna’s name is non-different from Him, why do I not feel anything while chanting?**

Perhaps you are distracted by other thoughts. Even the thought of “I’m not feeling anything” is a serious distraction. The chanting works if you both chant and listen attentively, allowing the mantra to supersede any other thoughts you might be having. Even if your mind drifts away from the chanting, simply bring it back and re-focus. You’ll experience something if you chant for long enough.

**Why do I have to chant a fixed number of rounds?**

It takes around a month to develop a new habit, and new habits are best developed by daily regulation. Counting the number of times you go round the circle of 108 japa beads is a traditional practise: it fixes your mind during a set period of chanting, prevents you from chanting less than your chosen commitment, and provides you with a sense of achievement.

**Can you chant without beads?**

Yes, you can chant without beads but you’ll find it impossible to count how many times you’ve chanted without using them! Devotees have traditionally used a string of beads made of carved wood, often from the medicinal Neem tree or the sacred Tulasi. Should one not have access to japa beads, it is possible to use the thumb to count the twelve finger joints nine times (108) or to use a tally counter.

**Why can’t you do it privately?**

Mantra meditation is a private practise, in the sense that it is your personal contemplation and doesn’t involve anyone else. But if you chant with others there is the benefit of mutual support and concentration.

**Why do people have bags on their hands? Why are people pointing when they’re chanting?**

The bags are used because it is customary not to display the japa beads while chanting on them. They are also used to keep the beads clean, and the strap can be used to hang the bag around the neck. The finger ‘pointing’ out of the bead-bag is not actually pointing at anything at all. The index finger is known as ahamkara, or the ‘ego’ finger, and it is used to accuse others when the ego is aroused. As a consequence, it is traditionally kept away from sacred items such as beads or other ritual objects.

**Do I have to chant every day?**

Do you brush your teeth every day? Do you eat every day? Do you sleep every night? If the answer is yes it will be because what you do every day is very important. Less important things we tend to do less frequently. But the most important we do every day. Our life depends on what we do every day. Chanting the maha-mantra is the most important thing that anyone can do because it frees the soul, and that is the very purpose of life. Like eating, chanting nourishes us; like brushing our teeth, chanting cleans our mind; and like sleeping, chanting restores our vitality. So yes, chanting every day!

**Is it better to chant one round really well, rather than more rounds in a distracted way?**

Both are important, and this is a question that does not really have an either/or answer. It is very good to chant one round really well, free from offensive mentality or distraction.

But if you did chant such a round you would immediately chant some more – you wouldn’t be able to resist it! And in order to reach the stage of chanting one pure round you’d have to have already gone through the stage of chanting many more rounds in a distracted way. First come rounds with distraction, then comes the one round without distraction. The first is probable and the second is entirely possible. Both are important, but one focused round of japa is better, so aim for that, then chant more of them!

**Is it more important to chant or to read?**

They are both important. From the chanting we develop the desire to read the scriptures daily, and from the scriptures we’ll understand how important the chanting is. But they do have relative importance. An example: water and oxygen are both important for our life. We can survive without water for seven days, but without oxygen we can survive for only seven minutes. They are both essential for our survival, but one depends on the other – without oxygen you can’t drink water. Similarly, without chanting you can’t do anything else. Chanting is the one essential practise upon which all the other aspects of spiritual life depend.

**Is japa more important than kirtan?**

Kirtan is easier for those who are newcomers to the maha-mantra, and it is easier as a form of chanting the maha-mantra. There is the melody, the musical accompaniment, the rhythm, the companionship, the call-and-response singing, the elevated, joyful mood in the room – and the dancing. All of this makes an attractive package in which to experience Krishna’s holy names. Japa by comparison is a relatively lonely pursuit, involving only one tongue, two ears and some wooden beads. And yet the sound of the maha-mantra is exactly the same in both cases. The difference with japa is that in just seven minutes we can chant one attentive round of 108 mantras. The same number of mantras in musical kirtan mode would take at least thirty-six minutes. To sing sixteen rounds worth of maha-mantras – 1,728 mantras – would take over nine hours. So for sheer practicality and sustainability in our daily life it seems more advisable to make your personal japa the foundation of everything.

**Why are sixteen rounds the standard?**

Sixteen rounds is the standard for initiation, but many devotees have made their decision to chant more or less rounds than this. Some chant four rounds, for instance, and this is their own personal standard. Others chant 6, 8, 12, 20, 25 or 32 as a standard. What is important is to choose your own personal standard and to stick with it. Gradually the number can be increased.

In India, 64 rounds each day is a standard for many devotees, but when Srila Prabhupada tried to introduce this in 1966, his young followers protested. When even 32 was too much, he finally drew the line, and so sixteen rounds became the standard for his initiated disciples. To become a disciple today simply means to follow this original request, and to not go below that number. However, Srila Prabhupada felt that he was being particularly lenient and mentioned that this full complement of rounds could be completed in less than two hours. “You have 24 hours,” he reasoned with his disciples, and he felt that 90 minutes or two hours wasn’t too much of an imposition on them. It is a fact that, when the mantra meditation is done properly, the experience of chanting is so pleasurable that the time simply flies by.

**What is Ekadasi?**

Ekadasi means the eleventh day of the moon and falls twice a month. Just as certain places have a special atmosphere conducive for our purification and spiritual advancement, so do certain phases of time. The Ekadasi day is spiritually surcharged with devotional energy. On this day we do not eat grains, beans, legumes or anything containing them.

There are many side benefits from observing Ekadasi, such as good health (regular fasting allows our digestive organs to rest) and economy (if everyone followed Ekadasi, imagine how much food would be saved). But by far the main purpose of following Ekadasi is to help us awaken our love for Krishna. Srila Prabhupada taught that following Ekadasi means minimising the demands of the body and maximising our service to Krishna.

On Ekadasi we should eat more simply and try to put into practice the maxim: “Eat to live, not live to eat.” He advised that on Ekadasi we increase our chanting of the holy names. To a disciple who asked, “Should we chant twenty-five rounds on Ekadasi?” Srila Prabhupada replied, “Why only twenty-five rounds? You should chant as many as possible.”

## Outreach

*There’s probably nothing the world likes less right now than the religious preacher. Either a figure of fun or a threatening source of volatile rhetoric, the preacher is almost universally mistrusted. And yet the Krishna consciousness movement is a ‘preaching mission.’ So what about preaching for Krishna? Is it still relevant or appropriate today to try to change people’s minds about belief? Here we look at the difference between sharing a message and ‘conversion,’ and then overcoming our own resistance once we’ve decided to do it.*

**Why do you sing in public places? Isn’t religion a private thing?**

Certainly your relationship with God and your personal prayers to Him are private. But if God Himself appeared before you and personally asked you to sing His names so that others could hear, what would you do? In the Srimad Bhagavatam, compiled 5,000 years ago, it was predicted that a golden incarnation would appear in the Age of Kali, the present age, and that He would sing the names of Krishna surrounded by His associates. When Krishna actually appeared in this form that is exactly what He did, and what He asked everyone else to do. That congregational singing, or Harinama-sankirtan , was spread all over India, and has carried on until the present day. Now you will see people of all nations, in every major city of the world, singing the Hare Krishna mantra with drums and cymbals. It gives millions of people the chance to hear the transcendental names of Krishna, and to continue their spiritual journey with fresh inspiration.

**I believe that knowledge should be free. Why do you ask money for your books?**

Knowledge is free, but book production is costly, so we ask everyone for a small contribution to help with the printing. In our temples there are two or three daily classes that are free to all visitors – every day of the week, throughout the year. Similarly, we distribute large quantities of delicious prasadam for free, to all comers. Book selling is one way we support everything else we do. Our books are printed in large quantities, sometimes 100,000 for just one title: that keeps the costs down. We also give them away if people genuinely have no money. Noone is denied. But if someone can make a donation to cover the cost price, even a little more to support the temple, they will still have an interesting book at much less than it would cost in a shop, and they will have done a good deed by supporting charitable work.

**Aren’t you worried about all the attention you get when you sing outside?**

The singing with cymbals and Indian drums in public is not a form of exhibitionism, entertainment or selfish attention-seeking. The chanting of the names of God in call-and-response fashion is an act of sharing the practise with others – giving them an opportunity to experience it for themselves and perhaps trying it out when they get home. There are many devotees today who had their first experience of Krishna consciousness through the street singing. Practically speaking, that is where it all began, with Srila Prabhupada singing under a tree in a park in New York City. The vision of devotees singing in the street is the enduring image of ‘Hare Krishna’ all around the world.

**Why are you trying to convert people?**

Krishna consciousness does not involve a ‘conversion’ from one religion to another. It is not a set of religious beliefs amongst many others. The path of bhakti-yoga is a spiritual discipline that can easily restore our dormant relationship with God, no matter how we may worship Him presently, or which holy book we honour. God is not Christian or Muslim or Jewish or Hindu, and the soul is also none of these. The soul is purely spiritual by nature, but has forgotten God. Bhakti-yoga is non-sectarian and universal. It consists simply in serving God without any selfish motive. If a person is a convinced Christian or Muslim then we don’t ask them to convert to anything else, just to add something to their life: the practises of bhakti.

**Aren’t we destined to practice a certain religion? Why are you trying to change that?**

You might have been born into a family or community that practised its faith in a particular way. That much was your destiny. But you are also born with the free will to enquire and to discover how to further your spiritual progress in this life. That’s something different from having a religious badge. Faith in God is not about the culture of your birth community; it’s the deepest part of you inside. The part of you that needs to find answers.

Krishna consciousness is the original consciousness of the soul, not a set of sectarian religious beliefs. You have an identity that you’re born with, and as you pass through life you adopt many other identities, most of which you can decide to change. You can change your hairstyle, the way you dress, your name, your passport, your educational qualifications, your career – and you can also change your religion. All of them are external to the real you, and none of them means you become a different person. That part of you doesn’t change; the real you always remains the same. Krishna consciousness is therefore not about changing religion – it’s about growing your spirituality within, whatever religion you follow.

**I heard everyone should ‘preach’ – is that true?**

Most people don’t like the word preach because they think it means aggressively arguing for a set of beliefs that the other person doesn’t share. Preaching by this definition is either boring or threatening – either of which will lose you all your friends. And who wants to lose friends? But imagine if you’d just seen a good movie and you wanted to tell everyone at work how they should try to see it too. Is that preaching? Yes, by definition it is.

The word preach means ‘to exhort another person up to a higher level of action,’ something that will make them healthier or happier. By sharing your happiness with someone, you won’t be boring or threatening them; unless you are by nature self-righteous and overbearing, that is! If Krishna consciousness has made you better-informed or one degree happier, then telling others about it is the greatest service you could do for them. The qualification for being a preacher is all about wanting to share something good, and doing it in an amiable way. The movement of Srila Prabhupada is a preaching movement, and every member is therefore a preacher.

**How can I overcome my fear of speaking to others about Krishna Consciousness?**

Most of us have two main fears regarding preaching, and the first is always this: “What if they don’t like what I’m saying and reject me?” Rejection hurts. Nobody wants to be rejected. We all want to be loved, and when we finally pluck up courage to speak about Krishna and someone rejects our best attempts, we can’t help but take it personally. The best antidote for this fear of rejection is to realise that it’s not about you at all. It’s only about that person and Krishna. You are merely the messenger, the postman. What they do with the message is up to them and Krishna within their heart. And how you feel about preaching on behalf of Krishna is between you and Him. The only thing you have to do is stop wanting to be loved when you go out to preach! Just take it for granted that only one out of many people will be remotely interested, and then you’ll never be disappointed.

Srila Prabhupada said to imagine that you’re selling diamonds. You may not get many customers, but the ones that buy are the ones that appreciate real diamonds. And as every expert preacher can testify, once you stop thinking about yourself and begin thinking of yourself as simply a servant of Krishna, you’ll immediately become happy and everyone will want to listen to you!

**What if I don’t have enough knowledge to speak to others?**

And this is the second fear we have: “I don’t know enough about the philosophy of Krishna consciousness to speak about it. Besides, even if I did, I’m not convinced enough to convince anyone else! I’m not even a devotee!” This kind of self-doubt is another doorway to the mental platform, and a crippling thought for the preacher. As above, it stems from the fear of rejection mixed with the self-doubt that you are not really a devotee of Krishna because you are insufficiently convinced. Even worse is the thought that: “Perhaps Krishna does not want me to represent Him – perhaps He doesn’t even like me?” The solution to this almost universal problem was resolved when a young disciple asked Srila Prabhupada a similar question: “Should we wait until we’re pure before we go out to preach?” His spiritual master laughed and replied:

**“Oh if you wait, that day will never come**

Preaching is such an important activity, not simply for the mission, but as a powerful method of self-purification. By going out to preach to others we take time to learn beforehand so that we don’t forget what to say. Then, when we inevitably say the wrong thing, or forget what to say, we learn from our mistakes. And on some occasions we speak from the heart with words that don’t seem to be ours. At these times we understand Krishna Himself to be speaking through us, using us as an instrument. The experience is so tangible that we become convinced of our actual relationship with Krishna, and of His affection for us.

**How can we ignite interest in people – most people don’t seem to be attracted?**

Yes, some people are like damp grass and some are like dry grass. It takes longer for some people to reach the point of ignition! Your skill as a preacher is to say something of interest to the person in front of you, and to be polite, affable, accessible and patient. Everyone finds spiritual topics at least marginally interesting – you just need to know what their interest is. So listening is even more important than speaking. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Thakur said the job of a preacher is to find the spark inside someone and gently fan it into a blazing fire.

**Why do we go door-to-door?**

This is the original form of preaching recommended by Sri Chaitanya Mahaprabhu. Back around 1505 AD the first to take the sankirtan message out to the neighbourhood in Bengal, India, were Haridas Thakura and Nityananda Prabhu. They went ghare-ghare , or door-to-door, and they also met with a mixed response, just like our devotees today. Some things never change. Yet from these earliest attempts many thousands came to follow the teachings. We find that people are generally friendlier in their own home; the stresses of the high street or the workplace are far away, and they can offer a few minutes of their time, take a book or magazine from us, and even offer their hospitality.

**Why are book distribution and Harinama so important?**

As devotees, we are all assisting Srila Prabhupada in his work to share Krishna consciousness with as many as possible. Our movement has one motto: “ Jiva Doya, Nama Ruchi, Vaishnava Seva ,” which means: “Kindness to all living beings, Attraction for the Divine Name, and Service to Vaishnavas.” These are the sacred principles upon which the movement is built. Distribution of books about Krishna, and singing the holy names of Krishna, accomplish all three of these foundational principles. A book is a very substantial object and, once purchased, can remain on someone’s bookshelf for years. We have many devotees who came to Krishna consciousness as a result from a book given to them years ago.

The sight of devotees singing in the streets is something unforgettable for most people. They might not join in, but they will always remember the sight and sound of the Harinama. As such, whenever they hear the Hare Krishna mantra again, they will have another opportunity to enquire more, and perhaps to sing along this time. And because it was his life’s work to have both books and kirtan in every town and village, if we help Srila Prabhupada by doing it, book distribution and Harinama are the greatest service to a Vaishnava.

**Should we find newer and more relevant ways to preach?**

There is actually no limit to the ways that preaching can be done. The only thing preventing us from doing so is our ingenuity and perseverance. Srila Prabhupada’s request was that we use our ‘intelligent western brains’ to come up with new ideas to ‘deliver your countrymen.’ So in the 1960s the distribution of colour magazines and large, hard-bound books was done in the streets, train stations, airports and homes. In the 1970s the sounds of kirtan were disseminated through pop music songs and vinyl albums. Devotional theatre was developed and taken on tour in HGV trucks remodelled as temples. Films followed and in the 1980s television and radio appearances. In the mid 1980s the devotees of Krishna were the first to use digital broadcasting in Italy, using 17 radio transmitters to cover the country with a 24-hour Krishna Radio. A big circus tent toured the country with a festival and show.

The 1990s saw the birth of preaching Krishna consciousness through the internet, and in Russia the first Hare Krishna television studio. Whatever ways in which rampant materialism is being spread, these are also the same ways that an alternative message can be spread. As for relevancy, Srila Prabhupada asked his disciples to speak and write ‘in your own language,’ always making Krishna consciousness relevant and accessible by ‘showing how Krishna consciousness is the solution to all the world’s problems.’

## Culture

*From moustaches to ‘cow poo’ and cricket, here’s a miscellany of commonly-asked questions about the niggly little details of the Krishna devotee’s life. Too small to ask in public, but so important they won’t go away; these questions are not the deepest philosophical ones, but unless you have them answered they’ll be with you forever.*

**Why do I have to get up early?**

The word ‘early’ is a relative term – it all depends on what time you go to bed! Those who go to sleep at 8.00 pm think that 4.00 am is the right time to get up. For this reason, the middle of the sleeping period – the middle of the night – is called ‘midnight.’ Formerly, people would go to sleep earlier and wake up earlier. So ‘midnight’ came at the right time! This is not so in our stressed, 24/7 world – now many are only managing to get to sleep at midnight. Meditation the next morning is then difficult.

But the morning is actually the best time for any meditation or spiritual activity and we all need to reclaim it. In fact, if we use the morning hours we can maximise the effect of our meditation. According to yoga texts, the 24 hours in a day are divided into three periods of eight hours each, with the predominating influence of sattva-guna , or ‘the mode of goodness,’ in the eight hours from midnight to eight in the morning. One hour and thirty-six minutes before sunrise, a period of 48 minutes begins. This is known as the Brahma Muhurta and is a ‘golden period,’ the best time to meditate. Try it and you’ll discover why!

**What’s wrong with beards or moustaches?**

Nothing. You’ll see that Krishna’s friend Arjuna had a moustache, and the great sage Vyasa had a beard. But an inordinate amount of attention on bodily looks and preening can serve to focus our attention on the externals of our life – even more than we have to. Better to spend any time we have remembering our eternal identity than clipping hairs in the moustache of our temporary body. Being overly consumed with appearance, fashion and bodily decoration can take much time out of our day. Added up, it totals weeks or even months of our life. Time is too precious to waste. When the great sages wore beards and long hair, it was because they were ignoring their hair completely, rather than making themselves attractive.

**Is astrology OK? Should I have my chart done?**

Astrology or Jyotish is a Vedic science and can help us or hinder us, depending on how we use it. It is compared to a weather forecast as it can tell us what to expect at certain key points in our life. It can also help with marriage compatibility or in choosing a business partner. If we know that rain is forecast then we can take an umbrella; and if we know that we are compatible with the personality of another then we can form a relationship with them. But too much dependence on astrology can be unhelpful in our dependency on Krishna and His personal plan for us. Astrology is a Vedanga , which means a ‘limb,’ of the Vedas; the ‘body’ of the Vedas being devotion to Vishnu. And according to our level of devotion, the Lord Himself takes charge of us, and His mercy can cause our fate to be altered according to the new direction we are travelling in.

**What’s wrong with gambling? I heard that Maharaja Yudhisthira gambled?**

King Yudhisthira did gamble, that’s true, and the result was that he lost everything. And that’s the danger of gambling: you might win, but you might lose - and you might lose a lot. That, of course, is the temptation of gambling: you have a chance of winning a fortune that you didn’t work for; and what could be a better proposal? We all work so hard for every penny in this world, and so much is immediately spent just on living. Who would not want to win a small fortune for just a small initial stake? Yet for a few people to win, many more gamblers have to lose. To imagine that you will be lucky, or that God has arranged for you alone to win, may be just that: your imagination. Gambling ruins lives just as much as alcohol does, and it is placing your imagination at the centre of your life rather than God, who will provide you with what you need.

**What about stocks and shares?**

Business itself always involves taking a risk, and that risk can be said to be a form of gambling. But there is low-risk investment and extremely high-risk speculation. The greater the level of risk the more it approaches more spiritually harmful forms of gambling. For instance, the chance of winning a national lottery might be 1 in 14 million, so that would be considered a gamble. The initial stake is lost as the gamble is lost. A bond, or a share may involve much less risk so can be considered proportionately less of a gamble. However, daily meditation on the fluctuating prices of the market may take precious time away from other, less stressful pursuits.

**Why can’t I eat meat? I heard that Rama ate meat?**

For every action there is a reaction. When we help others there’s a good reaction – we’ll enjoy at some time in the future – and when we exploit others, we’ll have to experience the same level of pain they suffered. For consumption of meat there are several stages involved, and a commensurate level of reaction for each stage. Those who kill the animal, transport it, sell it, buy it, cook it, serve to others and those who eat it, are all guilty of the killing and will have to experience the reactions to that act. Only if one is prepared to become an animal in his next life and be killed should he eat meat. Regarding Rama, the verse which explains what He ate is in the Valmiki Ramayana, Sundarakanda, Skanda 36, Sloka 41, says:

*na mamsam raghava bhunkte na chaiva madhu sevate vanyam suvihitam nityam bhaktamsnati panchamam*

**The literal translation of this verse is: “Sri Rama does not take meat or honey. He partakes every day of wild fruits and boiled (wild) rice fully sanctioned (for an ascetic) in the evening.”**

**Should I use Ayurvedic or Allopathic medicine?**

Srila Prabhupada himself was once asked this question. He replied: “Whatever works.”

**Can I keep pets?**

Devotees of Krishna are very kind to animals and we like them. But it has become a common practise now to bring animals indoors for our own pleasure. This is not always helpful for the animals, which belong in more natural surroundings. If we become emotionally involved with our pets, imagining that they experience human emotions or have become family members, that level of emotional projection is unhelpful for us. Animals living indoors can hardly keep themselves clean, and it is often observed that the owner has to become involved in cleaning their mess. If one worships Krishna inside the home a pet can be unhelpful at cooking times – or at times of meditation!

**What’s the harm in cricket, football or so-called ‘frivolous sports?’**

It depends whether you’re playing them or simply sitting and watching! One will keep you healthy and the other can get you over-excited or make you gain weight. In moderation, and for your health, any games can help you as part of your physical exercise programme. But as in all things, too much sport can dominate your life and lead to the diminishing of spiritual activity. And over-consumption of sport simply as a fan won’t help you physically or spiritually. ‘Frivolous’ means ‘characterised by a lack of seriousness or sense; self-indulgently carefree.’ Again, it all boils down to what you consider your life’s priorities.

**Can you kill mosquitoes? What about rats?**

Best if you don’t do it and especially if you don’t really need to. While in India, the bites of mosquitoes can be avoided by protecting yourself with some insect repellent, especially on exposed feet and elbows and at sunset or in dark areas. Rats arrive when there is food for them to eat, so cleanliness and placing all foods under cover are essential in dealing with them. Prevention is much better than the cure of killing. However, we do have permission to kill poisonous creatures such as snakes and scorpions if they are a threat to health or life.

**What do you do with holy pictures and sacred items or broken statues?**

They should never be thrown away as you would rubbish. Traditionally, they are respectfully placed in a flowing river. But since the materials of which they’re made may, in modern times, not be environmentally-friendly, and access to running water may be limited, a slightly better alternative is to periodically dig a deep hole and bury them.

**Why do we have cremations instead of burials?**

For some, burial is based on the conception that resurrection of the physical body will take place at the second coming of Jesus Christ. At that time all the dead bodies will be revived intact from their graves. For others, burial is chosen due to the wish to preserve the body in death as in life. In the Vedic understanding the body is a temporary vehicle for the eternal soul. Burning the body allows the soul to break its connection with all the false identities invested in that body, so that it can move on to another destination. In the UK, cremations used to be the choice for only 20% of all families, of all faiths; now it is 70%. One reason is that it is clean, relatively inexpensive and saves precious land space.

**You say that you have a yoga tradition, but I don’t see you doing any yoga.**

The word yoga actually means ‘to join,’ like the English word ‘yoke’ when one thing is harnessed with another. The traditional process of yoga involves eight stages, the third and fourth of which involve physical postures and breath control. The postures ( asanas ) and the breathing ( pranayama ) make the mind peaceful for contemplation and meditation, the next stages. You may not see the devotees of Krishna in the temple do anything more complicated than sitting cross-legged, but it’s enough for them to focus their minds on the sound of their mantra. Outside the temple there are many devotees who practise, and teach, the postures of yoga.

**Is there anything wrong with sex?**

Sex is compared to a river: it’s very useful but needs to be directed and retained by strong banks. When a river bursts its banks it becomes a flood and can no longer be used in the same way, or can even be harmful. Sex within boundaries serves a purpose, but gratuitous sex can be personally and socially harmful. That’s why every human society has some form of restriction. Krishna orders that sex be regulated by the institution of marriage and furthermore for the purposes of procreation.

**If people are supposed to be detaching themselves from all things material, why do they get married and have children? Surely that involves a certain amount of attachment?**

Although life is very short from the point of view of the philosopher, the experience for most of us is that life is indeed very long – and many challenges must be faced. Spiritual life is progressive, but that doesn’t mean that there are no ups and downs.

For the long race, it is generally advised that emotional stability is a great help. The natural attractions of the world include the desire for a life partner, and the time-tested way of creating a long-lasting relationship is through marriage. Children are the natural consequence of a marriage, and the next generation can be helped greatly when those children are trained in the art of Krishna consciousness. The world is over-populated by people who care little, if at all, for spiritual life, and we need spiritual leaders to help heal the world. The purification provided by marriage, and the children produced, can help the individuals and the world greatly.

**Why is sex within marriage supposed to be restricted? It’s not illicit so why is it not recommended for couples to have sex unless it’s for procreation?**

Sex is natural for the body – it’s one of the important functions it was designed for, and the body was designed by God. But the body and soul are different, and what is good for the body is not always good for the soul. In fact, the pleasures available to the body often serve to hinder the experience of one’s real, higher identity. And that means there is an ongoing struggle between one’s striving for self-realisation and the lower urges. As you say, sex within marriage is not illicit, but by regulating the passions we can make great strides upwards in spiritual life. It may be regarded as a restriction by some, but an embracing of freedom by others.

Of course, it is something which is meant to be voluntarily accepted, and in a relationship one’s partner has to be in agreement. But many couples have experienced a rejuvenation in their marriage when, together, they have dedicated themselves to new goals in their relationship.

**Are all the cultural items – clothing, language, food – necessary to practice Krishna consciousness?**

Krishna consciousness is based on the principle of “Simple Living and High Thinking” and the cultural aspects are an integral part of the complete lifestyle package. Yet any form of dress which is decent, any language that can be understood, and any food which is clean, pure and healthy, and can be offered to the Lord – all these can be used to practise Krishna consciousness. The dhoti, saree and so on can be produced very simply by hand-looms, thereby making such clothing easily sustainable for generations living more closely to the land. Food which is produced locally without unnecessary expense for transport is the best. Whilst devotion to Krishna is an internal adjustment that can be done by anyone, anywhere, it thrives in, and is safeguarded by, a social culture that is land-based, environmentally aware and of good morals.

**Why do you worship cows?**

We don’t worship anyone or anything apart from Krishna. But if something, or someone, is connected to Krishna, that becomes worthy of our reverence. Cows are essential to the life of humans, and we always respect them. They are considered sacred because they give us milk and the bulls plough the land from which comes grains. Once a year we give them special honour in the form of a short ceremony where they are publically offered a lamp and some jaggery, or cane-sugar lumps.

**Why all the fuss about the cow’s poo?**

We don’t fuss, but gobar , or cow dung, is great for manure, excellent for fuel when dried, and, strangely enough, is even considered to have antiseptic properties! If you keep cows, there’s an unending supply of these helpful resources. Believe it or not, cow urine is even recommended by the Ayurveda as a medicine!

**Why do you eat with your right hand?**

Everyone in the world eats either with a knife and fork, chopsticks – or with their fingers. Eating with your fingers is considered helpful for appetite and digestion because you touch the food before you put it in your mouth. Because most people are right-handed they’ll use that hand for eating. There is a consequent practise of using the left hand for unclean tasks, and this is helpful for hygiene, too.

## Holy days &amp; festivals

**There are places in the world that are sacred: holy mountains, rivers and forests. But there are holy calendar dates too. Sacred times means celebrations and festivals. This section looks at some of the questions surrounding the holy days and festivals that are spread throughout the year.**

**Do you have a holy day, like Saturday for Jews, or Sunday for Christians?**

Every day is a holy day for a devotee, but devotees around the world tend to get together weekly according to when the working week of that country allows them. So in Muslim countries, devotees gather on a Friday, in Israel they have Saturday Feasts, and in many Christian countries they have large gatherings on Sundays. Sometimes a midweek evening is chosen because the weekends are set aside for visiting relatives and for families with younger children. There is a special day every two weeks though, and on that day we do extra things to remind us of Krishna.

**What is that day called?**

That day is called Ekadasi, which means ‘The Eleventh.’ It is the eleventh day after the full moon and eleven days after the new moon. On that day the devotees do not eat any grains, beans or legumes, and try to chant extra names of Krishna, and to read more about Him. Some members even fast from all food on that day, but most honour the day by eating only foods free from wheat, corn, rice and so on.

**You seem to celebrate a lot of festivals!**

There is a lot to celebrate! In fact, every day there is an incarnation, the birthday of a saint or some other special event to celebrate. Of course, we have to choose only a limited number for our bigger public occasions, but our devotees celebrate in their own way at home with their families.

**What are your largest public festivals?**

First in the year – February-March time – is the birthday of Sri Chaitanya Mahaprabhu, the ‘golden incarnation’ of Krishna who came to teach the process of self-realisation for the current age: the congregational chanting of the names of God. Although He was Krishna, He had the colouring and compassionate mood of Krishna’s most confidential devotee, Srimati Radharani. Devotees of the Hare Krishna movement are all followers of Chaitanya. He appeared in this world at the same time as the Spring festival of Holi, the day when people throw coloured powders at each other in celebration of the new season. At this time the story of Prahlada being saved from fire by Holika is also marked by burning a bonfire.

**What’s the next festival?**

In April comes Rama-Navami. The word navami means ‘The Ninth Day’ and on this day, many thousands of years ago, Lord Rama was born in the ancient capital of the Koshala province, the city of Ayodhya. Lord Rama and His young queen, Sita, His brother Laxman and faithful monkey-servant Hanuman, are very well known throughout India. Their story has been told countless times in drama, dance and song. He appeared at midday, so devotees normally skip breakfast on that day and eat in the afternoon.

**Then comes Janmashtami?**

Correct. This is by far the biggest festival of the year, in August-September time. At Bhaktivedanta Manor it is the biggest Krishna celebration outside India. More than 60,000 pilgrims come over two days. The festival celebrates the day when Lord Krishna appeared in the world. Krishna is, of course, always within the world, but this was the time when He became visible to all, and remained in the world for more than one hundred years. Because He was born at midnight, the devotees fast all day, only eating after the celebration of the Lord’s birth. Two weeks after Janmashtami we celebrate another important festival, the birth of the Lord’s eternal consort, Srimati Radharani. This festival is also on the eighth day, or ashtami , so is known as Radha-ashtami or Radhashtami.

**If it’s a celebration, why don’t you eat anything?**

Fasting is a mark of respect to the divine person you are remembering. Eating tends to make you think of yourself! Although it’s difficult at first, the fasting actually does help you spiritually. And the time you save by not eating can be spent in some kind of spiritual activity. Fasting until midnight is perhaps the hardest fast of the year, but those who do it can testify as to its spiritual power – especially by the middle of the night!

**Do you also celebrate Diwali by fasting?**

No! That’s a day for feasting! For most Hindus, Diwali is the biggest annual festive period; a day for worship of Laxmi and for honouring the gods one regularly worships through the year. For the devotees it is the day when Lord Rama returns to Ayodhya after living in forest exile for fourteen years, doing battle with the evil king Ravana, and being reunited with Sita. The inhabitants of the city lit lamps for Him as He came home; and devotees do this in their homes to this day.

**What about Dushera?**

That is two weeks before Diwali. It is also known as Rama-Vijaya-Dashami or ‘The Victory of Rama on the Tenth Day.’ The tenth day of the moon of that month is when Rama defeated Ravana. In northern India it is known as Dushera, and on that day a huge effigy of ten-headed Ravana is burned and fireworks are set off.

**It seems unkind to keep burning an effigy of someone who died thousands of years ago. Why don’t you just drop that celebration?**

It seems to be a popular festival that no-one wants to drop. Certainly the children would complain. Besides, the English are still burning Guy Fawkes who died in 1605 – we have long memories, it seems! This particular festival highlights the ultimate destruction of evil after a long period of struggle; something that is analogous for our own struggle against our lower instincts.

**What about Raksha Bandhan?**

That is a popular custom celebrated by many in their families. We don’t specifically observe it within the temple, though. It also happens to come on the same day as the birth of Balarama, Lord Krishna’s brother. So we tend to focus on that in particular.

**Why are the same festivals celebrated on different days by different groups?**

India has different calendars in different parts of the country. Its a very big country, and in olden times, there was no way, and really no need, to harmonise the different calendars. Celebrations took place according to local custom. These days, although the business world has universally adopted the western Gregorian calendar, the calendars for religious observances still retain their regional differences. And they are lunar calendars, which means the phases of the moon are used to mark the date. One calendar marks the beginning of the month the day after the full moon, and another calculates from the ‘dark moon,’ the night when there is no moon in the sky. So in different parts of India, some dates can be two weeks apart.

The observance of specific events occur according to the calculation of a particular ‘lunar day’ known as a tithi. A tithi is the duration of time it takes for the longitudinal angle between the Moon and the Sun to increase by 12 degrees. A tithi does not have a fixed duration, and can begin at varying times of day. They range between 19 hours and 26 hours in duration. As a result of this, certain solar days do not sufficiently overlap with tithis and are therefore not suitable for fasting or observing events. In such circumstances, the relevant observation is held the following day. Since a particular tithi occurs at different times in the world, a festival held in India on Tuesday might only be celebrated elsewhere on Wednesday.

Finally, because the festival calendar is a lunar calendar, each month runs for one complete lunar cycle. That’s 28 days - no more, no less -and that means the year of 365 days contains 13 months. The western calendar has months of 28, 30 and 31 days - and sometimes 29 - and this means that the two calendars hardly ever match up. Every year, festival dates calculated according to the lunar calendar will fall on different days of the solar calendar. Dates may vary by several weeks. That’s why these movable festival dates are generally given as ‘February-March’ or ‘October-November.’

**What’s that time when everyone offers candles?**

That is the entire lunar month of Kartik. The whole month is considered a special opportunity to perform devotional activity. One common activity that everyone can participate in is the offering of lamps in the temple. Our particular focus during that month is the time when naughty child Krishna – during the Diwali period – was once tied to a wooden grinding mortar by His distraught mother, Yashoda. The vision of God Himself consenting to be restricted by His own devotee, out of His love, is said to be one of the most inspirational aspects of the affection between God and His devotees. So we sing about that and offer lamps to the Lord in that form. In that particular form, the Lord is known as Damodara, and so devotees also use the expression, ‘the month of Damodara,’ to refer to the Kartik month.

**Are there any other festivals?**

There are many of course, as I’ve said, but those are the large public ones. There’s one more. Every year we stage a large festival in central London where we pull three large wooden chariots bearing the temple Deities of Krishna, Balarama, and Their sister Subhadra from Hyde Park down Piccadilly to Trafalgar Square. It’s a very colourful sight and thousands of people come to it. It’s called the London Rathayatra. It marks the journey of Lord Krishna from Dwaraka to Kurukshetra, where He was reunited with His beloved friends from Vrindavan.

**Do you celebrate Christmas or any other religious festivals?**

Of course, Christmas, Hanukkah, Kwanzaa, Saint Lucy’s Day and so on are winter festivals honoured by members of their respective communities, and there are so many other religious and cultural festivals celebrated throughout the year by people of different faiths and countries. We don’t particularly celebrate Christmas at the temple, but no uplifting or religious festival is ever condemned. We all need to come together with our families and communities, and it may be more appropriate to do this at the most popular times. Our devotees come from many different communities, and we leave it up to them to choose which additional festivals or events they celebrate.

**What is a Yatra?**

The word means ‘movement’ and is most commonly used to signify a religious pilgrimage, particularly to India. It’s not a festival, but a sacred journey to live for some time in those holy places where Lord Krishna or Caitanya Mahaprabhu actually descended into this world, or where they themselves travelled, or performed some heroic deed. So devotees visit holy places like Vrindavan in Uttar Pradesh, Dwaraka in Gujarat, Jagannatha Puri in Odisha, and Mayapur in West Bengal.

Holy places in southern India are also popular. Devotees typically go to these places for 23 weeks and take advantage of a knowledgeable guide to show them around. There is also the more rugged option of pada-yatra , which means ‘movement on foot,’ and refers to a holy walk. One popular such walk is the 128-mile, month-long barefoot pilgrimage around the land of Vraja where child Krishna performed His heroic deeds. Because it is a circular walk for religious purposes it is also known as a parikrama .

**What is a Marathon?**

Well a marathon is a long race, as you know, and it’s the name given to the annual winter book distribution effort that lasts the entire month of December. Srila Prabhupada, the founder and spiritual master of the Hare Krishna movement, translated and wrote commentaries on many scriptures, and he asked that those books be distributed by his followers. So not only do we do that all year round, we make special efforts during the ‘marathon.’ The result is that more than 80,000 books are sold in that month – something that any publishing house could be proud of.

## Guru &amp; disciple

*One of the most important steps one takes in their path of devotion is that of accepting a spiritual master, or ‘guru’. It is said that accepting a guru means you have made a commitment to spiritual practice and are surrendered to accepting instructions from someone who you consider is a representative of Krishna. You don’t have to be around the devotee community very long before you hear the word guru. And not long after that before someone asks you: “Are you initiated?” There’s an entire study course for the subject of guru and disciple, but here are a few of the most common questions – just to get you started.*

**What is a guru?**

A guru is a teacher. Anyone who knows a subject and can teach it can be a guru. In India there are gurus of music, pottery, archery, medicine and every other skill or department of knowledge. However, the word is most commonly used to indicate a person who teaches the most important form of knowledge: the science of self-realisation. The Upanishads state that such a person will know the Veda, practise the teachings of the Veda in life, and be ‘firmly situated in transcendence.’ A guru is also someone who has the compassion, ability and desire to teach others, someone who has also been a faithful student of a guru, and someone whose teachings can be demonstrated to have brought about inspiration and transformation in the lives of others.

**How do I know who is a guru?**

Lord Krishna told His friend Arjuna to approach a guru, and He also told him the qualities of such a person. Thirty-six years later, when Krishna was about to leave the world, His friend Uddhava also asked Him about finding a guru. Krishna advised him to find a sadhu , a ‘good person,’ and He gave him a list of twenty-eight qualities to look for in such a person. But the most important quality, said Krishna, was that the sadhu should be ‘surrendered to me.’ (Srimad Bhagavatam, 11th canto, Uddhava-gita ).

Arjuna in the Bhagavad-gita asks Krishna twice about the qualifications of a truly selfrealised person and the Lord told him that such a person gives up all selfish desires (2.55) and lives free from yearning, fear, passion and anger (2.56). If he has knowledge he retracts his limbs from sense objects just like a tortoise (2.58), and he has genuine experience of something higher (2.59). Krishna also gives an extensive list of characteristics of someone situated in spiritual knowledge (13.8-12). The second time Arjuna asks, Krishna replies that a liberated soul is free from dualities such as joy and sorrow, praise and blame, honour and dishonour (14.22-25), and that one who serves the Lord with bhakti attains the Absolute (14.26). He also describes the divine qualities that become manifest in a spiritually advanced person (16.1-3). Finally, the Lord asks Arjuna to abandon all other dharma and to surrender to Him alone (18.66) indicating that surrender to Him is the sign of the fully liberated soul.

**What are the steps to finding a guru?**

The first step is to study the life and teachings of Srila Prabhupada to learn how a selfrealised soul behaves and speaks; what he teaches and how he guides. He is our example of a world- acarya and all other devotees of Krishna, the sadhus , will need to be compared to him.

The second step is to write a list of the specific qualities of a sadhu, and then you’ll know what characteristics you’re looking for. The third step is to find a sadhu who inspires you, who is able to encourage you, and can discuss your progress with you. Fourth is to check that the sadhu comes in an authorised lineage and that he was a faithful disciple of his guru. The fifth step is to confirm if he is actually accepting any disciples. The sixth step is to get ready for becoming a disciple.

**Can women be gurus?**

Since a sadhu is a person whose spiritual qualities shine through, and a guru is a sadhu who consents to teach others as disciples, the role of a guru is purely spiritual and neither male nor female. Both men and women have served in this capacity throughout history. However, the roles of guru and disciple also fit within the varnashram social system, so there may previously have been other social considerations to be taken into consideration.

For instance, a male disciple was customarily given a sacred thread by the guru along with mantras at the upanayanam initiation.

If the female sadhu had neither mantras nor thread she could not become that type of guru, since guru means to give the mantras that you have been given by your guru. Similarly, a woman would not act as a guru if she was young and the prospective male disciples were young. Such a relationship would not be acceptable in former times. But acting as a spiritual guide and teacher of sacred scripture, and acting as an exemplar of the tradition, is the most important feature of a guru.

**What is initiation?**

Initiation means the beginning of something, and in bhakti-yoga the word is used to indicate the formal beginning of the relationship between the guru and disciple. The Sanskrit word diksha is also commonly used. At the time of initiation the disciple makes formal vows to recite his mantra a certain number of times daily, to follow any rules and regulations the guru gives him, to study the Vedas and to serve Krishna according to his ability. In return the guru gives the disciple a new name, usually a name of God or a great saint. The name ends in the word das or dasi meaning ‘servant.’

**What does a disciple have to do?**

A disciple is a student of a guru. He learns the scriptures from him, follows his example and moulds his life according to the guidance of the guru. A disciple makes a commitment to follow the disciplines of the spiritual path, to be determined in the face of life’s challenges, and to assist the guru in his service to his own spiritual master.

**How can you revere human beings as gurus?**

Gurus are revered because they give us the most powerful thing: knowledge. The Vedas are the best form of knowledge because understanding them can put an end to the problems of life, and a guru is one who has learned the Vedas, lives the Vedas, experiences their spiritual benefit and teaches them to others. Krishna is the original and ultimate guru who dwells within our hearts, but we often have difficulty listening to Him, so He kindly appears before our physical senses as a human representative. The guru is therefore not God, but the dear servant and messenger of God. As such he is deserving of our reverence, respect, gratitude and service.

**What offerings or gifts should be given to a guru?**

That depends upon whether he is your personal guru, or the guru of someone else! Traditional gifts are given to one’s own guru on completion of your education from him, or a tributary offering is made each year towards his maintenance. That may include grains, ghee, shoes, clothing, or some other contribution towards living costs. The idea is to make a voluntary contribution – the guru is not supposed to ask for a specific amount!
