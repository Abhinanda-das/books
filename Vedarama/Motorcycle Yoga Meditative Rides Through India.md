## Preface to the Second Edition

Once upon a time old school riders threw a leg over the saddle of a British single or twin, or a German boxer, or even a Sportster in a spirit of throttling away from the complexities of life. Way back then motor biking was a divine experience created by the mercy of God Almighty to give the best and smartest of mankind a chance to leave behind much of that which motorcycling has become today. Old school riders rightly wonder why if there is so much progress in the design and development of motorcycles, then has why hasn’t the loner's spirit of the sport improved. For the motorcycle *yogi* the soul of the ride is the same as it ever was.

Before bikes became overdressed, was there any question of a naked bike? As far as sport biking is concerned, should not a sport be defined as a rider’s decision to throttle according to his own particular love of the two wheels he straddles? When it comes to cruisers and cruising, then why not just back off the throttle a bit? And what is the point of a streetfighter when you are out on some quiet road all by yourself? Once upon a time there was an all-purpose kick-started motorcycle piloted by riders who understood what all those purposes were. There still is. Today it is built in India, which is also the best place to ride it.

With those words I extend a welcome to my fellow riders and armchair travelers alike to the second edition of *Motorcycle Yoga*, “Meditative Rides through India.” I could not have hoped for a more worthy publisher than Enfield Books since the man at the handlebars Gordon May appreciates Enfield adventure touring more than anyone else I know. Or else why would he have ridden solo from England all the way to Chennai in South India just to visit the transplanted factory of the esteemed British marque? If East is to meet west, then probably the best way to “go troppo” is on a Royal Enfield. To all wayfarers seeking the bliss of *samadhi* on the back roads of India---or the back roads of your mind--thank you for joining me on these solitary rides through Central India.

--Miles Davis

## Introduction - Moto-Biography of a Yogi

SOMEHOW ROTATING FEELINGS OF LIFE'S ENDLESS CYCLES find themselves heightened while grasping the motorcycle's chrome handlebars, a phenomenon that riders understand. Though we mortals may try to transcend the dualities of love and hate, attraction and repulsion, these feelings get magnified further still upon the dangerous yet blissful roadways of India. As such I have tried to treat the good, the bad and the filthy with candid honesty, for only a mad man or a fool could love everything about any place. Yet, it is true that in my thirty-five years of visiting India, I have learned to love that country, and true love provides no room for diplomacy.

And if you are a rider who loves the gentle loneliness of the warm breeze, the soft Moonlight shining amidst bright stars in a place far from city lights, the solace of the self, the excitement of the risk, and the search for Truth, then you, too, are a motorcycle *yogi*. There remain places upon Mother Earth, as well as in the mind and soul, which are relatively unexplored. A twist of the throttle takes you there.

The following pages carry descriptions of several of my motorcycle rides into India's whirlpool of humanity. All that has been written is true, but for literary purposes, sometimes two or more rides have been combined into one. India is a wonderful land of meditation and philosophy; therefore I have shared my wheel-inspired revelations which culminate in *bhakti*, devotion to the Supreme Absolute Truth in His most personal feature.

Day of the Full Moon, 16 Feb 2003 (Magha Purnima, 2059)

## 1. Riding Bombay - 1981

IT WAS MY FIFTH YEAR IN INDIA AND I HAD TRAVELED most of it by way of the trains and buses of the sub-continent. However, in 1981 I came by my first chance to ride a motorcycle there. My friend Avatar Singh, son of a Bombay businessman, asked me if I'd like to ride his old Royal Enfield along the frothy, water-packed sands of Juhu Beach. Riding a bike for the first time in about a decade, finding once again the balance that comes with motorcyc1ing, made for a perfect day on the ocean's edge. Noting my delight, my bearded Sikh friend made me an offer that I could not turn down. Avatar suggested that we take an all-day ride throughout Bombay. He would borrow his friend's Enfield and I would ride his. This would be no small task, for there was hardly a commotion on Earth that could equal Bombay traffic.

Yet the chance to wheel through Asia's premier kaleidoscope port city was something I could not pass up. "Don't worry, Pavanji," Avatar told me calming my trepidation, "I will take the lead and you will stay out of trouble if you just tag along."

It is said in India that the taste of water changes every twenty miles and the "taste" in the dialect changes every forty. The rail traveler who boards a train in the Punjab and gets down the next day in Madras actually sleeps through several different sub-countries, old kingdoms, linguistic regions and a vastly changing geography. Riding that day with Avatar Singh, I would discover that a hundred miles by motorcycle was superior to a thousand miles by train.

Juhu, once a village where fishermen of mixed Portuguese and Hindu descent cast their nets, had by the 1970's grown into a prosperous suburb of beachfront hotels and Bollywood bungalows. Bombay too had once been a Portuguese colony, ceded by the Sultan of Gujarat in 1534. It fell into British hands as dowry in 1661 when King Charles II married Catherine of Braganza. History includes references to Bombay as Heptonesia ("seven islands") by Ptolemy, visits by Marco Polo (1298), and Vasco da Gama (1498) repeated raids by pirates like Captain Kidd in the early 1800's, and attempts by both the Dutch and French to colonize it. By the time Britain succumbed to the Quit India Movement in 1947, Bombay had become a hopelessly congested magnet for the futile dreams of millions of poor villagers.

We discussed various routes. Finally I agreed to just follow Avatar by splitting lanes with him south through the towns of Vile Parle, Santa Cruz and Bandra and into downtown Bombay. There we would pass the giant statue of Shivaji, the 18th century King of the Mahrattas, then follow Marine Drive into the bowels of the teeming metropolis. On the way we would see the cathedral-like, British-built railway stations of Victoria Terminus and Church Gate while riding through a hundred pages of history. We started out the next day, wheeling through and around every sort of traffic snarl for an hour and a half until we came to the heart of the city. There we slowed down unnoticed like invaders exulting in our own imagined victory. We circled the block outside Crawford Market, a giant indoor bazaar the interior of which was designed by Rudyard Kipling's artist father John.

Here, where urban chaos reached its zenith, turmoil was redefined. *Brahmanas* dressed in spotlessly clean white robes were rushing past the innumerable vendors and roadside filth on their way to worship Mumba Devi, the goddess who lent her name to the city. In the narrow *gullis*, Mussalman tailors, bookbinders and fragrant oil merchants--their mustaches shaved above long black beards--plied their trades. Maharashtrian deliverymen were carrying steel tins of home-cooked meals to South Indian *brahmana* accountants whose wives could never think of their husbands eating at some restaurant. Olive-skinned Parsis in simple Western dress, followers of Zarasthustra of Old Persia, were greeting each other with a nodded "Sahebji." Short and pudgy Gujarati gold merchants, balding heads neatly oiled, passed by in tightly packed rickshaws seated next to their smug wives dressed in saris immaculately starched. The rickshaw pullers, poor men from up north in Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, were bearing the burden of crushing poverty underneath another man's luxury. Yet neither had chosen to argue with the hand dealt by Fate. If there is one thing that all Indians have common, it is an understanding of the laws of *karma*. Neither did I lament for one, nor condemn the other. The Laws of Nature are inescapably stringent and harsh, yet just. In this great samsaric go-round, he who receives must repay that which he owes either in this life or the next.

The season of *hemant* or winter only served to make Bombay's tropical weather pleasant. Nepali Hindus with brown faces reflecting an Oriental mien were selling hand-knit woolen sweaters on the sidewalk, hoping for a cold snap. Descendants of Chinese cobblers were hurrying to their chop suey *dhabas*. Dark-skinned villagers from Goa with Portuguese surnames like DeMello or DeSouza were rubbernecking like any visitor who has come to see a big city for his first time.

Crazy-glued in the endless traffic jam like everyone else, Marwari ladies--wives of wealthy merchants and industrialists--sat in chauffeur-driven Ambassador sedans gossiping about forthcoming marital alliances. They had stepped out of their Malabar Hill mansions to shop for things none of them could possibly need. On sidewalks and upon carts all about us, vendors were offering for sale, at ridiculously low prices, items few Westerners ever knew existed. Young monks in saffron, the color worn by renunciates symbolizing dead to the world, were trying to discuss the saga of the *Gita* with complacent shopkeepers who tried to get rid of them by offering the smallest possible donation for their *ashram*. Jaded hippie tourists from America and Europe, playing *yogi* for a few weeks on the Goa-Bombay-Kathmandu trail, tried to fit in with an aloof crowd that preferred they proceed on their way to Thailand.

Long-robed Arabs, mere taxi drivers back home in the Gulf States, stood about importantly posturing as sheiks, and enjoying the power that their oil-rich currency would yield against the rupee. Gaping Buddhist tourists from Japan were there, too, to see and experience all aspects of a country where the founder of their religion had walked and taught two and a half millennia earlier. As a Westerner I was dumbfounded by seeing the surprise shown even by other Asians at Bombay's measured disorder. Throttling towards the waterfront docks, we noticed the occasional Russian and Greek sailor darting in and out of shops making a few last minute barters before shipping back out to sea.

We turned a corner just in time to be greeted by a somber crowd chanting "*Ram nam satya hai*!" ... which translates: "Truth and goodness are found in the holy name of Rama!" Like marching soldiers on a mission, the crowd hurried past our bikes, now respectfully stilled, carrying a dead body wrapped in a white sheet, decorated in marigolds, and tied to a bamboo stretcher. Suddenly I found myself reflecting upon my late Sindhi friend Mr. Mulchand, who in the days before partition had been a policeman in Karachi. Although Mulchandji was past ninety, we had become close as he had moved in with us at the Hare Krishna *ashram* at Juhu. One night Mulchand sent a message asking to see me. I sent back word that I would visit with him the following morning. This turned out to be Mr. Mulchand's last night on Earth. The next day I kept my word and I was one of those whose shoulder bore his remains to the burning ground chanting "*Ram nama satya hai*!" Old Mulchand and I were good friends, and as it is said, ''A true friend follows his friend all the way to *smashana* (the burning *ghat*)."

As Avatar and I circled towards the downtown residential areas, we found ourselves in the midst of a marriage procession complete with a brass band. The bridegroom, looking like a prince seated upon a white horse, was on his way to claim the gold-adorned hand of his partner and to have his own wrists manacled in iron. A few minutes later near the Gateway of India monument, we encountered an on-location film shoot. Perhaps no other scenario in India could have equaled the absurdity of this Bollywood moment. The beggars had all been marshaled to one side out of the camera's eye. The painted heroine was gyrating upon an ancient flight of chiseled stone steps leading into a stone building. She was screw-dancing and lip-synching some blaring, screeching future hit song--and destroying a million years of traditional art in the process. Next, when the film would be released with much fanfare and ballyhoo in a month or two, the dream-state audience would be wooed into believing for two hours that poor people like themselves ceased to exist.

Absurd, yet compelling, as things appeared that December day, bald-faced contradictions continued to beckon like the mesmerizing wheel of life and death itself. I saw in that brown sea of humanity:

...the goddess-like beauty of young virgins laughing socially together while stepping over the distorted ugliness of poverty,  
…the vast wealth of the privileged contrasting the ability of the poor to subsist on nothing,  
…the intoxicatingly sweet fragrance of night-blooming jasmine bushes wafting over horrible piles of refuse,  
...the wisdom of sages and the blind ignorance of the faceless masses,  
…the emerging middle class's eagerness to ape the West and the Old Guard’s contempt of it,  
…the ability of some to do nothing and yet be served by those who spend their entire lives in service and receiving little,  
…the golden-toned Konkan *brahmana* passing by a black-skinned tribal without so much as a glance at one another,  
…the poor old lady whose job it was to pamper a rich dog,  
…the power of human endurance and dignity which must stand up to a tidal wave of insurmountable odds,  
…and the whispers of past empires whose footprints, though wearing away, are not entirely blown over by the sands of time.

It seemed as though elements of all the three worlds and fourteen levels of the Universe were reflected there on the streets and alleyways of Bombay. For all that can be seen over time in India can be seen in a day's motorcycle ride through Bombay. At once comical, tragic, awe-inspiring and ridiculous, this show of generation, growth and dissolution that played before our eyes was running before we had arrived and doubtless continued to play after we would turn our wheels to leave. Yet the seemingly impenetrable curtain of India had lifted for a day in celebration of my first ride there. I had seen people at opposing ends of the spectrum passing one another separated only by inches, yet divided by a crevice as broad as a canyon. People from different eras had shuffled in and out of scenes as though emerging from a time machine or contrasting worlds. In this city of extremes--in caste, in education, in status--which revolves like some self-propelled velocipede of life, even the seasoned wanderer grows bleary-eyed.

Back in America my compatriots would be frittering away their days like some chosen few sampling their own private garden of delights. That is the way of the *bhoga-bhumi*, or "land of enjoyment," where the fruits produced of a past life’s forgotten good deeds are squandered like customers at a bank heedlessly withdrawing their money, without making fresh deposits. If America is the *bhoga bhumi*, then India is the *karma-bhumi*, where the concomitant reactions to activities seem to make a more immediate stamp. The pious Hindu would rather suffer the mishaps of his misdeeds and mistakes in the here and now than be burdened with his heavy load in the hereafter. And here in Bombay, like no place else, will the soul swirling in the current of *samsara* be carried aloft by the incoming waves, or be drowned in the outgoing riptide.

Just as theatergoers watching a film flickering upon the screen are helpless to alter the plot, so the theater of Bombay had stimulated philosophical reflection. From a distance, separated by an invisible barricade, my unshared thoughts were mine alone. The rupee coin I dropped into the cup of the leprous beggar chanting "Hey Babaji!," to get him out of my face at a red light, had only served to insure that he would remain a beggar for another day. No amount of money can refurbish reactions to past sins, which are waiting to sprout like seedlings in spring. Only the wealth of devotion to the Supreme Personality of Godhead grants freedom from *karma*.

I had seen an endless show like some observant wayfarer just passing through. My rulebook had been left Stateside, for none of those restrictions would have applied in this world anyway. Realizing that my every twist of the throttle was as precarious as riding on an oil slick, I surrendered to the guidance of the Director of the stage, the Soul of all souls who has always watched each of us from within and without. In His hands alone are found the power to control the external and internal energies that harmonize all multifarious Universal forces. He alone can protect us from the reactions to our past errors and the ultimate ride terminates at His lotus feet.

The secret to seeing the Supremely Personal Force at work in all this passing parade is described in the ultimate text of *yoga*, the *Bhagavad Gita As It Is* 5.18. Therein Krishna advises *panditah sama-darshanah*: "He who is wise views all things with equal vision." He who knows this secret *darshan* has attained equanimity, for he perceives that the different types of bodies produced through the intertwining modes of Nature, or external energy, are the culmination of the past deeds of the doer. These modes of Nature, or *gunas*, are threefold--goodness, passion and ignorance. They alone are responsible for every type of body born of a mother, from a worm to a king. From the *indragopa* germ, the most insignificant living entity who lives less than a second, to Lord Indra the king of *swarga* (material heaven) who lives for millions of years, all are conditioned by repeated birth and death. Yet each gross physical body is animated by an individual spiritual particle each of which is a part and parcel of the Supreme Spirit Whole. The ride, the vision, the introspection: all had combined to make an incredible day of motorbiking.

Avatar Singh had apparently enjoyed himself too, going round from one bazaar to another. We had both forgotten to eat all day. As Lord Surya, the demi-god of the Sun, silently folded himself back into the Arabian Sea, Avatar motioned to the *chat* and *bhel puri* stands along Chowpatty Beach. We put the Enfields on their kickstands beside the curb and I walked alone over to the water's edge. Leaning into the direction of the setting Sun, I scooped up a handful of sea water to sprinkle over my head in a silent ritual of self-baptism. I had become a rider in India on that day, and felt reborn.

## 2. Kipling's India

## "A Bubble Burst in Time"

NO WRITER, NO EXPLORER, NO ADVENTURER has ever enticed people on one side of the world to discover a culture on the other side like England's most esteemed poet laureate Rudyard Kipling. And no other work of literature has excited the world's imagination like Rudyard's *Jungle Books*. Woodsy tales of Balou, Sher Khan and Kaa have woven themselves into the fabric of western culture - in the form of post cards, comic books, cartoons, and full length Hollywood productions.

A half century ago at my bedside my mother read to me Kipling's masterpieces of India. How my childhood imagination would soar! I wanted to run and play with Mowgli, the mancub; Balou, the bear; or Bagheera, the black panther. Kipling, with the eye of a sage, the voice of a master and the heart of a child, has done more than simply achieve an exalted pedestal in the world of literature. He has taught four generations about a secret India hidden deep within the jungles.

Kipling began the *Jungle Book* with the words, "It was seven o'clock of a very warm evening in the Seeonee Hills when Father Wolf woke up from his day's rest, scratched himself, yawned and stretched out his paws..."

While perusing a map of Central India on a train in 1981, in the maze of railroad tracks, roads and villages, I spotted a small town to the south called Seoni. Seoni! Something jogged an ancient memory long dormant. Could today's Seoni be the Seeonee of the Jungle Books? I showed the map to my fellow passengers, each of whom knew of Seoni. I learned that Seeonee of Kipling's Jungle Books is today the prosperous little town of Seoni in the southern part of India’s Madhya Pradesh state. The town had taken its name from an ancient local temple of Goddess Shivani, the wife of Lord Shiva--to whom Bombay-born Kipling would also write poetry. Over the years through local dialect and English influence, the name had gradually changed from Shivani to Seeonee to Seoni.

Later I would learn that a 750 square mile area of jungle near Seoni has been declared a natural preserve called Kanha. In the early seventies, concern over encroachment had led the Indian Government under Indira Gandhi to create "Operation Tiger" for the preservation of the diminishing jungle. Kipling's India was alive and well!

Some time after this incident I developed a desire to undertake a two-wheel tour of Seoni and the Kanha Jungle Preserve. At the time I was in Jabalpur, two hundred miles north of Seoni. My lone cycle tour would circle over half a thousand miles of central India's rolling countryside. This area being the most inland from India's two coasts was therefore the last to be explored by European colonialists. Indeed, even now in the 21stCentury, this vast and diverse province still harbors jungles from which even Indians are prohibited entry due to the wild and deadly nature of the primitive *adi-vasi* jungle dwellers, followers their own laws.

The scenic roadways of middle India offer the adventurer a potpourri of towns, villages, jungles and rural pastures. Madhya Pradesh is a peaceful place whose natural charm is as enticing as any in the world. There are no wars, insurgencies, or looting gangs of brigands that can be found in the country's disturbed border areas; places where self-interested neighbors have been agitating ethnic groups on the other side of the frontiers into thoughtless and violent quests for unguided regional autonomy. Far from conflict, this is Mother India as she always was; her gentle pace of life vibrating warmly since time immemorial. Here you will encounter no strangers--everyone you meet along the way is an instant friend. No two-wheeling adventurer who breathes central India’s soft rural air, and who reciprocates the warm greetings of villagers, can avoid the transformation into a more timeless and transcendental mode. This is life as it was always meant to be.

After Siddiqui, my mechanic, polished and tuned up the Enfield, I asked Panditji, the astrologer, for a beneficial time to begin the trip. I needed a *muhurta*, an auspicious moment of good stars under which to depart the bike journey. "It had better be soon," I told him. The three-month season of the rains would soon begin. Kanha would be closing its green doors at the start of July's monsoonal onslaught, leaving the jungle's wildlife free to roam, safe from the prying eyes of man. Panditji hurriedly examined his *panchang* stellar atlas and replied, "You may leave tomorrow when the Moon conjoins the auspicious Rohini star in Taurus." Offering him a few rupees, I hurried back to pack the Enfield's saddlebags for the trip.

A steady ride to Kanha Preserve via Seoni would take six to seven hours. This would be no Paris to Dakar run; the slow timeless pace of India demands the rider’s obliging participation. Riding in India is a mystic and pleasurably intoxicating slow drift into another world. Neither can this land be gulped down all at once. It is no exaggeration to say that the motorcycle offers the third world adventurer the best means to see a country whose colorful people, tropical climate, spectacular views, wildlife, and gentle rhythm of life bombards the rider's senses all at once. Yet the lullaby of a country ride in India can mask the fact that there are no roads on Earth more dangerous. During a recent two hundred-mile trip, I counted five fatal accidents along the way. Roads here are like the trees of the sandalwood forest: beautiful and fragrant, yet full of cobras.

At seven the next morning, I turn south onto National Hwy 7. Under the bright morning Sun rising from behind the Vindhya Hills, activity is seen everywhere. Dairymen on two-stroke 175cc Rajdoots are putt-putting about delivering scoopfuls of milk from aluminum urns mounted fore and aft their bikes. A few truckers in their loud and unsightly behemoths are carelessly and dangerously careening all over the road with scant regard for the defenseless two-wheelers down below. Laughing students jam packed into rickshaws and onto bicycles head for school--each in his clean but faded uniform--are unmindful of the traffic and danger all about them.

I ride out past the Jabalpur High Court and the Jaya Stambha, or "Victory Pillar" in its courtyard, erected to commemorate India's independence from the British Raj in 1947, the year of my birth. Some claim this pillar marks this vast country's dead-center point, though recent geographers have placed the spot miles north of here. The High Court was built by the British to resemble a Gothic cathedral. In the yard are barristers, dressed in the black jackets and white shirts of the British system that they are still taught to emulate, stand about penguin-like like discussing the day's cases they will present before the bench. Others are just pulling up on their Bajaj scooters, India's version of the Vespa. Barristers do not ride Enfields, which are preferred by the military and the police: Bullets are perceived as too macho for lawyers. One Bajaj Scooter has been fitted with an extra set of twelve-inch wheels, an invention for a disabled man and an innovation that might be tried with success in the West.

Riding through Sadar Bazaar, I pass the dilapidated, abandoned bungalows of long-gone English officers. Legendary British regiments like the Bengal Lancers or Grenadiers once went through their exercises nearby at the Jabalpur cantonement. On the edge of the city I spot the old British cemetery where I have sometimes wandered just to ponder the fate of an evaporated empire that had sprung up suddenly only to burst like a fragile bubble in history.

I find myself magnetically drawn through the gates to the land of the dead like one who was buried here in some past life. The *chandala*, a Christian guard in charge of the yard, pleasantly greets me with the advice, "The rains have caused the grasses to grow tall hiding the older graves towards the rear fence. The cows and goats will soon be brought to trim them. For now, there are cobras hiding there." His smile turns sly as he adds, "Should you enter that part of the yard, Sahib, you may not be able to leave."

Why have I stopped here anyway? It was Kipling who coined the expression "white man's burden." Many do not realize that his famous words were more a jab at colonialism than a nod of racial assent. And now, while India limps ahead into the 21stcentury, that burden with all its colonialist hoopla has come to this, a forgotten cemetery. Kipling called Victoria, the absentee Queen-in-mourning, "the Widow at Windsor," an expression that "did not amuse her Majesty." Kipling, too, wondered, "We don't know why we are here." But today I will walk amongst the crumbling gravestones of soldiers, wives and infants who died without knowing what brought them. I once asked a sage why the British came and why they left, and he answered with one word, “*Karma*.” It was their *karma* that brought them and the *karma* of Indians to have them. And *karma* at last finally took them away for *karma* collectively affects nations as it does individuals.A few years ago, while walking upon trimmed grasses atop these fossils of a lost empire, I sat down and penned the following poem:

Today the rays of the life-giving Sun ascending gloriously on my left dance upon the headstones of ancient gravesites as I wend my wheels away from the past and head out towards the inescapable living beauty of central India's countryside. About four hours out, a little before noon, wisps of bluish-blackish clouds begin to gather hovering over rich fields. The scent in the wind tells me that soon I will witness the handiwork of Indra, the god of weather. It looks as though this year the monsoon will start on time. I make a quick guess that it will be more expedient to try to outrun the gathering storm than to take shelter in some abandoned shack and risk getting caught in the middle of nowhere in case the rains last till nightfall.

As the three-month monsoon season progresses, rainfall will become more severe as storms intensify. Racing over a windy bluff I reflect back to a July bike trip a few years earlier. Out in the middle of nowhere, an angry storm suddenly churned up all around me. Giant black clouds had gathered with such profound force and ferocity, I felt they were singularly seeking me out. As I was riding next to a mountain, the storm--appearing as vast as an ocean--stalled and seemed to sit on my shoulder. Huge pellets of water shot through my bike and me from every direction at once. No shelter could be found anywhere, under neither tree nor bush. I was drenched and had to pour quarts of water from my boots every few minutes as clouds disintegrated against hillsides. As rushing waters rose above the ditches, I soon had to battle my way up to high ground to escape a flood's torrential flow.

On other occasions, a more gentle cloud would titillate and refresh me with the lightest spray of water. Within a few minutes the Sun would reappear in full glory and without even slowing down the bike, the mist would bathe me and even launder my clothes while the joyful Indian Sun would dry me. Today, by good fortune and a shift in the wind, this rainfall never reaches me as I watch the clouds dwindle in the distance. By evening time I have passed through villages, towns, forested hills, and fertile countryside; and have felt the timeless beat of central India’s heart all along the way. As I arrive at the outskirts of the Kanha jungle via Seoni, the sky is the color of a Ceylonese sapphire. The Sun as it sets in the West is followed by a sliver of a newly waxing Moon conjoined Saturn and Jupiter. I had booked reservations in a log hut two miles inside the park. Since two or four wheeled private vehicles are allowed inside Kanha, tomorrow I will hire an elephant. At nightfall begins the wicked chorus of laughter from a blood thirsty gang of hyenas, so despised by the other animals, making me glad the iron bars on the windows.

The next day I join a group of four or five others, all Indian tourists. Our elephants are Kamala, a female named for the lotus of Goddess Lakshmi, and Badal, a male whose name means "cloud." In Kanha the mahouts have a rule that no procession will venture into the jungle without a male lead elephant, for it is the male that the tigers fear. Further, the elephants are not allowed to follow those tigers that have not recently feasted, as evidenced by a nearby carcass of some unfortunate deer. Not long ago, quite by accident, the son of a mahout fell from the wooden *palki* atop the elephant. Before he even hit the ground, he was locked within the strong jaws of a tiger. Sometime later, whatever little remained of the boy was found in the jungle.

Kanha's elephants and their *mahouts* know every tiger, every Sher Khan, within the jungle and daily stroll fearlessly amongst them. We will be safe too, no matter how close we get to the tigers, as long as we stay on top of the elephant. To walk or ride a motorcycle alone in these woods would be suicide. The four-hour ride atop the elephant brings me very dose to many forms of wildlife: tigers, laughing hyenas, families of wild boar trotting in procession, a few black bears, snorting Indian buffaloes, langur monkeys in every tree and many unusual species of deer. Here and there huge jungle ants have constructed hill like mud hives, some over four feet high.

Our elephants carry us into a deep dry gully where, upon the gully’s bank we observe a tiger at eye level. Not six feet away the tiger yawns, stretches out in the Sun and rolls over playfully. Then Sher Khan shows me his fangs before reluctantly abandoning the gully and moving on. Kamala and Badal appear to smile, overjoyed at putting a tiger to slow chase. No doubt they have done this hundreds of times.

That night in the hut, sleep is profound amidst the hypnotic jungle chorus. The next morning I decide to return to Jabalpur on another route traveling via the holy town of Mandla. Turning onto the Mandla Road, I spot a group of men staring at some object lying by the wayside. Curious, I pull over to see what has attracted so much attention. Leaving the Enfield on its sidestand, I gently elbow my way to the front of the murmuring crowd. To my surprise, this gathering of rustic gentlemen, farmers and laborers is standing at a respectable distance from ... an ordinary truck tire! As I lean over to see what has fascinated them about a rubber tire, a hand grabs my arm and pulls me back. From the tire's center rim the shiny head of a black cobra emerges gingerly at first, but turning menacing very quickly. As the cobra expands its hood, the crowd takes a long step backward in unison. The *naag* quickly rotates 180 degrees catching sight of every eye in the crowd, leaving no doubts about picking up the abandoned tire.

From down the road a truck slowly approaches, then stops. As the driver exits his vehicle to join the onlookers, it is apparent from his excited Hindi that this is his tire. It had fallen off the rear of his flat bed truck just minutes earlier. He had swerved to avoid running over this very cobra slithering across the road. To everyone's amazement, the cobra, upon spotting the truck and its driver, deflates his hood and silently slipped into the woods, like a sentry whose shift at guard duty has just ended. A dignified grey-haired man in the crowd, perhaps a village teacher, turns to me and in good English says, "He will find his own way," leaving me to ponder about the way ahead that lies before all creatures.

It is no wonder that living here in India, Kipling could write about the creatures of the jungle, distinguishing each according to its own personality. Many will recall from the *Jungle Book* the treacherous yet wise old cobra named Kaa. It is said that Kipling coined the name from *kaala-naag*, Hindi for "black snake." Kipling wrote, "Kaa was everything that the monkeys feared in the Jungle, for none of them knew what the limits of his power were, none of them could look him in the face, and none had ever come away alive from his hug." Yet, as I have witnessed on this day, even Kaa must have a sense of justice.

Here in the magic India of Kipling, sometimes even the Sun, stars and Moon gazing down upon us appear to be like living, breathing watchers over the natural world below, a world where participants interact socially in friendship and enmity as do you and I. There is life in all corners of the Universe, and all the millions of interactions in the course of a day or a lifetime are carried out under the watchful eye of the Supreme Lord. From time to time we are afforded a glimpse.

The crowd disperses silently, each man going his own way as the trucker retrieves his tire by Kaa's will and continues on his journey. I am stunned into a thoughtful silence as I return to kick-start the Enfield to find my own way here in the India of Kipling.

## 3. The Road to Bhera Ghat

## "Rolling with the River"

SHIFTING THE ENFIELD INTO NEUTRAL I GLIDE TO THE PUMP at Jaiswal's petrol station, the only commercial enterprise for miles along this stretch of road. All around there is only lush farmland, unchanged since man first put p1ow to beast. I have been riding this road to Bhera Ghat for pleasure and my own inner tranquility for twenty years now, at least whenever I could tear way from the chains of America for the bosom of Mother India. Nothing has changed, including the Enfield, except for me. My only fear along this road to eternity is that this could somehow be my last ride. Since this road to Bhera Ghat presents ultimate moto-nirvana, I must ask myself to which world could the motorcycle *yogi* be promoted next? There can be no better ride than this.

Here on this stretch of National Hwy 12, the Bhopal Road, there is a tunnel of huge shade trees lining either side. Ancient Indian kings, followers of the Vedic culture and heritage, had trees planted along these roadways to protect their traveling subjects from the harsh Sun and the elements. Their gesture was not entirely a case of royal benevolence. It was important for the far-sighted monarch to facilitate travel. Caravans of trade that used this road for millennia provided a tax base that paid for the government's largesse. Each subject was expected to offer twenty-five per cent of his income into the royal treasury.

The Vedic kings employed revenue acquired through taxation for religious ceremonies called *yagnas*. Because of these *yagnas*, prayerful fire offerings to Vishnu, the ''All-pervasive One" was pleased to provide rain in season for His supplicants. The king rewarded the *brahmanas* who performed the *yagnas* and through the resulting measured rainfall farmers prospered. Thus the kingdom remained prosperous and plentiful. The economy therefore was based upon religious sacrifices that brought rainfall, all overseen by a Maharaja. In the Vedic age the king was highly respected as the *nara-dev*, or "human representative of God." Indeed, long ago some kings were themselves accepted as veritable *avatars*, as seen in this verse from the *Shrimad Bhagavata Purana*: "King Prithu will be as powerful as the Sun god, and just as the Sun god equally distributes his sunshine to everyone, King Prithu will distribute his mercy equally. Similarly, just as the Sun god evaporates water for eight months and, during the rainy season, returns it profusely, this King will also exact taxes from the citizens and return these monies in times of need." \[SB 4.16.6\]

This simple Vedic system worked beautifully until invaders from places like Afghanistan and Persia, and later Europe, pulverized India with demeaning physical and intellectual attacks. After 1,000 years of exploitation, India has been reduced to its present contradictory state of being a third world nuclear behemoth. India boasts one out of every five engineers worldwide, yet is stricken with poverty hardly seen elsewhere. Today India is a highly advanced, yet backward, land whose present cannot find the link that joins its past with its future. Confusion has become the national pastime.

It is not uncommon among first-time visitors to jump to the conclusion that it is India's indigenous religion that has failed her. This misconception has been successfully exploited by Christians since the British Raja, but it has also met with stiff resistance from staunch devotees faithful to the religion of the sages. The truth is that which has really failed India and brought her to this state is *not* following her eternal heritage. To some degree India can thank the generosity of ten centuries of foreign meddlers intent upon dismantling the oldest culture on earth. Of course, it would be un-Hindu for Indians to mete out blame, for they will explain it as the laws of *karma* at work: something done here in India must first have invited this cultural downfall. There can be no reaction without a primary action; some rooted misdeeds that planted karmic seeds, spreading exponentially like so many garden weeds choking out the flowers. Apparently when one's own culture is neglected, the door opens to other, sometimes unwanted, cultures.

These thoughts aside, today as I linger at Jaiswal's petrol station along the road to Bhera Ghat, it is as if the bloody tidal waves of looting, plundering invaders never arrived to wash this old land in red. These glorious old shade trees still shelter the wide expanse of the Bhopal Road. In the morning, before the Sun's rays turn harsh, the farmers till the soil made rich after evening rains. *Brahmanas* on bicycles hurry just a bit to meet their appointments with deities, while mothers bathe their children at wells set far from the road. In rotating eternal cycles, life goes on as it always has.

"Oi sahib," the attendant, Mr. Tiwari, casually greets me, although we haven't seen each other for a few years. He acts like I am among his most regular customers, as though only a week and a tank of gas separate us from our last pumpside get-together. Like many Indians of the countryside, he appears to have an unspoken grasp on the eternal.

In this land, even the commonplace becomes astounding. Tiwari fills the tank lovingly as though each drop is liquid gold. I feel welcomed just by the kind finesse he displays making sure that each drop finds its way into the reservoir, and that no careless splash stains the Enfield's chrome tank. My mind drifts to the secret places on the river's shore that will be mine to see in just a few moments. Some wanderers actually do feel affection for this crazy India and I am one of them. I have fallen entirely under the mystic spell of Mother India, and her rivers like the nearby River Narmada, just as those very waters run through my veins. Mere kilometers from Jaiswal's station are *ashrams* upon the riverbank in which austere *yogis*, drawing from the divine potency of the river's liquid goddess benefactress, cure the sick without medicines. There are places where *avatars* have meditated within the white cliffs above her emerald flow. Beneath her waves demigods and sages have entered into unknown subterranean worlds. Even today barefoot pilgrims circumambulate her entire length, a 1600-mile *parikram* over very rough terrain and among rougher tribals.

Suddenly my mind returns to the situation at hand, the petrol pump, for a stranger is approaching me curiously. He is a traveler from Bhopal, by the license plate on his Rajdoot motorcycle. He is obviously from an urban environment, since he does not posses the villagers' shy and distant friendliness. This aggressive young man wants to show off. He has probably come here because of some business with the nearby medical college, though he does not have the pensive features of an Indian medical student. He is apparently unaware that his ridiculous greasy Beatles haircut, sideburns, sunglasses and bell-bottom trousers that embody the Carnaby Street look faded away a few years before he was born.

He queries clumsily, "Have you ever been to India?" I can't but help finding this a bit too tempting. "Yes, in fact, I'm there now." I reply, dryly mirroring his deadpan expression.

He keeps his composure: "I mean before ... have you been there before?” His reference to the place where he is as "there" indicates that I’ve gotten to him.

"Yes, certainly, many *yogis* have told me that I was born there in my past life."

Amusingly, he shows no change in his composure except to take two steps backwards, while slowly wiggling his head. I wiggle mine to the same rhythm in reply as he responds by spitting out a huge red gob of paan the ground. Oh well, I joke with myself, enlightenment does not come easily. Many of today’s generation consider discussions regarding aspects of their ancient Vedic culture as outmoded. But I have managed to side step the two most asked questions. These are: "From which place you are coming?" and "Do you eat Indian food?" He refuses to even glance in my direction as he putt-putts off on his little two-stroke Rajdoot, which sounds like a well-played tabla drum. I'm not entirely happy about irritating him. There are others here who actually do have a sense of humor. I tell myself, he'll get over it. It is still mid-morning and a glorious day lies before me down the road.

Tiwari locks my gas cap, politely accepts my three hundred rupees with a nod and waves me a friendly farewell. I nose the Enfield onto the road towards my first stop, the temple of Tripurasundari Devi, literally "beautiful goddess of the three cities". These “cities” represent the three strata of this material Universe: *swarga* or the heavenly world where the pious enjoy their rewards; *naraka* or hell where sinners get theirs; and the middle world of short-lived humankind called Bhumi-*loka* or Earth, where futures are quickly decided. She is also the goddess of the three modes--or *gunas*--of material nature, goodness, passion and ignorance. The goddess also oversees the three types of woes that arise from violating Nature's laws: miseries caused by others; those caused by the body itself; and pains inflicted by the *devas*, or demi-gods who control Nature. Here within our limited citadel of the material Universe, it is the goddess of material energy who reigns supreme as the "goddess of threes."

This area of the Narmada is famous for its *swayambhu*, or self-manifested, deities. The holy *murti* form of the Mother Goddess Shri Tripurasundari is said to have mystically appeared some thousands of years ago to one King Karna. She is unique in all of India insofar as she has three faces not carved by any hand. Her form reveals three of the goddess' many aspects: one face is Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth; the second is Saraswati, the goddess of learning; and the third is Kali, the "black" goddess who destroyed the wicked demon Raktabija or "Bloodseed." I spot the jungle cow path to her temple off to the left.

As the Enfield finds direction, I am surprised to discover that the narrow jungle path to the temple has been recently paved. Later I am told that the state's chief minister, having heard of the once-obscure goddess and her glories, paid her a visit. As an offering to her, he had the rocky pathway turned into a solid strip of asphalt. Doubtless, the offering was made with taxpayer revenue and carried with it a prayer to the goddess that the minister be re-elected forever.

As the bike leads me along the new temple lane, a pair of humble beggars seated along the roadside catches my eye. A husband, in his middle years, sits peacefully upon an empty burlap bag beside his faithful wife. He bears the scars of some horrendous attack--someone has thrown acid onto his face leaving him blind and featureless. I discover some time later that the incident occurred over a Rs.250 debt. What is remarkable is how well cared for he is.

His wife, an ever-loyal Hindu *dharma-patni,* has kept him clean and groomed. Though a beggar, she is in so many ways the typical Indian wife known for her legendary fidelity. If in India a man is found who is as focused upon God as Hindu women are devoted to their husbands, he is revered as a *mahatma* and is seated upon a throne. The Hindu *patni* is not content to serve only in this life, she wishes to join him in the next one, too. The examples of Sati, Anusuya, Savitri and Damayanti, found in the historical epics *Mahabharata* and *Ramayana*, are dear to every Hindu heart. India’s ancient Vedic scriptures glorify those wives whose devotion has elevated each one to the venerable level of demi-goddess.

In the Rajasthani town of Jhunjhunu there is a temple called the Rani Sati Temple that was built to a *dharma-patni* whose husband was killed by local king in single combat. The grieving widow challenged the king and--defeating him in a sword fight--mounted the funeral pyre of her husband. Naturally, Westerners look askance at such extremes of single-minded love, and since the British times the act of *suttee* or *sati* has been declared illegal in India. Yet in this age of social liberation and supposed equality, the steadfast morality and unshakable devotion of traditional Indian women hardly seems dented. Their reasoning was that the burning of separation from the husband was more intense than the flames of fire.

Some years back while riding through Vrindavan and Vraja Mandala--a large area of Western Uttar Pradesh sacred to the *lila* of Lord Krishna, the Supreme Personality of Godhead--I chanced upon an unusual sight. There alongside the River Yamuna a group of villagers was hurriedly constructing a shrine. Stopping to inquire through an interpreter, I found that a young husband had suddenly passed on. His bride had immediately expressed her desire to undergo this *sati* rite of self-immolation upon the pyre of the husband.

The name of the ritual comes from the wife of Shiva who produced internal flames in a *yogic* trance out of disgust for her father Daksha. Daksha had insulted her husband Shiva, and she could no longer bear having a body produced by an offender. The *sati* rite was outlawed by the British because widows who had no desire to leave this world were forced to burn at their husbands' funerals by pitiless in-laws who did not wish to care for them.

The builders of the temple continued speaking excitedly. The authorities of Vraja informed the local constabulary of this unfortunate widow’s intention of following her husband to the next world. The police in turn declared that she would not be allowed to attend the funeral of her husband and quickly confiscated his body. The authorities then tried to have his remains cremated in a hidden area, when a sudden storm gathered and rain doused the fire. The police, exasperated, threw the body into the river. As fate would have it, the body washed ashore miles down stream, not far from the bereaved lady. Hearing that her husband’s body was discovered nearby, the weeping widow rushed there and placed his lifeless head upon her lap. After several hours of meditation, self-combustion sprang from under her fingernails and toenails, and in minutes the two were consumed. I was given a photograph of the young lady for whom the shrine was now being built.

Major General Sir William Sleeman, who was the British civil servant in charge of old Jubbalpore (Jabalpur), records, the following narration from 1829 in his remarkable *Rambles and Recollections of an Indian Official*:

The following conversation took place one morning between me and a native gentleman at Jubbulpore soon after suttees had been prohibited by Government:

'Sometimes women used to burn themselves with any relic of a husband, who had died far from home, did they not?'

Yes, sir, I remember a fisherman, about twenty years ago, who went on some business to Benares from Jubbulpore, and who was to have been back in two months. Six months passed away without any news of him; and at last the wife dreamed that he had died on the road, and began forthwith, in the middle of the night, to call out "Sat, sat, sat!" Nothing could dissuade her from burning; and in the morning a pile was raised for her, on the north bank of the large tank of Hanuman, where you have planted an avenue of trees. There I saw her burned with her husband's turban in her arms, and in ten days after her husband came back.'

Pulling up alongside the beggar couple, I respectfully offer a Rs 100 note. It's strange, but this is almost half of the debt that has now led to the couple's insurmountable suffering. The two of them are floored by a small offering of a little over two dollars. But then India is a land where peasants sometimes sell themselves into bonded labor to the village lender over a small loan, usually to raise a dowry so that a daughter may be properly married. Many spend entire lives as indentured servants to pay off the interest.

Before me, the thin asphalt line with its crests, curves and jungle serenity is the slow-wheeling road-*yogis* dream. It ends quietly at the parking lot of the Temple of the Mother Goddess. First I park the Enfield, then I park my shoes, and walk towards the shrine.

Just then the temple priest passes. He is herding the cows which belong to the deity. The milk produced by these cows is used in the worship of Tripurasundari Devi. Milk is transformed into ghee for the fire offerings, into sweets like *barfi* and *rasmalai*, or into yogurt or cheese and placed before Mother Goddess upon a silver plate. In India thousands of preparations are made from milk. Therefore, cow slaughter is considered akin to killing the goose that laid the golden egg, and those who eat beef are considered worthy of only being thrown into the darkest regions called *naraka* in a tormented afterlife. These cows will never enter a slaughterhouse; each be cared for in the temple *goshala* until her natural departure.

The priest Pandit Deviprasad, gently escorts the cows to their barn. Ten minutes later he returns bathed and dressed in his ceremonial *dhoti* with his fresh forehead mark of *kum kum* signifying his devotion at the feet of the goddess he serves.

Since I have come from a great distance, and the *pandit* and I have known each other for many years, he offers me a seat of honor in front of Tripurasundari. He blesses me by smearing *kum kum* powder with his thumb upon my forehead, and drops three small spoonfuls of *charanamrita* into my waiting right palm. Literally "deathless at the feet of God," *charanamrita* is water that has washed the deity during the morning bathing ritual. It is mixed with sacred leaves from the *tulasi* plant and is given as a sacrament to pilgrims as an elixir against the reactions to their bad *karma*. I drink it from the lower palm, from the *pitri-tirtha*, slightly to the left of where the fate line meets the life line. Panditji blesses me, *sarva papa vinasanam.* ''All sinful reactions to past misdeeds are now destroyed."

As Panditji prepares for the noon *aratik*, I return to my bike and retrieve my *japa mala*, blessed chanting beads that my spiritual master gave me at the time of initiation in 1968. The chant is called the *mahamantra* or "great chant for deliverance." In this age of discord, Kali Yuga, *mantra* meditation upon the holy names of God is the prescribed *yagna* or religious sacrifice for all people under all circumstances and at all times. Chanting *japa* consists of murmuring the *mantra* consisting of three divine names: Krishna, the All-attractive Supreme Lord; Rama, the Supreme Enjoyer; and Hare, the divine internal potency of the Lord. These three names of God arranged in a sixteen word *mantra* are the transcendental means of attaining ultimate spiritual enlightenment in this age. The entire universe is created in sound, the *tan mantra*. Just as our very lives and moral character are founded upon the instructions in the forms of sound vibrations received from our parents and teachers, so spiritual sound vibration in the form of the holy names of God can give us a foundation for eternal life.

The internal potency of the Lord, or the supreme spiritual feminine energy called Yogamaya, is personified as Shri Radha. Goddess Tripurasundari is an expansion of Shri Radha and is Her "shadow energy." This *mahamantra* is a prayer to the Supreme Lord Shri Krishna that we may become engaged continually in His service here and hereafter by the mercy of His internal pleasure potency. As a reflection of Yogamaya, Goddess Tripurasundari represents the external material energy or Mahamaya, the world of illusion. Hare Krishna quite simply means Radha and Krishna and is a devotee’s prayer to become engaged in Their service. *Mantra* recitation is soothing, elevating and ecstatic:

***Hare Krishna Hare Krishna Krishna Krishna Hare Hare***  
***Hare Rama Hare Rama Rama Rama Hare Hare***

Within a few minutes, the bells and gongs lift me from my meditation. Devi Prasad has begun his offering of a *ghee* lamp before Mother Goddess. Joining the other pilgrims in unison, we immediately spring to our feet, for not to do so would be disrespectful. As the ceremony progresses, it occurs to me that this site will one day become a principal center of worship of the goddess--who is generally known as Durga--just like Vaishno Devi at Jammu, Sharada Devi at Maihar or Savitri Devi at Pushkar. Soon the sound of the blown conch shell signals the end of the ceremony, and Devi Prasad honors me by escorting me to the bike. Riding out on a cloud of bliss, I pass the beggar couple. The lady smiles shyly and speaks into the ear of her blind husband, who likewise beams in my direction through layers of dark brown scar tissue. I lean the bike left onto the Bhera Ghat Road and pour on the throttle. The road to happiness can only be paved with bricks laid by acts of piety.

There is an analogy that those who worship the demigods and goddesses for material benefits are like prisoners who, being lucky enough to meet the lieutenant governor, ask only to be transfered to a better cell. An intelligent prisoner should request the lieutenant to talk to the Governor on his behalf, and arrange for a pardon. He who prays for material benefits must also accept a material body, with which to enjoy these "boons." The goal of *yoga*, of all worship, is freedom from this body and this world. Rebirth in the domain of the Devi, the goddess of the three material worlds, is the goal of the materialist. The true *yogi* achieves the Yogeshwar through abandoning all desires for material elevation and the fruitless quest for enjoyment in this material body. The difference between a motorcycle *yogi* and the two-wheeling tourist is the purpose of the ride. It is ironic that those who renounce the notion of pleasure--that is, those who ride for the pleasure of the Lord--achieve a higher pleasure than those who ride for the sake of pleasure. Riding headlong into the breeze now, I am soothed by a gentle wind that carries the fragrance of moist earth. I recall Yogeshwar Krishna’s words in the *Gita*, ''Among purifiers, I am the wind." I inhale deeply of His wind and exhale by calling His name into the incoming breeze as the Enfield sings.

Soon a large *darwaza*, a cement portal, welcomes me on the left. *Darwaza* is an Urdu word meaning "gateway" that has found itself into the Hindi language. For that matter, it has also found itself into English as the word "door," though likely both find their origin in *dwar* from the world's oldest language, Sanskrit. I successfully negotiate the left turn through the Bhera Ghat Darwaza despite the accumulated layers of rocks, mud, sand and rubbish that make India's "highways" such a challenge for the motorcyclist, and pass through the gateway towards Bhera Ghat.

For the purpose of helping the pilgrim to reflect upon his spiritual journey, this *dwar* to the *dham* was constructed by a local committee. The pilgrim who passes through the door to a holy spot should mentally prepare himself. Prayerful acknowledgement of the Supreme Spirit who resides within and without all creatures in all species of life is the wayfarer's first duty. The concept of passing through one door as we leave this world, only to enter the next one, is found in all religious beliefs. The question that the Bhera Ghat Darwaza forces the pilgrim to reflect upon is, "Which door will I pass through next?" Internal prayer and reflection might inspire the answer, while devotional service provides the light in a journey filled with uncertainty.

As I draw closer to the Narmada, the landscape appears to transform. Passing through the gateway, we have entered a mystical realm. Many of India's other sacred *tirtha-bhumis* have become exploited by charlatans, turned into carnivals complete with clowns in the dress of holy men and *brahmanas*. Yet motorcycling down this road is different: this is Vedic India as it always has been, Earth's most ancient civilization in tirelessly revolving cycles. That which was the past is now the present and will become the future. Just as a tire going round and round occasionally throws out a pebble, sometimes the wheel of *samsara* releases the pilgrim seeker from the ever-circling *mandala* of continued birth as he passes through the door to the eternal kingdom.

The Narmada is a virgin goddess, and this sacred site along her banks is unmolested and untouched by swindlers who make a living with their eyes upon the wallets of innocent pilgrims. She comes into view at her confluence with the Ban Ganga River. The genuine *brahmanas* who reside by the river say that the mere sight of her flow obliterates the reactions to millions of past sins, what to speak of the benefit derived by pilgrims who come here to perform selfless acts of devotion upon her banks. She is one of this magic land's seven rivers whose waters can grant final release after millions of birth in the whirlpool frenzy of *samsara*. Sacred bath at Bhera Ghat prepares the pilgrim’s soul for the eternal and anti-material energy, the effulgent Vaikuntha planets, and final liberation at last.

Bhera Ghat, in the Jabalpur District of Madhya Pradesh state, is approximately half way between the eight-hundred-mile river's origin and her place of merging with her lord, the ocean god Sagar. Once Lord Shiva was passing through the hills that are now known as Amarkantak at the Narmada’s source. Sitting down to meditate, he entered into a blissful trance. As a symptom of his ecstasy, Shiva began to perspire profusely. His sweat took the form of a daughter, a lady of great beauty and supple limbs. Because she was *narma-dayini*, "the giver of happiness," she was named Narmada. The demi-gods or *amaras* lusted after her and descended upon the earth to share her company. Desiring to remain a virgin she outran the gods all the way to the sea, leaving the *amaras* pulling thorns or *kantaks* from their feet. Hence, the place of the Narmada’s origin is called Amarkantak. There are said to be ten times ten million holy places at Amarkantak, where Narmada Devi is worshipped as a goddess who rides a crocodile.

Regarding crocodiles, there have in fact been a few sightings of them reported locally. A boatman told me a story involving his colleague who was rowing a group of about a dozen pilgrims upstream to the Dhuandhar Falls. On board the boat was a mother carrying her child in her arms, who was amusing himself by splashing his hand in the river. Suddenly the toddler screamed, a crocodile had locked onto his arm. The panicked mother called to the boatman for help. In response, the boatman grabbed the child from the arms of his mother, and threw him overboard. Later he explained that trying to wrestle the child out of the jaws of the crocodile would have been useless. The crocodile would have overturned the boat and every passenger aboard would have then drowned or have been eaten by crocodiles.

The river is hundreds of feet deep here, and the ferocious reptiles dwell on the bottom. Each time I have entered the river upstream at my secluded *ghat*, it has occurred to me that it might be my last swim. I joke with myself that leaving this world as food for the mount of the river goddess would be superior to so many other routes to the inevitable exit. Personally, I have never seen a crocodile here.

Riding alongside the Narmada, I soon enter the tiny village of Bhera Ghat and park the bike in front of my friend Mr. Jain's tiny shop. Mr. Jain vends marble sculptures to tourists. He'll keep an eye on the Enfield as I have decided to hire a boat for a solitary ride upriver to the falls. As I turn towards the steps leading down to the river, a voice calls, "From where you are coming, Sir." I turn to find a well dressed teenager eager for conversation. I answer in clear tones affecting an Indian accent, to assure being understood, "From America. Now may I ask you a question?"

"Yes, Sir."

"Do you like Indian food?" I have the drop on him now, and the irony of my query--that he would never have tasted anything that is not Indian food--escapes him.

After some serious thought, he replies, "It is good, Sir. Too good."

"Much too good." I add. "It should not be so good."

"Why, Sir? How it can it be too good?" I have his interest, yet his tone has changed to one of skepticism. He has told me himself that it is too good and wishes to find out why it should not be so.

"Because of your tasty spices you had to endure Britain's East India Company for two hundred and fifty years."

"Quite right, Sir. It should not be that good." My new friend has a sense of humor.

A long flight of stone steps, perhaps a quarter mile in length, leads down to riverside boat launch. Along the way, tiny shops sell marble sculptures of Shiva, Hanuman, Lord Rama and other worshipful Deities “Old Hindoostan," an India that here at least is still very much alive. Most of the sellers are very poor, earning only a few pennies daily. My habit is to search for *swayambhu lingas*, the self-manifested forms of Shiva that the Narmada is famous for producing.

Reaching the shore I no longer feel awe and reverence for the river, but rather the familiarity of seeing an old friend, one who keeps bringing me back to her emerald waves. The Narmada is a friend all the way to the last, for who else would accept the burnt ashes of the departed who looked to her for emancipation from *samsara*? Yet she is a friend whose steadiness one cannot rely upon entirely. Hence, the Sanskrit classics warn us, too much familiarity with rivers, as well as with women, kings or fire is unwise. Fortunately I have arrived in mid-June with the turmoil of monsoon season less than two weeks away. When the incessant rains come, and the excess water at the Bhargi Dam upstream will be released, this placid emerald stream will transform into a swirling, chocolate brown spate that only a fool would approach. The falls above Bhera Ghat will then disappear into an angry foaming whirlpool hundreds of feet deep. This pleasant jungle scene before me will be entirely transformed with the scheduled whim of the goddess of material Nature, as we mere humans look on awestruck, yet powerless. Seeing the placid forest and peaceful river life, I make a note to return in a few weeks to see her mood’s scheduled metamorphosis when two-wheeling through a hot summer monsoon rainstorm becomes an entirely different form of motorcycle *yoga*.

A gentle boatman, whose entire life has been spent at this spot on this river, greets me warmly. For twenty rupees Krishnalal agrees to take me upstream between the towering marble cliffs, to the falls. Minutes out, as the river narrows and gentle banks give way to steep rocks, the world is mystically transformed. It is like entering in an untouched cavern of the nether world. Aeons ago in caves far above us, *yogis* through penance and meditation sanctified the towering cliffs that loom on both sides. At Monkeys' Leap, Hanuman played with his friends before Lord Rama requested these *vanaras*, or supernatural monkeys, to help Him defeat the demonic King Ravana. Shanidev or Saturn, the lord of the planet of *karma* and *tapasya*, himself came here to penance and improve his own *karma*. So did the combined incarnation Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva, the sage Dattatreya, who long ago appeared as the son of Atri Maharshi and Anusuya. Indradeva, the thousand-eyed King of Heaven, dove into these waves with his elephant mount Airavata on his way to the nether regions far beneath the Earth. Villagers point to the shoreline mark of Airavata's huge hoofprint in the marble as proof.

As we wend our way upstream, I ask the boatman to pause at an island upon which yogis are still occasionally seen in meditation. On this small rock a century ago Queen Ahalyabai, installed a self-manifested Shiva-linga. "Exploring" the tiny rock island takes less than a minute. I bow my head with the prayer *vaishnavanam yatha shambhu*--an acknowledgement that the greatest of all devotees of Lord Vishnu is Shiva--and then return to the boat. Of the so-called "Trimurti," the three Lords who control the Universe; Lord Brahma creates and Lord Shiva destroys. Yet only Lord Vishnu can maintain. Hence Lord Vishnu is accepted by the greatest sages as supreme amongst them.

Over the ages, and from throughout India, kings and wealthy merchants who desired to build a temple to Shiva have made the trip to Bhera Ghat, and have returned home with a self-manifested Deity, the Shiva-*linga*. The Shiva-*linga* is an egg-shaped black stone of varying sizes found along the Narmada, and sometimes bearing prehistoric, fossilized markings. How the Shiva-*linga* appeared at Bhera Ghat is told locally, but is also described in the ancient Sanskrit literature, the *Puranas*. Once there was great demon king named Banasura. He propitiated Lord Shiva upon the shore at Bhera Ghat by fashioning innumerable Shiva-*lingas* with his 1,000 arms. Finally Lord Shiva became pleased with Bana's austerities and appeared to the demon king. "What would you like, my son?" the great god asked. "Oh Shiva, Oh Mahadeva," Bana replied, "Please turn all these sandy *lingas* I have made with my 1,000 hands into stone so that your image can be worshipped throughout the world." Shiva smiled and replied, "*Tatastu*. So be it." Riding upon his bull carrier Nandi, Lord Shiva departed leaving Bana with all his wishes fulfilled. Since then, this place has been called Banasura Ghat, shortened over time to Bhera or Bheda Ghat.

Whenever I come here, I find several Shiva-*lingas* which, in fulfillment of Lord Shiva’s blessings, I place in homes locally or abroad. Once I installed one about two inches tall in the home of a Maharashtrian widow. That done, I turned to her and said, "Your Shivaji is named Trimbakeshwar", so-named for a major temple in her home state of Maharashtra. The lady swooned and fainted, only her friend's catching her saved her from hitting the ground. As she returned to consciousness she confided, "My departed husband was named Trimbakeshwar." Returning Stateside with a cache of smaller ones, even well-known Hollywood stars and rock musicians have taken them from me for worship in realization of Lord Shiva’s wish.

I have also found or purchased three quite large Shiva-*lingas*, one of them weighing as much as fifty or sixty pounds, and have made local shrines for these around Jabalpur. Each one is in a public place, still worshipped regularly, and can be visited today by anyone who cares to take the time. These small temples are Rameshwar in Adhartal, Pavaneshwar in Ashok Nagar, and Janeshwar at Damoh-naka.

To my sheer delight, from within Krishnalal's boat, the beauty of this never never land comes to a zenith at the roaring falls. Krishnalal and I are the only ones floating here at the moment, so it is like having my own private mini-Niagara. This wonder of the world is one of India's best kept secrets, only a handful of non-Indians come here each year. Indeed, not that many Indians are aware of it, and of those who do come, very few know of its significance in the world of Puranic history. Rowing among the cliffs and into the chamber of the falls is an ascent into a mystical realm, whereas the return journey becomes a descent back into the routine of *samsara*.

At the boat launch, I pay Krishnalal, bidding him farewell as I ascend the stairs that lead up to tiny Bhera Ghat village. Every shop specializes in only one item, marble carvings. The locally produced sculptures are mostly carved out of the soft marble, actually soapstone, from the cliffs that rise from the river. Larger deities are made from harder marble that has been trucked in from Makrana in Rajasthan, the same place from where the Taj Mahal's marble originated. The stunning beauty of the deities of Hanuman, Sita-Rama, Radha-Krishna, Dattatreya, or of the river goddess Narmada seated upon her crocodile makes me wonder how these spectacular artistic masterpieces were formed by simple village craftspeople, most of whom are assumed to be illiterates. Larger deities, three to six feet tall, take several artisans working tirelessly all day for many months to complete. The finished products are incarnations in stone whose beautiful faces reflect the loving hearts of the artists, men and women whose single-minded devotion to putting God's face on expressionless marble is obvious.

Turkish and Persian invaders considered such artwork as blasphemy like the Taliban of Afghanistan, which destroyed the majestic 150-foot tall Buddha carved into the cliffs of the Bamiyan Valley. There is a temple above Bhera Ghat, over one thousand years old, called Sixty-four Yoginis or Chaunsath Yogini dedicated to the many rare forms of the Universal goddess. Although the temple is there and can still be visited since it has now been granted historical status, it saddens me to go there. Each deity has been cracked, defiled and desecrated by iconoclastic invaders. All *puja* has been halted at the temple for no *brahmana* will worship a broken deity.

The British, unlike the Turks, took a different approach in their zeal at dismantling Hinduism. The East India Company's method was to simply wear the Hindus down with arguments about their supposed backwardness and the impracticality of their religion. Certainly, in the British mind, the Indian was in every way inferior, but by becoming Anglicized, his poor lot could be improved at least a little. Under this charade, the East India Company looted India, and then told the Indians that it was due to their religion that they had become bankrupt. British archaeologists would wax eloquently about old stone, brass or marble *murtis* if they were several hundred years old. Such purloined artifacts are found in indological displays in museums of London and the world taken, like the Koh-I-Noor diamond, by India's "benefactors." Yet the creation of these same deities today handmade by the same ancient techniques is discounted as idolatry by Christians who themselves keep statues of Jesus and Virgin Mary.

A so-called "indologist" can authoritatively discuss an ancient Ganesh destined for some museum exhibit with enthusiasm, yet the same scholar will transform himself into a modern, disapproving moralist when seeing the same Ganesh today take shape before the creative hands of *shilpis* for temple installation. Somehow the revelation of coming face to face with the oldest continuous living, breathing culture on Earth eludes the erudite scholar whose head is stuck in times that are lost and whose morals are stuck in his own form of antiquated myths. For him the past belongs to his sciences like anthropology and archaeology, but the present belongs to religious narrow mindedness. Yet I foresee that in 5000 years the same deities will be carved and ceremoniously worshipped while every theory of modern science will have been revised a hundred times like clockwork, and all paid for by governments using taxpayers’ funds.

Under India's British educational system, modernization came to mean--and still means--rejection of thousands of years of rich heritage. *Brahmanas* who once sang beautiful *mantras* in praise of the Supreme Absolute Truth were transformed into clerks shuffiing around endless piles of paperwork. Traditional Indian dress was outlawed at government offices. A teacher of English received a salary twenty-five times greater than the few rupees thrown the way of a Sanskrit *pandit*. Still the British Raj brought undeniable advantages: building the world's most extensive railway, the English language or--closer to home--the Enfield motorcycle I ride today.

Today the deities of Bhera Ghat that I am admiring are destined for temples all over India and abroad. I hope that wherever each one is placed for worship through the *pran-pratistha* or "life breath ceremony," their supplicants will no longer have to bear the hardship caused by prejudice and intolerance. Each one deserves to prosper and to give prosperity for a thousand years and more. And Vedic India deserves credit for keeping her culture alive in the face of one thousand years of horrendous foreign onslaught.

Leaving the shops of the *shilpis*, I cross the packed dirt road to the waiting bike. I thank Mr. Jain for keeping an eye upon the Enfield, and kick-start its single cylinder for a ride through the town. At 15 kph Bhera Ghat village can be toured in its entirety in about five minutes. For the rest of the day I ride on throughout the district visiting holy lakes, cave and hillside temples. At Madan Mahal, the site of the fortress of Rani Durgavati, who was speared upon her elephant by invaders from Delhi, I reflect upon this young queen who married a tribal king--because he was the most qualified--and who died for her beloved Jabalpur in a fight with the army of Akbar.

As the brilliant lunar crescent comes into view over the Vindhya Hills at sunset, the melodious vibration of temple singing wafts across the lake called Dev Tal. As I slow down to listen, I am filled with the sound of *akhand-kirtan*, endless chanting of the holy names of the Lord. Since this temple was built half a century ago, the name of holy name of Rama has been chanted here day and night without break. I pass through the *darwaza* with the prayer that I will see this place again in this lifetime.

The ride home is meditative and reflective. It occurs to me that except for a few ridiculous conversations about Indian food, I have hardly spoken all day. It has been a journey of meditative bliss on two wheels, a good day’s ride. So good, in fact, that if am called from this world here and now I would leave entirely fulfilled and satisfied.

## 4. Around Damoh

## "... Of Dogs and Men and Demi-Gods"

THE ENFIELD FISHTAILS AS I SLIDE TO A STOP on the pebbles at a heavily trafficked intersection. Rows of soot-encrusted trucks carrying crushed stones stand here like elephants passing their time, snorting at each other. The large granite chunks they carry from local quarries are used for building roads yet there is hardly a road beneath their huge frozen tires. Constant heavy trucking and torrential monsoon rains have ground the thin hand-laid asphalt ribbon that once lay here into little more that dried mud. Nothing is permanent anyway; as the road once was, so it has again become.

Marking the dead center of the crossroads is an unfortunate stray dog who some days ago had carelessly wandered amidst insensitive giant wheels. He has been rendered into a sort of mangy dog-skin rug and now only a flat stretched out four-legged pelt remains of him. The trucks are all Indian-built Tata diesels. For protection, many have the grinning face of a *rakshasa* demon painted on the differential housing. Others sport an old shoe dangling from the bumper. Yet, judging by the mishaps caused by these giants, I doubt the efficacy of these measures.

Hand lettered upon the rear gate of each one is a slogan that reads "Horn OK Please" or ''Awazdo'' (make noise). What noise on earth could rise above this din? A road sign reads DEAD SLOW WORK IN PROGRESS. Without punctuation, I provide my own interpretation since these trucks can grind a road into pebbles before the asphalt dries. Rather than dead slow work, road building is an eternal process like the slow march of the soul towards *moksha*. I rev the bike's engine menacingly at the Tatas, but it is only for my own self amusement. I could slog around them on the left by sloshing 19" Dunlops through a 12" deep bank of mud, but I'd rather not get my shoes and tires weighted with wet earth that's better left and needed where it is. Besides, I am merely a wanderer just passing through and I need no mud clots as souvenirs. My wish is to change nothing and meditations will be my mementos. In any case, if the bike weighing 370 pounds plus me on it sinks a half-wheel in quick mud, it will be a near-impossible mess to extract.

Beyond the four-corners of this miserable crossroad lie green fields of rice covered by a serene mirror of still water. In one of the fields a dozen or so women in gorgeously colored saris of red or green or blue are bent overing the paddies, innocently displaying a row of round buttocks to the road. White cranes stand about socially off to the side; sharp eyes and ears focused at the shoots for frogs who surface to hear themselves croak. The frogs are not unlike me who sits here, respectfully wary of the Tatas and idly revving my Royal Enfield's single cylinder, wasting precious petrol just to reassure myself. Off to my right a mother rhesus monkey, her baby clinging tightly to her back, makes a mad dash up a tree. The baby rhesus gives me a fearful look the two disappear into the branches. Instinctively, I remove my sunglasses, lest one of her associates drops down from behind and tries to steal them.

A flock of green parrots dancing through the sky on my left zig zags skyward and merges with another group that flies in from my right, and they all wing off together one hundred strong into the clouds. I spot a momentary gap that suddenly widens in the line of trucks ahead. Pushing the gear lever with my right foot, I twist the throttle and quickly release the clutch. At 18 horsepower, the 350cc Bullet doesn't exactly do wheelies. But what it lacks in immediate response, it makes up for in reliability. With some gritty effort and derring-do I snake through the man-made truck jungle praying not to end up under giant wheels, as has unlucky brother dog. Racing around towering trucks, I blast my horn in submissive obedience to their painted "Horn OK Please" admonitions ... as if any of the drivers could hear my horn blasting above smoke and diesel engines of this brutal manmade hell mere meters from a natural green paradise.

Breaking free from the mob of megaliths I gun the bike through upper gears until the speedo shows 65 kmh upon a stretch of paved roadway. I must put some space between the bike and any of the trucks that are lumbering after me. When roads narrow to the width of one lane, the Tatas are impossible to get around and precious life in this land of one billion becomes cheap. Cheap, that is, in the estimation of others. Even an insignificant ant or a fly will try to save itself if threatened and I am no different. "Die Trying" may seem like an impressive sticker plastered on the bumper of some overbuilt SUV back in California. No one wants to die unsung and alone in this third world. Indeed, death has but few volunteers.

It is not wise to cruise any faster than 65 kph though the Enfield is comfortable up to 85 kph. Roads can turn treacherous in a heartbeat, and the only ambulance could be a bullock cart. Finally confident that I have somehow outdistanced the Tatas, I slow the Bullet down to forty-five to absorb rural India's countryside ambience. As I summit the crest of a hill, a fertile valley lies before me. The soft gentle breeze turns a bit stiffer up here, perhaps hinting a promise of coming rain. It is the monsoon season now, though the last storm was two days ago. Here atop the bluff the terrain is rockier and the soil redder than in the lush valley. On either side of me are dense forests of large-leafed teak trees.

As the road gently undulates down through the fertile fields into the valley, I spy a lone farmer guiding an ancient wooden plow behind a huge white bull. The observant traveler through India's countryside learns early on why the cow and the bull are so revered here. By his steady and patient plodding the bull provides food grains, while the cow gives her milk in exchange for mere grass. Milk, the staple diet of India's largely vegetarian country folks, figutes into an uncountable variety of recipes: *dahi, khir*, *paneer* and hundreds of milk sweets. Eighty-five per cent of India, Hindus as well as many others, will not eat beef. Indeed, it is difficult to explain to someone living in another time and clime, but here at least you find yourself in awe of the bull's nobility, plodding through eons of changeless, yet ever-changing, civilization. Maybe for me it is a bit more obvious since I have been a vegetarian since my teens. Now a national movement to ban cow slaughter is gaining power and I have no regrets that I, too, have marched with protesters chanting *go-hatya bandh karo* ("ban cow slaughter") here in India. The farmer returns my wave as I slowly pass by. It is certain that his bull will never see a slaughterhouse.

Anchored behind the span of chrome handlebars and surrounded by winds and living nature, rather than within a windowed cage submerged in an artificial climate, it becomes easy to appreciate this land. India's natural beauty could be reflected in many places of the world, like the rich agricultural valleys of Northern California or Southern France. Yet there is a big difference. Although the Sun has pushed the mercury to the mid-twenties and the morning sky is dazzling and bright, the monsoon air hangs rich and heavy with a soft tropical wetness. Suspended moisture of the season of the rains caresses the skin and even leaves the inner spirit refreshed. Here in the Madhya Pradesh countryside you feel the soft breath of eternity, life as it always has been in balance with Nature ... once, that is, the trucks have been left behind.

Perhaps the biggest difference between riding in the West and riding in India--riding India--is this overpowering and all-encompassing awakening which grasps the eternal. Here, it seems as if that road which runs beyond the world of death reaches down, inviting the road-*yogi* with the breath of eternity. That knowledge of transcendence emanates from within every penitent *sadhu* that I bow my head to as I ride by. Immortal India is a land whose timeless history has never been appreciably described by modern scholars. They who are so limited by time will never appreciate timelessness. From an objective point of view, the only real and proper texts describing how India arrived at the here and now of today would be this land's own historical Sanskrit *shastras*. Even today, the one-hundred-thousand-verse long *Mahabharata*, the *Gita* being a small part, is known as the world's longest poem. Yet it is so much more than mere poetry: it is literally "the great history of India, that in ancient days was known as Bharata."

Modern speculations regarding India's roots and culture are mere theories, many of which were conveniently designed by invaders to suit their own motives. Profiteering colonialists with their divide and conquer agenda, as well as patronizing modern so-called historians, have disallowed the old and indigenous texts of Mother India from commenting upon herself or her origins. In fact, the Holy Scriptures of this ancient land have all been neutered by one word, "mythology." Serious research and study of the Sanskrit texts has been not only ignored but condemned due to religious bias. Instead, with the sole intent being the social subjugation of India, the phantasmal, dishonest and--according to the Sanskrit histories--entirely unfounded ''Aryan Theory" has been slipped like a sedative into the history books. Undoubtedly, this ridiculous theory is one of the biggest concoctions ever to have been thrust upon an innocent population, yet hardly a whimper has been raised against it.

Entire histories of races, of dynasties, of religious schools of thought and of the many sciences that accompanied the rise of the Eastern civilization have been stamped as fantastic and unbelievable mythologies. Many of those nuvo "authorities" have never even visited India, let alone ridden through her countryside or knelt before her *sadhus*. Modern atheistic science cannot explain how many astounding yet purely scientific principles, as those freely discussed in the *Puranas,* have arisen from the "superstitious minds of India's underdeveloped people." Early British indologists like Sir William Jones appreciated Sanskrit as the mother of all language, though later colonialists whited out this fact when they observed that it did not suit their business of colonial rule. Indeed, it is the Sanskrit influence that has given the world even basic ideas like the measurement of time, though the link to India has hardly been appreciated. The Vedic concept of time, or *kala*, is the father of the modern calendar.

Remember those wise men from the East that blessed Baby Jesus? They were from India, where gold, frankincense and myrrh are still sometimes offered to new mothers. Amazingly, the birth and life of Jesus were foretold in the *Bhavishya Purana*, long before the Jewish Prophet appeared to teach the West love of God. *Bhavishya Purana* goes on to tell how Jesus escaped dying by crucifixion to the shelter of Kashmir. In Shrinagar to this day his burial site is pointed to as Rozabol, the "tomb of the prophet," atop Shankaracharya Hill. Truth really is stranger than fiction, Bubba, and India's history is the strangest truth of all.

That which lasts forever cannot be destroyed, and that which begins must meet its inevitable doom. Civilizations, religious dogma and scientific theories rise and fall. Entire nations once expected to rule the Earth forever have crumbled and have been forgotten, as the sands of time sweep over the graves of former kings. Darwin's misguided theory has tried to show that we all descended from the monkey, whose relatives caused me to hide my glasses a few miles back. Yet there are no monkeys in upper North America, except in zoos, so how did man get there? Right, the entire continent broke away from somewhere else and found a new home, leaving all the monkeys behind. But then, why aren't those zoo-monkeys giving birth to baby humans?

We are taught an imperfect science that has drawn many conclusions over the past few decades that college professors tell us are now forever engraved in stone. One by one, old theories crumble, only to be replaced by new "everlasting," government-funded theories. Yet there are to this day places upon this very Earth that remain inaccessible, and about which we haven't a clue. We explain that it is the Earth's "gravity" that keeps these two wheels rolling upon the road, yet no scientist's test tube has produced an ounce of this invisible thing that they have named as gravity. Likewise, the sweet morning dew has been named "condensation," as though that answers that. Is atheistic science too miserly to give credit to the Earth Mother? It is she who gently waters the plants, the greenery that cleans the air for us who pollute with inventions and ideas.

Every light must have a source, yet the awe-inspiring Northern Lights, science tells us, are merely "disturbed particles." For the faithful, these lights are emanations from the transcendental body of God, and they serve to remind us that there are questions that our empiricism cannot conceive. For the acolyte of the Absolute, devotion and faith--and not speculative atheistic science--are the keys to progress beyond the traps of body consciousness. Intelligence means liberating ourselves from ignorance. Useless scientific-sounding speculations are merely chain-like bonds to confusion. The seeker queries "Why is the sky blue," and the voice of the eternal answers "Because Vishnu is blue." But that takes faith and that faith will be tested.

That which is spirit is deathless, and that which is matter must expire. Patanjali, the ancient father of *yoga* and the author of *The Yoga Sutras*, wrote in verse 2.5 "Nescience is misjudging that which is non-eternal, the impure, the painful, and the not-self to be the eternal, the pure, the pleasurable and the self." Rather than pledging allegiance to some temporary government, baseless theory or sentimental system of belief, the devotee finds everlasting pleasure through eternal subordination to the Supreme Spiritual Whole. Neither is sense gratification the goal of *yoga*, nor is enjoyment of the body real pleasure.

The energies of the world, both those that are seen and those that are not seen, are basically of two types: internal and external. The external elements that comprise the body, in which we find ourself daily growing older, are the changing, eight-fold material energies of Maya. Though our actual identities are part and parcel of the internal energy or Yogamaya, we are hopelessly embedded within the external forces due to having identified with that which is ever changing. Only by our reverting to internal consciousness, finding within the Original knowledge of He who is the Master of all energies, can the transfer of consciousness to the domain of the spirit take place. The ultimate quest for *yoga* is realized in finding our eternal relationship with the Yogeshwar, Krishna, as told in the final verses of *Bhagavad Gita*. Krishna tells Arjuna in 18.66: "Abandon all varieties of religion and just surrender unto Me. I shall deliver you from all sinful reaction. Do not fear."

The physical body, like the motorcycle, is nothing other than a mechanical device formed of the variegated elements of inferior energy. And like the body, the Enfield needs to take in fresh air through the carburetor and blow out exhaust through the rear pipe in order to function. The yogic *pranayama* is no different. With each incoming breath the *yogi* draws in the life force of *prana*, then exhales when this force is spent. Just as the hands, feet and body control the motorcycle, so the spiritual spark, the *atma*, controls the body. That body in which there is no central spirit soul is like motorcycle without an engine; it is a dead and useless object. *Yoga* is the means of awakening dormant spiritual consciousness. The strict definition is "to link." The *yoga* force links the mind of the practitioner with his higher self, and then links the self with the Yogeshwar. When this linking relationship takes on the mood of a subordinate, loving relationship of service unto the Supreme One, Shri Krishna, then the *yogi* realizes his goal in *bhakti*. The mathematics of *yoga* is quite simple: one plus One equals One.

A little deep breathing - *pranayama* - to take in the landscape of both matter and spirit, and I find myself transported into bike heaven. Excitement followed by bliss joins the ride like that which encircles the adept as he approaches the dawn of *samadhi*. I feel suspended in time as another "me" is piloting the machine. I twist the throttle reflexively and as my head slightly jerks backward, I awake transformed from eternal participant to foreign spectator once again. For a moment there I was submerged as one with this timeless land, and now I have rejoined my status as "wayfarer just passing through." No longer suspended in transcendence, the wheeling *chakra* of *samsara* continues its spin and the trance of the fixed mind is lost. The road continues.

I am riding the blissful, yet terrible road to Damoh, a town I have wanted to visit for years, more out of idle curiosity than to sightsee. From a tourist’s point of view, Damoh offers very little. The town is literally and figuratively beyond the middle of nowhere. But in this weather, this place, who could argue the destination? I am here for the ride, the motorcycle *yoga*.

Arriving in Damoh, I will most likely see that it reflects so many other towns of this vast belt of central India. The green countryside will probably end abruptly only to be replaced by a crowded, litter-strewn town that appears to be in a continuous state of unhurried crumbling. With any number of paint shops, there will hardly be one building that does not need painting. In the narrow lanes with no sidewalks, the pedestrians forced to walk in the streets will far outnumber the cars. With much work to be done in all directions, thousands of able young men may be loitering around dozens of *chai* and *paan* stalls idly chatting like authorities on all subjects. Wiring that looks like some electrician-gone-mad installed it, will likely be jumbled around electrical poles that stick out of the roadside at odd angles. There will definitely be men spitting red paan all over the streets while others urinate against walls painted with admonitions like, "DO NOT MAKE NUISANCE HERE."

How did I end up here on the road to Damoh? Over a dozen years earlier beside the River Narmada, I obtained from a *brahmana* at a place called Lameta Ghat, a large *swayambhu* Shiva-*linga* for the grand sum of twenty rupees. Somehow I developed an urge to install this deity of the Lord Destroyer under a *bael* tree at a crossroads in Jabalpur. Soon thereafter I established this Shiva stone beside a small temple of Hanuman under that very *bael* tree, with priests chanting auspicious Vedic invocations. This *murti-pratishta* took place on the second full Moon in spring, the night of Lord Buddha's birthday called Vaishakha Purnima. Behind this small city shrine, which stands to this day, is a working class colony. Therefore I named the deity Janeshwar, "lord of mankind." Daily the people of this colony perform the *puja* to Lord Janeshwar. They are simple religious men and women, the likes of whose toil country upon their backs.

A few years after this *pratishta* I visited the shrine and found that out of gratitude someone had nailed a painted sheet metal sign onto the *bael* tree which translates from Hindi: "This Shiva-baba was placed here by an American *sahib*." Every time I pass by this shrine I am reminded of the appreciation shown by these strong-as-steel members of India's work force, people whose every breath appears to be drawn against all odds. Lord Janeshwara sits alongside National Hwy 7 which runs from Benares, "the world's oldest city" to Kanya Kumari, the "virgin goddess" at the far southern tip where three great seas meet. This is India's "Mother Road" beginning at the home of Lord Shiva by the Ganges and running to Land's End, home of the Virgin Goddess, where the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal merge with the Indian Ocean. Jabalpur, the home of Lord Janeshwara, is at the exact halfway point.

There is a crossroads near Shri Janeshwar called Damoh-*naka*. This is the *naka* or "toll gate" where a fee is paid by truckers waiting to ply the Damoh Road. Thus for no particular reason other than breathing the air of the journey, I knew that someday I must make this trip to Damoh. Perhaps this ride is Lord Shiva’s will, for a Vaishnava satisfies all desires. On a day when the *panchang* astrological calendar showed an auspicious date for travel, and my Enfield was in good tune, I left the teeming bazaars of Jabalpur behind. And that is how I now find myself embraced by the green countryside along the Damoh Rd.

Damoh is a little over 100 kilometers northwest of Jabalpur, a distance covered regularly by many commuters twice daily back in America. Yet here it is considered a long trip. A voyage of one hundred kilometers in this land of twelve hundred dialects can see a change in language, dress and customs. Due to the horrible conditions of the Damoh Road, a solid Indian Ambassador car must allow for a minimum of three and a half hours for this trip that, on an American highway, would be completed in air conditioned comfort in less than an hour. For the motorcycle *yogi* whose destination is always where he happens to be, this trip will take a few days or more than that. The morning is bright and cheerful. Puddles from the recent rain reflect the Sun's rays back to salute the sky.

I soon cross the mighty Hiranya ("golden") River via a submersible bridge. Only days earlier this flat non-barricaded bridge lay beneath four feet of water. Now the river level has fallen to ten feet below the bridge. The Hiranya, as many others I pass over on this day, has turned a muddy brown from the rains. Beneath the bridge in an eddy play a dozen or so chocolate brown village boys. On a dare from his friends one of them jumps from the river wearing only a sarcastic grin. He is nine or ten and, like the others, is completely naked. With his left hand he shakes "himself" in my direction as I try not to appear amused. The little daredevil cannonballs back into the swirling waters to and laughter of his friends. Chances are, I am the first white man he has seen, hence the extraordinary greeting.

A few kilometers onward I cross a small and unnamed rivulet, the pristine appearance of which reflects one of the Puranic descriptions of *swarga-loka*, heaven. The waters of this stream could be worthy of the sports of demi-gods and their goddesses in love. In my rolling quest after the elusive secrets of India the eternal, a bath in her many waters has become an important ritual of *yoga* linking me to this sacred land. Through the sub-continent run the seven major holy rivers, although many other streams are also considered sacred. They are practically all females, but a few are actually *nadas* or male rivers, like the Brahmaputra ("son of Lord Brahma") *nada* in Assam. I have been the guest of India's waters for many decades now, so automatically the bathing *mantras* begins hypnotically revolving in my mind. This is the song that turns all waters holy, water being essentially pure. Among the four gross elements, water represents the *brahmana* class. My heart sings,

*gange cha yamune chaiva godavari saraswati*  
*narmade sindhu kaveri jalesmin sannedin kuru*

**"The seven holy rivers that can confer liberation (unto whoever bathes their sacred waters) are: the Ganges, Yamuna, Godavari, Saraswati, Narmada, Sindhu and Kaveri."**

By some great fortune, I have managed to bathe in each one of the holy rivers over time and can testify that each river goddess has her own personality and qualities. Emerging from a holy bath from a river, in the Bay of Bengal at Puri, or in the Arabian Sea at Dwaraka, or from the *sangams*-- places where two rivers meet--the result is invariably the same. I am refreshed and renewed, and just a bit closer to understanding the heartbeat of India.

We are born from water and maintained by water as we live. For the Hindu life ends not within earth but in waters that must accept his final ashes. From Gandhi to George Harrison to Jerry Garcia, the Ganges has accepted the remains of billions, transporting the souls of the faithful to heaven.

Flat rock slabs carved by the hand of God form a pathway through grasses to the water's edge. Tapping the overhang of the rock with my foot assures me that no snakes, the spoilers of paradise, are lurking unseen. In spite of being alone, I feel no fear. Wearing only a loincloth, I have left my clothes on the shore. The water is warm, soft and inviting. The rivulet embraces me with its high energy as I dog paddle to the center where a submerged boulder becomes my holy *asana*. It is no wonder that in this land rivers are worshipped as personalities: loving, benign and caring. Like a mother, the river goddess embraces us; washing our bodies and making us fit for social contact, never being repulsed by our uncleanliness.

As I become accustomed to my underwater throne in the river, a school of hundreds of friendly minnows surrounds me. I feel like a member of their social circle. My new friends are only two or three inches long and have large curious eyes. It is the noon hour now, the time for meditation upon the *gayatri mantra*. In 1969 when my *guru maharaja* gave me *diksha* or initiation, he draped a *brahmana* thread from my left shoulder around me to my right torso. This is the *yagnopavit*, the six thread-loop worn by the initiates of the twice-born caste. As per tradition, I wrap the thread around the thumb of my right hand. Silently I recite the holy *gayatri* prayer to the Sun god, counting ten times seven *mantras* upon my fingers. *Om bhur bhuvah sva tat savitur* ...

The Sun is the source of life for all the three worlds. The brilliance of the Sun in this our Universe, one Universe among many, represents only a tiny fraction of the overwhelming light of the Supreme Lord. To help the *yogi* understand the brilliant light of the Supreme Godhead, the *Vedas* explain that His effulgence equals millions of suns. Cognizant of the power of that all-transcendent light, there are many spiritualists--Shaivites, Jains, Buddhists, Sufis, followers of Adi-Shankaracharya and others--who set their goal upon obliterating all sense of egoism in order to merge with God's all-encompassing spiritual lustre, His *brahmajyoti*. My *guru maharaja* instructed that it is even higher still to go beyond that light, to the personal form of the Supreme Lord Shri Krishna Who is the Source of that blinding light. To underscore that point, he taught this *mantra* from *Shri* *Ishopanishad*:

*hiranmayena paatrena satyasyaa pihitam mukham*  
*tat tvam pushann apaavrinu saatya-dharmaaya drishtaye*

**"Oh Supreme Absolute Truth, sustainer of the Universe, Your beautiful face is covered by Your golden effulgence. Please unveil the dazzling curtain of Your light and let me, who am purely devoted unto You, behold Your form of bliss."**

My *guru maharaja* Shrila Prabhupada, a Gaudiya Vaishnava who left this world in Vrindavana in 1977, never denied that the all-transcendent Supreme Lord is the source of the *brahmajyoti* or the "white light." He urged his disciples to venture beyond merely merging with that spiritual light to the lotus feet of the Personal Godhead. Above the act of allowing the self to be swallowed by an ocean of effulgence is discovering our eternal relationship with the Supreme Lord. Love is expressed reciprocally and, based upon our willingness to serve Him, the next move is ours.

Eternally liberated souls or *moksha-jivans*, having achieved the goal of *yoga*, reside in an anti-material universe beyond this one, one that is free from the fear of death and rebirth. That supreme abode is called Vaikuntha, the "place where there is no fear." My *guru* *maharaja* never condoned the frustrating attempt at extinguishing the ego, as urged by many so-called *yogis*. It was Shrila Prabhupada’s challenge that each of us discover within the real ego which is both eternal and transcendental. This is the *atma*, the spiritual self which transcends this concocted, body-based material personality we have formed as a reflected image of our real selves. Having founded our sense of who we are based on our physical shape, race, age, nationality, religion and other temporary by-products of material inebriation, our vision of the transcendental self within has grown dim. Our original and unique spiritual personality, from which the materialistic ego is a warped reflection, lingers asleep behind a wall of Maya, illusion. Discovery of the Lord of the Heart, the Self of all selves within the very *atma* particle, awaits those eyes that are turned inward. And today motorcycle *yoga* also means my inward journey to the soul, the Soul of all souls, the most courageous trip of all.

Since God's gift of this human form of life is temporary and must not be wasted, it behooves us to make progress in *yoga* while we can. Defeat alone can be expected if life is aimed at trying to enjoy a gross physical body that is doomed to perish from its very birth. My *guru* *maharaja* taught, "Develop spiritual assets by serving Krishna, for real wealth can never be taken from you. All material wealth and opulence ends with this body. *Daivi sampath* or eternal wealth will journey with you beyond this life and into the next. Your temporary fame, fortune and social standing will be finished when this life's journey is finished. Your body will become the food for jackals, worms or fire, this is certain. Map out your journey for the trip back to Godhead." Completing my *gayatri mantra*, I offer due obeisance to God and *guru*.

*Mantra* literally means *mana-trayate* or "that which delivers the mind." The fish nibbling upon my body, their good company notwithstanding, have not assisted me in savoring the full mind-elevating power of the *mantra*. I feel like the great ascetic Saubhari Muni, whose under water meditation was spoiled when he saw fish copulating, awakening his latent desire. As a result, he soon found himself married and entangled hopelessly in the world of in-laws and offspring. But was it my imagination, or were the fish attracted to the tattoo of Lord Krishna on my right arm?

Glistening in the Sun the chrome fender of the Enfield catches my eye. The bike stands waiting: loyal, royal and ready to boil. Now with my meditation completed by the grace of the Sun god and the deities of the river, I dress quickly and return ro the saddle, rejoining the road to Damoh. With a quick glance over my shoulder I see that a herdsman from somewhere in the forest has arrived with more than a dozen big black water buffaloes that one by one lumber into the stream. By divine will I had left the water just in time.

A few months back in April and May, before the onset of the monsoon rains, this land was scorched by temperatures that could sometimes exceed 45ºC. Now fields made productive once again through seasonal cycles stand bursting with grain, obscuring peasant huts of mud and straw. The hues of the soil are reflected in the bricks of the houses: as the colour of the land changes from brown to shades of red over miles, so does the colour of each hut. Passing through the tiny village of Patan I continue onward. The bike is holding a steady 45 kph while my mind is holding onto the *mahamantra*, the sustenance of the journey.

Soon I am beholding a huge fertile valley perfectly cleaved by a small stream. Off to my right, about three kilometers below, proudly stand the painted white spires of an old temple beside the river's edge. A pleasant, slow glide into the valley brings me to a *darwaza*, or gateway. The gate reflects a recent design, one that is engraved with a Hindi greeting welcoming visitors to a Jain *mandir*. Many think of the Jains as a "branch of Hinduism," although the Jains consider themselves to be entirely separate. In truth their roots are found in the scriptures of Vedic India. The first of the twenty-three Jain teachers or *tirthankars*, Rishabhadeva was a king and an empowered *avatara* of Lord Vishnu whose glories are narrated in the *Shrimad Bhagavatam*. He is called Adinath, the "first Lord," by the adherents of this austere yet impersonal sect. King Rishabhadeva taught the science of self-realization to his one hundred sons. The twenty-third and last *tirthankar* Mahavir appeared about the same time as Buddha, or half a millennium before the appearance of Lord Jesus Christ.

The Jains are divided into two classes. These are the *swetambars* ("those who wear white"), and the *digambars* ("those who wear the directions"). Dressed in full white robes, the *swetambars* keep facial masks that prevent them from breathing insects and to preserve the life of even the most humble of creatures. The white robed *swetambar* *munis* walk with brooms to brush insects from their path. The *digambars* wear no attire whatsoever, hence they are "dressed by the directions. " They eat once a day at noon, and only the amount of food that will fill up their two palms clasped together, this being the size of one’s stomach. Both groups are strictly vegetarian, and eschew all foods that grow beneath the surface of the earth. By chance, I have had the occasion to meet with the spiritual leaders of both groups at their *ashrams*, and have had several interesting discussions, politely agreeing to disagree over their conviction that the eternal soul lacks a personal identity.

I once met a Jain gentleman on a train. The gentleman asked me "From where you are coming", and I responded "From USA."

"And you, Sir, are you from Madhya Pradesh?" I asked.

"Yes. Yes."

"Ujjain?" I asked, with the name of a nearby city.

"Yes, yes. Myself Jain."

Old temples attract me like a magnet. Unhesitatingly, I follow the narrow pathway to the temple door. Slowly I put the Enfield on its side stand under a spreading banyan tree that appears to be several hundred years old. A few countrymen have gathered with the Jain elders in front of the massive doors, greeting me as though they were expecting me. Not only do they welcome me, but they also block the door, leaving me to interpret mixed signals. Now the next move will be mine, so by way of responding I remove my shoes and my belt. The strong leather belt that I bought from a policeman's uniform store in Sadar Bazar, the cantonement market, gets rolled up and placed with my shoes. The shoes were brought from America since no store in India has size 13. The crowd beams approvingly, noting that I have indeed picked up a few eastern manners during my peregrinations. They move aside allowing me to enter.

Smiling and nodding politely to all gathered there, I pass through the temple's palatial gateway, trying to fit in yet all the while knowing I am an oddity in their midst. Having visited thousands of Indian temples, I appraise the architecture and from its simplicity I estimate that this temple is from the British period, probably built in the first half of the 1800's. Beneath a tree in the courtyard lie broken stone carvings that once belonged to a vastly more ornate temple that must have stood upon this spot in centuries past. During the iconoclastic rampages of invading Moghul hordes, beautiful temples belonging to Hindus, Jains and Buddhists were razed as the resident monks were slaughtered wholesale. In days past, furious fanatics pummeled peaceful local folk into submission for generations.

Within the temple compound I notice a group of men sitting pensively beneath a bael tree and talking softly. They rise to greet me, which gives me a chance to examine the archaeological treasures propped against the tree. These are ancient carvings of celestial musicians, *gandharvas*, and of their beautiful consorts the dancing *apsaras*. They are playing their stringed *vinas* and their *dholak* drums, praising God with song. These are the same universally adored angels that Christians read about in the Bible. In very ancient times religious teachings emanating from here in the East migrated West with the travel of merchants and sages unrestricted by passports and immigration laws. The celestial musicians reside in a heaven which is a material one and is not eternal. With their instruments the angels celebrate and adore the One who is without a second, for He is the Lord of the eternal world that lies even above theirs.

Even though the angels of this Universe dwell for vast periods of time without disease within the upper or "heavenly" systems called *swarga*, they must still succumb to death. That which is born must die. Birth in the *swarga* is an elevated yet temporary position that a man or woman of impeccable piety may hope to achieve. Despite life there being more sensually fulfilling than our brief spot life of a mere one hundred earthly years, residence in *swarga* is also temporary and subject to certain death. No one can remain in his body, not even in heaven, once his *karma* has expired, just as a hotel guest is expelled as soon as the time he has paid for has been used up.

Like us, even these angels live in the world called *mritya-loka*, "the place of death" where embodied souls must pay for their sins or reap the temporary rewards of good deeds in the coming rebirth. Like ours, the very breaths of the angels are precisely numbered. Unseen laws of *karma* govern these details of individual fate, under the control of the leaders of the demigods like Yamaraja. There is a lesson to be learned from these singing stone angels, undaunted as they are by the destruction wrought by time. Continuing to use their talents in praise of the Supreme Lord, these ancient stone pillars that lie here broken still resound with the eternal message that our lives and talents are meant for recognition of He Who maintains us.

The *karma* of each individual living entity can be read through the stars at birth. Here in India that science of astrology is called *jyotish*, the "science of light," because it throws light upon the future. It is very ancient, and is therefore the source of Western astrology. Only pure devotees, who have transcended the dualities of *paap* and *punya*, sin and piety, good planets and bad, make it to the other side to the everlasting Vaikunthas, the eternal domain "where there is no fear." In the *Bhagavad Gita* Lord Krishna describes all this in different ways to Arjuna on the Battlefield of Kurukshetra. In the eighth chapter, verse six Krishna explains, "Whatever state of being one remembers when he quits his body, that state he will attain without fail." Considering that any moment could be our last, *yoga* advises dedicating this life to training the mind to remember Krishna at all times.

Sitting amongst the farmers and Jain monks, I muse that anyone with a shovel could undoubtedly unearth hundreds more priceless artifacts sitting inches beneath our feet. A young *brahmachari* dressed in the white robes of the *swetambar* sect carefully observes me, without being obvious about it, to see if any leather remains on my person. Then he smiles gently and wordlessly directs me into the deity hall for *darshan*. There within the sanctum sanctorum two deities, marble carvings of Jain saints, sit upon the altar gazing into the void of oneness. The monk identifies the first *murti* as Adinath, which I am grateful to know since all meditating Jain *tirthankars* sitting in the *padma-asana*, or the lotus pose, appear similar due to their doctrine of undifferentiated monism.

I recognize the second 24" standing deity, as Bahubali because he is not sitting. I have visited his original shrine near Mysore at the Hill of Shravanabelagola. It is said that Bahubali stood in meditation unmoving and naked upon that hill for such a time that the vines and creepers of the forest entwined about his legs. There stands today a magnificent statue of him on that hill as tall as a twelve-story building. It is world famous and, despite being a religious shrine, it has become a tourist destination which attracts almost as many foreign tourists as the Taj Mahal. This is the first time I have seen a smaller version of that gigantic deity. The *brahmachari* smiles approvingly as I drop a few coins into the locked *hundi*, the donation box. It is the custom and duty of the layman to make monetary offerings according to his means at the temples he visits for the maintenance of the temple ascetics.

Though I wonder if their extreme degree of self-deprivation is necessary, I do admire the Jain ascetics. As a follower of the gospel of the *Gita*, I have my philosophical differences as discussed. But points of divergence can be saved for later as there is a technique here in the East for friendly discussion, not argument, in a civilized manner.

The Sun has traveled well past his zenith now and will be setting soon. I need a place to sleep in a part of the world where motels and hotels do not exist. Darkness will descend and the night with its snakes, gangs of highway robbers and drunken truck drivers make the road dangerous. My only solution is the *dharmshala*, which literally means "religious shelter." In traditional Indian fashion, the *dharmshala* provides austere lodging and simple vegetarian meals to pilgrims in return for a small donation at places of pilgrimage. The wise motorcycle pilgrim soon learns the locations of holy spots. At each of these *tirtha-bhumis* he may meet charitable souls who offer shelter for the night. A trained eye quickly distinguishes which persons are indeed trustworthy caretakers of the *dham* from the confidence men who prey on simple tourists. In return for shelter, the pilgrim must carefully avoid offenses to holy persons and local citizens. He must abide by local customs, behave in a very grave and respectful manner, as well as pay the expected fees. Often I have been charged as little as ten cents for a night's stay at a *dharmshala*, and thirty cents for a square meal, which was about what I could afford.

Built alongside the temple wall are several small quarters. I delicately broach the subject of staying the night. The *brahmachari* apparently likes me since I have mentioned that I am a vegetarian and have demonstrated respect and friendliness. Yes, he consents, I will be allowed to remain as a guest for the night. I thank him with folded hands, "*Dhanyavad*."

Soon, I excuse myself for some moto-exploration. Now the descending coolness of the evening air provides the most serene moments for local discovery: to observe with wonder those facets of Indian village life that I have already seen a thousand times before, but which never cease to fascinate me. What could be nicer than this? I have no worries about where I will sleep, and before me is an evening ride into the glorious countryside of Madhya Pradesh. Through the open gateway of the temple wall my eye drifts wistfully to the Enfield sitting stoically under the vast maze of a single banyan tree, one that hundreds of years ago arose from a single seed. I reflect momentarily that the bike looks quite at home here in peaceful, eternal India. Unlike cars that protrude and impose upon the landscape, the Royal Enfield motorcycle looks like it was never invented. It seems to have been here just as long as the banyan tree itself which today, at least in my imagination, is forever. One swift kick and the cylinder fires up.

In the sapphire sky, colored by that moment that hangs between night and day, lingers a brilliantly waxing Moon waiting his turn to folIow the western path of the setting Sun. Just below the golden pearl of the Moon is the white diamond of Venus; while above this conjunction is the yellow sapphire of Jupiter. It is the meeting of celestial *brahmanas*: Jupiter or Brihaspati, the priest of the gods; Venus or Shukra the guru of the demons; and Chandra the Moon god, lord of medicinal herbs and supplier of the juice of life. Who could put a price tag upon the blessing of this ride in this place and under these stars? The Moon and his associates are freely spreading their blessings upon everyone, never considering if the recipient is old or young, pious or impious, *shudra* or *brahmana*. This night the evening Gayatri *mantra* will be chanted from behind chrome handlebars.

According to the lunar Vedic calendar, today is *shukla* *chaturthi*, or the fourth day of the waxing Moon in the lunar month of *Shravan-masa* or July-August. As the Moon and his retinue enjoy their descent over the old rolling hills, a timed chorus of frogs arises in unison from the roadside marsh, amplifying with the growing darkness. As I roll back the throttle, my ears delight in hearing the thumper’s beat as it crawls in harmony with twilight's orchestra. The frogs know they are safe from hungry birds, hence their bravery now. The birds have all to their nests for the settled company of their mates. There is no traffic now but for me.

With the turn of the engine; the whir of the chain; the rolling of the tyres touching the Earth; and even the rhythmic song of the mufflers; I feel myself moving in harmony with the stars above. The seven great sages, the *sapta-rishis* of the sky, who reside in the constellation of the Great Bear, lead the other stars by respectfully circumambulating the Pole Star, keeping that fixed and holy point on their right. Now that the cycle of the day has turned to night with the exit of the Sun's chariot in the west, the weather has changed. I am cloaked in the breeze of evening time.

Like night following day, India's six seasons follow one another: *vasant, grishma, varsha, sharad, shishir* and *hemant*. These are life-giving spring, summer, monsoon rains, autumn, early winter and deep winter that comprise one *ayana* or solar year. Next in the grand scheme of time the generational changes of the Earth are measured by Saturn through his thirty year round through the twelve *rashis*, or astrological signs. The Universe, too, has its tour of seasons that are measured in four *yuga* cycles: *satya, treta, dwapara,* and *kali*. These are the ages of gold, silver, copper and iron. We are presently a little over five thousand years into the discordant iron age of *kali*, marked by widespread rancor, which lasts 432,000 years. Each *yuga* lasts for 432,000 years longer than the previous one, so the full cycle equals 432,000 + 864,000 + 1,296,000 + 1,728,000 = 4,320,000 *ayanas* or years. The downward series of numbers which begins and ends the equation 4, 3 and 2 together add up to 9 which is the perfect full number. Following 9 is the number 10, which equals 1 + 0 = 1, starting the numerical cycle over again. And indeed it does, for these *yuga* cycles continue throughout the life of our temporary Universe, which is predominated by the very *nava-grahas* or "nine planetary deities" I am observing as they circle above.

Along the roadside ahead I see a couple of still draped human forms. As the bike draws me through the sapphire night, I can make out that these are two ladies, heads and faces modestly covered by their *saris*. Each woman holds a *lota* of water in her right hand, and each turns her back to my lone procession as I pass. I twist back the throttle just a bit to leave them in the dignity of attending to their evening business undisturbed and covered by the blanket of night. There is no plumbing in these villages. The fear of snakes in the fields and jackals in the forest has sent these ladies over to the roadside.

Apart from my headlamp, one solitary light bulb disturbs the music of the night's descending landscape, and that is the one affixed beacon-like to the temple gate visible from far away. Following that 100-watt beacon like a moth, I find that the Jain *brahmachari* and his village associates are waiting for me by the doorway. This group of new friends, who were so casual about my departure a little over an hour earlier, has stood vigil for my safe return. Putting my faith in the best of human nature, I decide that they must have been worried for my safety on the road alone at night. Bolstered by these good thoughts, I become as happy to see each one as they are to see me. Now that I have placed my trust in them, within their temple compound --for this night at least--I shall be protected from all that is evil in this world.

A quick decision about where confidence should be placed is the acquired skill of a savvy wanderer. In a land that is strange to him, a lone motorcyclist must learn how to tell who is to be trusted--and how far. The price of a foolish and poorly calculated friendship can be a very bitter lesson. Learning this skill is an important means of avoiding catastrophe. Any number of dropped out, would-be worldwide wanderers can be found in America or Europe. They will try to convince you that all Indians are cheats simply because they carelessly chose to befriend some Delhi train station con artist or Bombay predator. To such as these I would advise that he who abandons his homeland must not assume an air of abandon.

The Jain monk chooses this moment to introduce himself. I learn that he is a college graduate from Jabalpur. As several of his friends stand about in a half circle, he tells me of a Jain *muni* or "great thinker," who performed penance here over a thousand years ago. He performed many mystic feats after which the place came to be known as Atikshaya Kshetra, "place of miracles." Worship has been ongoing at this spot for over a thousand years. There was a vast temple complex, already several hundreds of years old, when it was razed by barbaric invaders from Delhi some four centuries ago. No temple stood for a couple of centuries, as long as the Islamic sword of intolerance was held over their heads. With the rise of the British Raj, a new *mandir* arose thanks to more laissez-faire policy. Despite that, this area was home to a number of freedom fighters during the revolution of 1857. Therefore this land also witnessed many harsh reprisals by British forces that burned many of the nearby villages to the ground.

In unpracticed English syllables, the monk beckons me to sit. We find ourselves discussing the spiritual question of personalism versus the absolute realm. His concept is that the individual personality we perceive is merely an illusion and must be transcended. I agree, yet emphasize that above the material ego, which is based upon temporary bodily distinctions, means not to become *shunya*, or devoid of personality. Rather, liberation from matter into spirit means finding the dormant and hidden spiritual identity the eternal super-ego. He feels that a liberated soul is free of duty, whereas I profess that the real activity and function of the entity becomes awakened at the point of true enlightenment. Patience has paid off and now it is right time to convey my point of view.

"Listen, *brahmachariji*," I insist, "I have come here upon my motorcycle, right? It needs two wheels to travel. Similarly, the two personalities, namely the subordinate individual *jivatma* and the worshipful Supreme Lord Who predominates all separate individual entities are a necessary formula in the concept of personal spiritual emancipation. How many unicycles have you seen plying the Damoh Road?"

The monk has never heard of a unicycle, so the example flies over his head. Here on the other side of the world you get used to this. I mean, how much do we Westerners know about their culture, history or religion? We finish up politely enough, yet predictably in the end neither one convinces the other: it is a mere exercise of wits. Yet it is an important exercise because through this dialogue I have shown my new friends a familiarity with their culture. My point having been made, I lead the topic to a more mundane one. I inquire, "Do you see many foreigners here?"

At this point, the monk translates for the benefit of the others who now have now found a way to join our conversation; the previous topic being too esoteric for farmers. They deliberate the issue with great interest among themselves for a few minutes before the monk answers, "Yes, many."

"When was the last one?"

"Not long ago. Maybe six months or a year. Before that we cannot remember." He replies with an air of satisfaction.

With my Enfield as my steed, I have been to many places where a white man has not been seen in recent memory. Yet here my desire to see myself as some Columbus-like trailblazer evaporates. I rationalize that I never liked the courageous brute anyway.

Finally I am allowed to bring my bike into the courtyard. I wash and am offered a little *prasadam*, temple food of rice and dal, which is served upon a plate of dried flat leaves held together by tiny twigs. After dinner, I am shown my quarters. Hospitality, food, and shelter: here in India even those country folks who have little to offer give freely of what they have. And it is amazing just how soundly you can sleep after a day of good-weather motorcycling, for the ride with its sense of relentless forward motion--Enfields like life do not have reverse gears--is carried into your dreams.

I awaken just before sunrise as the head monk is preparing for the first *puja* of the day. The other *ashram munis* have already left for a temple atop a nearby hill, a steep walk of about a mile. A bucket of unheated water and a steel *lota* are offered for my morning ablutions. Bathing takes less than ten minutes the Indian way, by pouring water over my head. Long ago necessity taught me how to bathe in public. The rule is to never become totally undressed even in bathing; instead one deftly substitutes his wet *gumcha* for dry garments simultaneously. After the *arati*, the traditional offering of a *ghee* lamp to the temple deities accompanied by the rhythmic ringing of bells, we partake of an austere breakfast together in silence.

All this brings to mind the dozen years that I too spent as a Vaishnava *brahmachari* in celibate service to the *ashram*. They were the best years of my life, and I am fortunate to have lived them in that way. Yet today though I live apart as a householder, somehow nothing has really changed and only the externals differ. I am still the same soul struggling for inner realization. The *yogi* must remain indifferent to the insensitive gyrations of the world as heat and cold, pain and happiness, wealth and poverty, popularity and solitude pass before him. These by turns touch each one of us, and then go on their way leaving the essence unchanged. Each rising and setting Sun leaves the body a day older and nearer to the appointed time on the calendar though the soul stays as it ever was. But here and now I am a rolling pilgrim in an ancient land, savoring eternal truths on a timeless quest, and on two wheels, too. What more could be asked for? I have lost nothing but have gained everything by the grace of Shri Guru.

The lead ascetic and a few village men, my send-off committee, stand around politely as Old Reliable cranks over on the first kick. While it is still early, I need to make a break, to merge with the road. Even if the temperature stays in the 70's, the Sun will turn brutal after passing the noon zenith. In India it is said that the morning Sun gives energy while the afternoon Sun depletes it. That is why the Indian *pandits* and astrologers refer to the Sun as a *krura* or cruel planet. The other planets of Vedic *jyotish* or astrology are either *shubbhas* (benefics) or *paapis* (malefics). But the Sun has a touch of both, like fire that cooks our food yet burns our fingers if we get too close. Indeed the entire schedule of India is adapted to this solar principal. Most Indians take their midday meal around one o'clock, then rest for an hour or two away from the Sun. But they are not lazy: they make up for it by working late. During the hours of late day when the Sun's rays are cruel, only "mad dogs and Englishmen" will venture out. It is not uncommon to walk into a store in the bazaar during the afternoon only to find the owner stretched out on a straw mat. He knows that shoppers will reappear in the evening hours.

The morning ride is refreshing. With the help of the early Sun's benefic rays, my mind can reflect upon life's secret mysteries...and upon the Hands that pull the strings. The easy rolling motion of the wheels directs the motorcycle pilgrim to see himself in the spokes of the changing wheel of life. I know from experience that moto-meditation will be difficult later when the rays become like flaming needles. When the mind is boiling and on the verge of sunstroke, the ability to calculate split second movements so necessary for road survival will be compromised. Starry solar flashes blur the vision causing blind spots that can hide oncoming vehicles. The instinct for survival demands that the moto-*yogi* guards himself from the torment caused by baking in the afternoon rays. The early start is a good one.

Listening to the purposeful resonation of the engine, I reflect that this bike is built from the inside out and from the rocky road up. It has been created from a noble British pedigree, and is produced today by Indians for India, which pretty much means that it can cover any worldwide condition. The Royal Enfield Company began in the 19thcentury by making cannons and small arms in England. Their first motorcycle appeared just after the turn of the twentieth century, and Royal Enfield--like Norton, Triumph, BSA and Matchless--eventually became a top marque. Royal Enfield motorcycles won many races and supported the British military in two wars, as they support the Indian Army to this day.

The single-cylinder Bullet model that I ride was developed in the late forties, and was built in England until the mid-fifties. Operations eventually wound down in England in 1969, but have continued in India where production was set up to accommodate the Indian military in 1955. At the Royal Enfield factory in Madras (now Chennai), a half-century old technology has been churning out the same bikes year after year. If there ever was an "if it isn't broken, then don't fix it" philosophy in action, then this is it. The Indians have left the design alone, and have only tightened up any weak spots. The India-built Royal Enfield is a neo-classic ideally suited for adventure touring, a trail bike, a sub-continental big cruiser. It is reliable, sturdy, secure and leakproof. Like wisdom, it is a faithful friend on the road. Adventure riders have ridden Enfields from the Arctic Circle to Antarctica, and all the way around the world. In a fine and old tradition, India's military uses the machine in blazing desert sands, and in snowy Himalayan outposts. The Guinness Book of World Records states that the maximum number of people to ride a single motorcycle was the Indian Army at Jabalpur, and they did it balancing on an Enfield. The Royal Enfield is the world's oldest motorcycle in production. My Bullet is one of 600,000 that have been made since 1955, making it the world’s longest-produced model.

I am neither a stunt rider nor a trick rider; simply negotiating the most dangerous roads in the world is daring enough for me. Back in America I have watched with awe as motorcycle racers lean into corners defying every law of nature that the common man holds true, only to lean over to the other side knees scraping the track as they head into the next S-turn. I did try some improvisational mud jumping with the Enfield once and, misjudging my take-off, landed in a shallow monsoon lake. Indians who were not accustomed to a foreigner play Evil Kneivel had gathered around to watch a white man make a fool of himself. When I landed in the water, the Indians turned from a giggling audience to my salvation. To my surprise the Enfield kept humming in two feet of water. By riding the tough clutch hard and with two men wading in to push, I was quickly extricated from the mud. That ended my career as a daredevil.

Many daredevil foreigners do come to India and Nepal to hike the mountain wilderness, bike the Himalayan high roads all the way to Tibet or raft the white waters of the mountains. Inevitably a few self-patronizing friendships with smiling natives begging for green cards are formed. Then it's off to perform the obligatory white man's burden form of charity for a few days. If they survive, and some do not, they've earned feel-good bragging rights back home in the manicured suburbia of the West. Yet that pilgrim who wishes his heart to beat in synch with the very heartbeat of the Motherland of All Culture must take it low and slow. For me this means disappearing into my own practice of Enfield *yoga*. There are mountains to climb other than the the geophysical ones. The mountain of Indian thought looms far above Everest's highest peak. A true pilgrim knows that the secret gifts of the pilgrimage are far greater than anything he can ever repay.

Still, India's roads will eventually bring out the racer and the off-road scrambIer, the mud rider and the dirt tracker, the cocksure adventurer who feels like a winner just by surviving. In tranquil valleys of the idyllic countryside where roads are either hardened mud or have been recently paved by tribal cliques riding is performed as much as with the hands and body as with the breath. This is the trance of *samadhi* steering. But it is when you find yourself on roads that are neither asphalt nor baked brick-hard dirt that a degree of skill is required. Roads that were once paved yet now through neglect have turned into gauntlets of old asphalt stalagmites make the road *yogi* a fierce competitor just as a matter of existence. You are likely to run into heavy traffic at times even on the worst and most remote country roads. At such times the reflexes needed to negotiate the slalom of India's pitiful highways will be all that lies between you and infinity. Falling off the bike is the motorcyclist's greatest fear. For the *yogi*, falling into a lower species of life wherein self-realization is not an option is the greatest fear. The motorcycle *yogi* defeats all fears through his introspective faith in the Yogeshwara.

Much of what was once the road to Damoh has long since disintegrated from merely a pothole infested lane to an endless path of teeth-rattling craters. The primary layer of fist-sized gravel has been beaten back up to the surface. Tire harassing rocks bounce in every direction like aimless window shoppers gawking at some Calcutta bazaar. To make it worse, speed bumps on Indian roads are often all that remains of a gauntlet that is nothing but a series of speed bumps. Mysterious holes dug several feet deep beside the roads are common, sitting patiently like traps-cum-future-gravesites for the unwary two-wheeler. Dunlop tires take the Rubicon challenge well, for the Indian rubber industry has risen to the occasion. Surprisingly, flat tires are relatively rare, and if your tire does deflate, there will always be some local genius around who can patch it.

It is said that that one-quarter of the world's engineers are Indians, and sometimes I think that the lanes of this land must be responsible. The roads themselves are mostly old cow trails that have evolved into by-ways that were next turned into engineering disasters both by design and by neglect. The way in which the citizens that ply these roads negotiate them is another engineering marvel. This is the only country in the world where you can get pedestrian traffic jams, and the only way to face lndia's roads is to adopt an Indian mentality. You can forget whatever concept you’ve carried regarding right from wrong since birth; you are now swimming in a sea of humanity, balancing on two wheels and spinning like a gyroscope. You are steering, rocking, leaning, wiggling, twisting and sliding only inches from thousands of others doing the same. Road inspiration has enlightened hordes of Indian engineers who now congregate in all corners on earth. This is the Road Microcosm reflecting God's Great Macrocosm--the divine universal marvel of galactic engineering brought down to earth for each rider of Hindustan to behold and to contempIate. For the foreigner who somehow still believes in signals, yellow lines that divide traffic, stop signs, highway maintenance, courtesy and a standard body of rules, life will be all too short. In the West everyone can count upon fifteen minutes of fame. However, on these roads it is hard to foresee fifteen minutes of life.

Astronomers theorize that even if different clusters of stars were to pass through each other, there would be little chance of collision. In a nutshell, this is the wonder of the Universe at work, one that reflects India’s roadways most accurately. One lane often equals only the width of a truck, yet the road gets subdivided into as many lanes as dogs, rickshaws, bicycles, mopeds and motorcycles can run side by side, the direction they face being arbitrary. From hidden side streets, vehicles and goats enter the roads without stopping like sudden meteors shooting against the established order. As they merge into the pulsating jet stream of traffic, they are quickly gulped down by infinity. Vehicle contact is actually relatively rare. Through it all, the Indian rider carries upon his face an otherworldly and detached *samadhi*-like expression that is an endearing trademark of this land of *yoga*. As I bite the bullet and sternly gun my Enfield through some traffic snarl, at times it seems to me that any Indian could write the future bestseller *How to Get Nearly Crushed to Death without Changing Your Facial Expression*.

Here the menacing looks and gestures that form the American driving ethic are simply bad manners. In India it is OK to nearly kill your fellow traveler providing you stay calm and poised while doing it. Tolerance is The Way. Like the harsh *tapasya* of ascetic *yogis*, indifference to the sometimes miserable, hot, dusty, smoky, crowded, unkempt conditions of India's roadways awaken higher realizations of man and his place in the cosmos. If highways like America’s were ever to find their way to India, then a hundred years of road *yoga* would go down the drain as the Ayyengars; Chatterjees and Sharmas motor off to their favorite fast food hang outs or drive-through wedding chapels.

How else can you find yourself riding these country roads except by passing through the bazaars? The changing seasons like the cycles of the Moon god above rotate quickly before the motorcycle *yogi*. He must stay in touch with the *atma* as well as the inner piston-propelling force of the engine’s explosions. He navigates through the passing parade of external attraction and repulsion like the alternating cycles of intake and exhaust. Ultimately, whether the ride takes us into a man-made hell hole or through glorious nature in all ten directions--the eight points of the compass plus up and down--the conclusion which we must eventually arrive at is this: all that we see is nothing but the many illusory faces of the goddess of Maya. Where will we be when those faces we hold as real melt into the earth or vaporize into the clouds? Who amongst us has not drawn a ticket and does not stand in the queue? I sneak a peek at my watch--although the overhead Sun, which like time itself never reverses direction--is the best instrument of time in this stageplay of life. It is getting near noon.

We struggle for survival in a world wherein the basic principal of *jiva jiva sa jivanam* reigns supreme. This rule of Nature means that no living entity can survive without eating another entity, moving or non-moving. The superior entity exists only through the unwilling sacrifice of the inferior one. No being invites another to come over and eat him except for cartoon farm animals in ham and chicken commercials on American TV. Living at the top of the food chain like an omnivore for the sake of devouring all those below is no assurance that we will not be next. This is the relative world, Bubba, and for the here and now no one person's perch is absolute, be he the President or the Pope. Neither can we say with smug assurance to which unwilling level of the food chain we will find ourselves assigned in the next cosmic go-round.

Within the spectacular woodlands all about me the animals follow the principle of survival to the letter. Only man parading mere inches from the giant wheels of the juggernaut foolishly thinks that he is invincible. Even the very trees lending us shade in the selflessness of their spreading boughs grow and survive from a very soil whose recipe has been spiced by thousands of bodies, the collective rot of which has nourished the forest over the millennia. The dwellers of the forests say that the trees, too, know when the woodsman is sharpening his axe.

The *Bhagavad Gita* says that this natural beauty is neither false, nor is it an illusion, yet all this is temporary. Lord Krishna informs Arjuna that this world is a personal, yet material, reflection of the original and personal spiritual world. That which is reflected has its origin from that which is original. Without the prototype, the copy could not exist. The reflection or secondary is as real as the original for it too has been generated. However this world of reflections or Maya should not be mistaken to be the primary. He who finds himself bewildered by the reflection should consider seeking out the original, lest he become lost in a continuous maze of reflections of the reflections. The net is never-ending, Bubba. But he who has been empowered with the sword of knowledge to cut through this web of reflective illusions is he who can hope to discover the original. He who is entangled in the image cannot be relied upon as a proper leader to guide us who are confounded by the question of reality versus reflective-reality. Who will lead us to that place which is beyond the forest of illusion? That *yogi* who is qualified is the *guru*, the *nayaka*, the *acharya*, and the spiritual master. He is the self-realized devotee of the Supreme Absolute Truth who becomes the eyes of the struggling seeker. He is both easy and impossible to find. Discovering the qualified *guru* depends entirely upon the willingness of he who would surrender. But a candidate’s first qualification is an intense desire to abandon this material world and that requires detachment from sense gratification.

My *guru* is as deep as the ocean; or rather even the ocean becomes shallow before his munificence. Today I am astride my Enfield in the bliss of motorcycle *yoga* carrying the Name of God only by his grace. We have but little time upon this earth and the foremost mission is the supreme self-knowledge that propels us back to our original position. Achievement of the eternal platform from which rebirth is never a possibility is the true goal of all *yoga*, whether *karma, hatha, ashtanga, jnana*, or motorcycle.

Lord Krishna informs Arjuna that this world is one of many personal yet material reflections of the one personal yet spiritual world. In the One there are many, and many find their refuge in the One. In other words, this material Universe with its finite-in-number and all-too-temporary planets is a reflection of the spiritual Universe with its infinite and unfathomable spiritual planets inhabited by eternally liberated souls. The many who dwell in the liberated state are cognizant of the Supreme One. The unfettered *bhakta*, liberated through his loving service to the Lord, is the *yogi* who has crossed the finish line. He alone knows the Yogeshwara, the Master of all mysticism. This is the final word of the *Bhagavad Gita: yatra yogeshwara krishna*. Liberation awaits the moto-*yogi* who has become a rider in that fuel-enriched school whose all-propelling petrol is the holy name of God.

Soon my occasionally transcendental thoughts are interrupted by a huge shadow blowing from the West. A fast-moving rider, wearing a cinema hero's pencil lead-thin mustache, zigzags around me, glued to his Rajdoot. He indifferently passes within inches of me though the road is empty. Noting that he is drenched, I realize that he is outrunning an approaching storm. As giant drops begin to sacrifice themselves onto the dry earth all around me, tiny wisps of dust rise up from delicate crater-like impressions. Since early monsoon systems can be quite localized, I decide to race the cloud. As the *tap tap tap* of the rain god Indra's handiwork becomes audible, I must make a decision. Then as quickly as it appeared it is over. While the dark blue cloud pours itself into oblivion onto some grateful farmer’s field, I gun the throttle pushing onward.

Another two miles and there appears off to my right an old temple upon an older hillock that has been brightly festooned with banners. The sharp rhythmic beat of the wooden *dholak* drum echoing across the road accompanied by the wail of reed horns is irresistible. The joyous sound of the temple symphony bouncing from hill to hill beckons each villager to attend, and I am not one to refuse an invitation. Today is *naag-paanchami*, the fifth day of Moon in the Vedic month of Shravan, a day when the simple folk perform *puja* to India's most deadly residents, her cobras. As I wend the bike through the maze of unknown lanes like one hypnotized, the sounds of celebration grows louder. The temple sits proudly atop the hill guarding the Damoh Road and its wayfarers as though it has been there since time immemorial. For a deja-vu moment it seems as though I too have always been here: I am transformed from an eternal stranger now home at last by the smiles of the snake worshippers. The bike gets parked beside a few others, mostly beat-up two-strokes and step-throughs.

A large crowd of simple villagers and a few separate groups of tribals huddling on their haunches are watching the show of the *sapera*, a snake charmer. Today is one of the very few days of the year when he can make a little money: this is his window and he knows it. From a demographic perspective, his job is probably among the world's worst career choices. Recently a *National* *Geographic* film crew followed a snake charmer from Govardhan to Delhi. The documentary noted that in the nation's capitol there is hardly enough interest for him to earn his meager two-hour bus fare. India wants to lope along into the 21stCentury and the snake charmer has become the unwitting symbol of all that stands for Third World superstition and backwardness. Not only that, but animal rights activists have made him a target by forming a sort of impromptu serpent liberation front, forcibly seizing snakes from their circular straw baskets. The snake charmers wonder: at least my snake gets to eat, why don’t the do-gooders just go adopt all the stray dogs?

The simple design of the white washed temple suggests that it is a little over one hundred years old. Asking locals a temple's age will inevitably yield the answer, "*Bahut purana, Sahib*" or "It is very old, Sir." So I rely on my crude methods of trying to date them based upon architecture and other clues. Inside there is an older Shiva-*linga* which, having received festive prayers, is adorned with red hibiscus flowers and has been bathed with milk. Upon the black *linga* the temple *brahmana* has drawn three parallel lines with sandalwood paste, the *tilak* of Shiva's followers representing horizontal or material expansion. A *ghee* lamp's five cotton wicks still smolder before the great demi-god showing that the *puja* was completed just moments before my arrival. Ghee-rich smoke from the fire *yagna* still hangs in the air. I feel in my pocket for a rupee note and pull out a fifty. All eyes are on me and I do not wish to appear miserly. Nor do I want to reveal how much money I have, so the fifty is offered beside the incense. I bow down among the lingering prayers of a hundred simple supplicants, and the devotion is heavy.

The *Puranas* tell how once upon a time Lord Shiva dutifully drank some poison that otherwise would have caused the destruction of the world. As the demi-god lifted this deadly potion called *hala-hala* to his lips, some drops fell to the ground. Serpents licked it up and from that time onward became venomous. Therefore snake festivals are always associated with Lord Shiva who sports a cobra, or *naag*, as a garland. During my travels I have also visited a few temples of the snake god himself, like the Vasukinath Mandir at Prayag or the Sangameshwar Temple on the Naag River. I reflect for a moment that today there must be a gala festival at those places, too.

I offer my respects to Shiva who is known by a variety of names. Because Moon god rests as a decoration within his dark locks, he is called Chandrashekhar, "he who keeps the Moon as an ornament." Due to the spot of poison on his otherwise golden throat, he is called Nilkant, "he whose throat is blue." After the Ganges came crashing down onto his head from *swarga* or heaven, he came to be known as Gangadhar. He is considered the "the greatesr of the demigods," or *devatas*, and hence is called Mahadeva. He lives as an ascetic in a remote Himalayan region called Kailash and is known as Kailashpati, "the lord of Kailash." He is Parvatinath, "husband of the goddess whose father is Parvat--the lord of the mountains." With his fiery third eye he is Trinayana. He is Trishuladhar, the carrier of the trident. The great god is the lord, or *ish*, of the serpents is called *Naaga-ish* which combines to form "Naagesh." These very concepts of Shiva-worship quite understandably vexed Christian missionaries, who are said to have designed aspects of their fanciful devil after Lord Shiva. But no amount of foreign coercion could decelerate Hindustan’s love of Lord Shiva.

*Mantras* said, I leave the tiny temple to sit outside among the celebrants. However, mingling is not all that easy in a place where many have never seen a foreigner. A crowd of young men quickly gathers around me grinning and bobbing heads. Uneasily I return their looks trying to show no nervousness, despite my obvious numerical disadvantage. With a sort of warped grin, I fold my hands and say "Namaste." Having had this kind of attention before I am well aware that unfriendly or fearful body language can be very hazardous to one’s health. But this rustic group redefines intimate; they roll up my pants to see what a blonde white man's leg hairs look like. Dozens of inquisitive brown hands glide over my shins. I know that this is not merely good-natured fun, they are daring me to react, and if I don't pass their little acid test I could be ground into snake food. Eventually I rise slowly and back away to the bike, grinning at them as they grin at me. "Grinfest at High Noon" wouldn’t make a great movie, and I don’t want to hang around to experience the ending.

Maybe I have won them over and maybe any one of them at this point would give up his day's meal if were to announce that I am hungry. But on second thought I wouldn't bet on it. Wiser men than me have advised that "maybe means maybe not." Besides I am less of a gambler than a traveling man, and the Enfield beckons.

After making sure that the bike will fire, I shout out my best "Har Har Mahadeva," as my own "Hi Ho Silver." Each person in this all-male crowd responds jubilantly, hands waving in the air, shouting back "Har Har Mahadeva." I wend my way down the maze of paths that lead out of this tiny hilltop village, the name of which I still do not know. Ladies walking along the hard dirt roadway turn their heads away and resist the temptation of even giving a glance to a member of a race they have only heard of. Chastity is the supreme quality of the Hindu woman. The legendary shyness and modesty to which she is heir is the very quality upon which all other facets of Hindu culture and religion rest. It has been rightly said that no object of veneration occupies a more noble position one's mother...and to the saint all women are his mothers.

The remaining hour or so that it takes to get to Damoh proves to be a glorious ride as expected thanks to the early start. Damoh, I will find, is a town of approximately 100,000 people. The biggest industry here is the *bidi*, the cheap, hand-rolled, lung burning, string-tied, horn-shaped, smelly, brown cigarettes that kills millions of addicted peasant men who smoke together in roadside huddles. The town of Damoh is the headquarters for the Damoh District, which is comprised of about five smaller towns and 1400 villages with a total population of around 900,000. In a place such as this, wherein half the people know neither the Hindi nor English alphabet, optometrists make patients read eye charts depicting smaller and larger images.

From Damoh, the middle of nowhere cannot even be seen, yet as I enter the town I find a satisfied self-sufficiency about the place. It moves at its own pace and is nothing more or less than what I had expected it to be. Riding Street to street I realize that when all the Earth's big cities are leveled, Damoh is the sort of place that will always be here, come flood or nuclear holocaust. Bare electric wires dangling from a power pole throw sparks inches above the heads of nonchalant passers-by. Bizarre scooter-powered goods carriers with seven wheels wobble down lanes with bicyclists hanging on the rear for a free ride. Seven men dressed in orange dhotis in imitation of sadhus are marching a huge and noble elephant through town. Here, just like every place in India, lesser beings who walk must fatalistically accept that anyone who has a car "has permission" to jeopardize a pedestrian's life. It is frustrating and useless, yet tempting, to make the natural comparisons: if this America everyone driving would be thrown in jail.

After *darshan*, or "seeing" the deity, at a locally celebrated Shiva temple, I relax upon the *ghera* platform that has been built around a *bael* tree. Bael trees are considered sacred, and ladies seeking longevity for their husbands have circled this one with red strings. Probably the number one prayer of Hindu wives is to never face widowhood, since they fear being swept aside by upcoming generations with no one to look out for them. Those who outlive their husbands often choose--or are forced--to spend their impoverished last days in widows' ashrams of Vrindavana or Varanasi.

For fifteen rupees, about thirty cents, I purchase six bananas and mangoes from the *phal-walla* pushing his wooden cart, and can now enjoy a satisfying lunch. Some locals have been observing me politely, out of the corner of their eyes, though pretending not to. In the big cities like Calcutta strangers will gladly interrupt your lunch to pound and bait you with puerile questions, but here in Madhya Pradesh it is different. These unsophisticated people have manners and dignity.

A Mr. Gupta, an educated man who has patiently waited for me finish my repast, approaches. As we chat, he narrates a very funny episode. Yesterday he had met a group of village pilgrims who had come to Damoh hoping to witness miracle. On the outskirts of town, there is a pair of temples side by side, one for Shiva and the other for his spouse Parvati. The pilgrims had somehow heard that when these two temples merge together, a gala wedding ceremony would be performed for the divine couple to celebrate the miracle. However, they were a little disappointed because what actually occurred was two temple flags had become entwined due to a sudden shift in the wind. The mere combination of the two temple flags had let loose a volley of rumours.

Sitting under the *bael* tree, I notice a small seedling that has struggled to life between cement cracks in the *ghera*. This is a plant sown by the hand of no man; neither does anyone tend to it daily. This plant reaching upward against all hope reminds me of a seedling that my *guru* *maharaja* once pointed to in New York City, growing from the cracks of a Brooklyn sidewalk. He mentioned that denizens of heaven, with their pious assets exhausted, return to Earth to begin their journey again through 8,400,000 species from the bottom up. For the student *yogi*, even the *guru's* casual, off-handed comments are as good as scripture.

Resuming my ride around Damoh, I arrive upon an unusual sight. A group of over two dozen *hijras*, or eunuchs are singing in and around the apartment house of a newborn boy. Superstition says that if they are not adequately compensated, a figure that can set a gainfully employed householder back a two-week's salary, then the baby will bear the eunuchs' curse for life. The *hijras* are one group of entertainers that no one pays to watch, they are paid to leave.

Above the doorways in the narrow lanes, the entrances to apartments bear markings of house blessings: the auspicious red symbols and *yantras* drawn there by pandits with bright red *kum kum* powder mixed with ghee. India is the place on Earth where the *swastika* and the Star of David sit side by side as religious emblems, innocently oblivious of any other connotation beyond their culture. The Star of David to the Hindus is the ancient Lakshmi *yantra*. Like the yin-yang symbol of the Universe, the downward triangle represents the female principle and the upward triangle or *trikona* represents the male energy. Meditation upon this *yantra* carries the thoughtful *yogi* from the material and embodied concept of male-female, to the diverse energies of the spiritual world, ultimately culminating in the Supreme Enjoyer and His Pleasure Potency, Shri Radha-Krishna. The concept of Enjoyer and Enjoyed remains fixed even in absolute transcendence. The *swastika* is drawn by Hindus to invoke all that is auspicious. Unfortunately, it was ripped off from India by the Nazis, and misused as a symbol of hatred and false supremacy.

As red as each *kum-kum* *yantra*, are the splatters of dried *paan* spittle on side doorways. These have been deposited by neighbors visiting the homes of one another. Alternating smells of incense and horse dung, cooked spices and exhaust fumes tantalize and torture the senses. All around me are the usual small town establishments, some with signs that read "Beauty Saloon," "Homely Restaurent," "Departmental Store" or "Meals Hotal."

An overturned pedal *rickshaw*, clumsily overloaded, has caused traffic jam in the very narrow lane. In front of me a nifty econo-box, a Tata Indica, tries to back into me. This particular car has a whining electronic warning system that plays "Jingle Bells." The irony that this is a Hindu country and Christmas was six months ago is apparently lost upon the driver who is trying to run me over in reverse. Noticing that the *rickshaw-wala* has righted his rig, the car stops reversing.

As I maneuver around the car, I somehow recall a cycle *rickshaw* driver I rode with twenty years ago in Lucknow. He was bragging that talent scouts from the Bombay film industry had noticed his daughter's natural abilities, and had offered her film role with some of the day's leading ladies. To tide him over a bit, until scouts called him to Bombay, they gave him five hundred rupees, and took his daughter. The simple fellow had not realized that he had been tricked into selling his own daughter into prostitution and would never see her again.

Soon the time comes to search for a clean hotel. Finding accommodations will not be difficult, but locating a reasonably clean lodging can be real conundrum. The problem lies at the root of India's top-heavy class structure, one that is a snarled little socio-economic problem to unravel. The lowest rung on the ladder is the *shudra* or working caste, yet still lower than they are the *bungis*, the toilet cleaners. No uppercasteman wishes to be known as a toilet cleaner, so rather than clean his hotel facilities himself, the hotel owner hires *bungis* to clean the toilet areas. The *bungis* are a wretched lot, miserably underpaid, and as a result they quite naturally take no pride in their work. Consequently toilets can go far beyond reeking; they can boil your nose with toxic ammonia fumes. In a hot country where almost everything that is drunk down becomes perspiration, any liquids that pass out naturally are rather distilled. Some toilets could actually make you faint from the obnoxious fumes, and at times I've nearly fallen victim to dreaded TDS, toilet death syndrome. Ordinary intelligence would dictate being a little bit more generous toward the *bungis*. At least here and now in the new India of *kali yuga*, it is a place for transcendental philosophy, and not common sense. Yet either one without the other is actually useless.

Finally I find a reasonably decent hotel room with not only a clean toilet but also a strong fan above the bed. Better fans in India rotate 1,000 times a minute and create a wind that is powerful enough to blow away the dreaded malaria-carrying mosquitoes that appear at sunset. The wise traveler soon learns in the interest of survival to discern the cheap fans from the good ones. I have estimated that a mosquito can at bed level enter a wind current created by a fan that rotates up to eight hundred times a minute, but at around one thousand rpm's the frustrated little bloodsucker, try as he might, is blown away.

I once came down with malaria in the northern UP city of Sitapur on a pilgrimage to Naimisharanya. And there I lay burning with a 105 degree fever for a week next to an outdoor garbage pile infested with millions of biting, buzzing insects, where my Hindu host had dumped me. When I would come out of my shakes and delirium to beg squatting, staring peasants to go get me medical help, they would tell me that it was "too far" or "out of their way." "*Bahut dur, Sahib*." The experience cured me of any illusions regarding the hospitality all Indians claim they have, for jewels are not to be found in every mountain, nor in every oyster a pearl.

Eventually the jewels and pearls found me. Vaishnava *sadhus*, holy monks from the Gaudiya Math in Naimisharanya took me in. The doctor they summoned visited me twice daily. Arriving by bicycle, he gave me quinine injections each morning and herbal ayurvedic ("life-knowledge") supplements in the evening. For these fourteen visits plus medicine I was billed ninety rupees which was about ten dollars at that time. After a week, a *sadhu* came in to show me a peacock outside the door with his feathers in full fan. That was his way of telling me that I would recover. A week earlier I had made out from the doctor's Hindi, as he spoke with the *sadhus*, that I might shake my way to my next life. I'll never forget the doctor's look of compassion now that he was telling me with a casual wave of his hand, *bimar hogya*: "Your sickness is gone."

To this day I owe my life to that doctor and the Gaudiya Math *sadhus*, although I remain wary of mosquitoes. If biting bugs come too close, I am merciless. My Jain friends are taught to tolerate them, fearing next-life retribution. I’m more concerned with this life because my tolerance must run out when survival is threatened. I follow that of *yoga* that dictates, "The highest goal is to save yourself, for he who is in a well can do nothing for others trapped with him in the same well."

Looking back, I remember an early introduction to the basic scripture of *yoga*, the *Bhagavad Gita*, some thirty-five years ago. It was the sixties, Haight-Ashbury, and the edition I carried with me was not very carefully translated, hence I drew many incorrect conclusions. I imagined that soon I would be able to discover my own Lordship over the Universe, my Universal Form, when I would become God. It would be not much later that when I would meet Shrila Prabhupada that His Divine Grace advised me to become like Arjuna and work for the satisfaction of the Yogeshwara rather than waste time trying to become the Yogeshwara. He would say, "You are servant of Godhead ever, and master never."

That evening in my hotel room reading those same verses again from *Bhagavad Gita As It Is,* now fixed with a deeper understanding, I doze off under the fan. On the Battlefield of Kurukshetra, at the start of the Great War, Arjuna has just witnessed the Universal Form of Yogeshwara Shri Krishna. Realizing the supreme potency of the Lord of the Worlds, Arjuna begs to be pardoned for any offense he has caused "in friendship or in love." Sleep that night after a long slow ride in the Sun is deep and delicious, the kind you drink in gulps. I have yet to understand the level of those *maha-yogis* who have renounced even sleep, like my own *guru maharaja*. But I am still a novice and cannot even fathom those *yogis* who renounced motorcycles.

The next day I awaken refreshed to the mantric chanting of the Hindi *Ramayana* of Tulsidas. Tulsidas was a pioneer who translated the Sanskrit classic about the life of Lord Rama into Hindi poetry, and thereby opened up the secrets of this great classic to the common man. Truth is one, no matter what language it is conveyed with, and that is why *Bhagavad Gita As It Is* now carries the same message, pure and unadulterated, in dozens of languages worldwide.

The all-auspicious blowing of a temple conch, the sound of which gives flight to ghosts and all other evils, enters my room from some nearby temple. After bathing, I apply coconut oil to my face as protection from the wind on the day's ride ahead. Coconut oil is also my thermometer because it solidifies into a cream at around 20ºC and turns to liquid above that. Hence it's a lazy man's thermometer, one that reads either hot or cool. Sailors used to look forward to India as a land where they would find trees right on the beach that provided both a meal and a drink. And skin lotion, too. The hotel owner, Mr. Agarwal, spots my open door and peeks inside to invite me to his home behind the hotel. He is from the *vaishya* caste, the merchant community and is a *pucca* Hindu. We walk through a garden-like courtyard full of sparrows and enter his drawing room.

Upon the walls of his house hang here and there the obligatory black and white photos of departed relatives. They gaze downward from dusty frames, each with the serious concentration of one who is trying to get the punchline of a joke he cannot understand. Somewhere in India there must be a School of Photography for the Soon-to-be Deceased that teaches the subject how to make this quizzical face. The photographer is taught how to tell a joke with no punchline. Then while the subject struggles with its meaning, he clicks the shutter. This could be the only explanation for millions of photos in homes India with the same quizzical facial expression. The art of mortuary photography means telling a joke that the subject gets only at the last moment of his life. The punch line is that you have struggled so hard your entire life, yet all that is left to recall your contributions to the world is a black and white likeness of yourself gazing down from the wall. Each photo could be captioned "You're next." This is a bad joke written by the goddess of illusion, and is why the moto-*yogi* has a throttle to twist and a credo that states "just ride away." Yet unless the holy name is the vehicle, the joke of no laughter will be repeated *ad infinitum*. Though the demigod of death Yama is patient, he has no dearth of work.

To my pleasant surprise as Mr. Agarwal sits me down, the television goes off. In many homes nowadays the other family members will raise the volume to intrude over your conversation. When I first came to India in the 1970's there was only one channel called Doordarshan, or "that which is exceptional to behold." There was no colour TV nor did I even see a colour television until 1981. At the houses of the rich, thirty or forty members of the working class would huddle quietly on the *seth's* door and peer in through the screen door to see the dim TV. My first glimpse of an Indian TV show was of pudgy Indian actors showing off their overstuffed handsomeness, starchly dressed as cowboys and enjoying a badly-rehearsed saloon brawl in the Wild West. Today, thanks to the ubiquitous television, the final threads of the social fabric of a once great India have been nearly unraveled. Vile Hollywood-style degradation has been invited into every household. Ancient India's refined art, culture, dance and theater have all turned south taking morality down the tubes--yes, pun intended--in the wake of music videos. Many of the films Bollywood recycles on TV are actually thoughtless word-for-word translations of Hollywood movies. Not only that, but a huge portion of the products advertised nationally are actually manufactured by American companies which have displaced many really decent local products. Today many Indian owned companies have been bought out by American corporations, who simply phase out their products in favor of Western brand names. Remember the Lay's potato chip "betcha can't eat just one" ad? You guessed it. Thirty-five years later this ad has finally arrived in India with predictable success.

In the 1970's the Indian government demanded the formula for Coca Cola, a bottle of which cost a rupee and a half at the time. When Coke refused to divulge, the Indian government ordered the shutdown of their factory near Delhi. An indigenous cola--and quite a good one--called Thum's Up was formulated to replace Coke. Today Coke is back and is so omnipresent; it is as it never left. And you guessed it, Thum's Up, which has it's own motto, "I want my thunder", is now owned by Coca-Cola. So whether you want your thunder or not, getting away from the exploitation of first-worldism gets harder with each passing day, even on a motorcycle in Damoh. Not to say that you can't find many really great indigenous products like toothpaste made from the bitter nim tree. Or Chyavan-*prash*, the ayurvedic tonic that can boost vital energy and clear out the lungs. Or *sari bada salsa* which cleans the blood and the urinary tract. From coconut oil to sandalwood soap to *kalmegh* for the liver, India's list of ancient gifts goes on despite the glare of Western commercialism that has blinded the new generation.

Recently a hundred and fifty Michael Jackson "look-alikes"--and thousands of fans--rioted in Delhi when the idol did not show up "as promised," as if anyone had really invited the mega-star. Nonetheless, today young hopefuls in every Indian film imitate the Moonwalker's vulgar dance Crass moves that would make a lambada dance teacher blush have replaced the rich story telling tradition of *Bharata natyam* and *kathak*. Once culturally rich periodicals graced every news stand, but now all those lively and literate journals have all been replaced by TV and film gossip tabloids. Rags that celebrate Bollywood careers that are born with one issue and, like the stars they tattle on, are tossed out before the next month's issue hits the stands. You tell me, will Mungphalli ever marry Mirchi now that his little tryst with socialite Zhareefer Batliwalla been discovered?

The hot milk spiced with cardamom, saffron and ginger served by Agarwal is refreshing. The entire family is pious and beautiful, and even the usual questions become a source of joyful exchange without the distraction of television. I wonder how the Agarwals survive running a hotel in an unheard of place like Damoh. Yet Mr. Agarwal is quite wealthy. In our casual conversation I am surprised to learn that he has a son who has settled in Calgary. When he tells me how smart and honest we Americans are, I realize that their concept of us falls as short of us as ours of them. Not only is India 180 degrees from America in terms of physical location, but everything else is quite the opposite as well. Damoh is not for the Western traveler who wants quasi-Americana waiting for him wherever he gets off the plane. Damoh is for the curious adventurer who prefers to see a place on Earth which least resembles that place he calls home.

Desiring to take leave of the Agarwals while the morning Sun is still offering his abundant energy; I request their kind permission to go. This is the one point that is not spoken, for that would be abrasive and abrupt. This is done with a particular look which conveys that the moment for departure has arrived. Politeness demands that this look must be communicated at the right time, which means when the teacups are empty. For a Westerner, this look is an acquired art, yet an essential one, because it can save you from quite a bit of monotonous routine conversation. After all, a visit to India is not for talking about America, it is for getting away from America. Recently an Indian host wanted to show me a video of his son’s house in Cleveland, and the look saved the day.

I give the nod, and immediately receive the answer. Mr. Agarwal returns the glance that permission to depart is granted, and the family in unison with their head respectfully rises to see me off. All the members of the cultured Agarwal family escort me out to the bike. TV show be damned, each one will stand there until I have left. The guest is God at the Agarwal’s: *atithi devo bhava*. Before me sits the world's most beautiful sight, my Enfield, since it is morning time, the road awaits and I am in the mood to ride.

Waving good-bye to them, the feeling of love I harbor for not only the Agarwal family and their ancestors whose photos had welcomed me, but for a million other honest souls struggling here in this vast strange country, each one according to his own *karma*, is undeniable. Somehow people in India eke out a living against all odds in an environment that makes America's poor appear affluent by comparison. Against all odds the faithful are preserving the ancient heritage of India--that is, Bharata--and extending the world's oldest culture into the future. Many times *pandits* have explained to me that only certain souls with the asset of past-life piety--*purva-punya phal*--may be born here. Only through an inborn and inbred devotion to God is the eternal religion of *sanatan dharma* preserved in Vedic India. Toil he must, yet according to his fate the religious Indian stoically faces his due suffering his accepts his reward. God alone decides the result, and His final edict is recognized unquestioningly. Besides, whatever happens here is temporary anyway, and in the future a better place awaits the faithful.

Riding through the bazaars of Damoh, the city seems much the same as the one that welcomed me the previous evening. Except it is the early morning now and the sweepers are expected to single-handedly push back the tsunami tide of rubbish that has been thrown down by insensitive hands. On my left a frail old lady sweeper--whose father was a sweeper and whose children and grandchildren will all die as sweepers--pushes with her straw *jharu* several layers of garbage into a huge mountain. Young men with nothing to do stand indifferently chewing *paan*.

Around town I notice a couple of old missionary schools. After independence the British went back to their foggy islands leaving the work of conversion to the converts. The Christian missionary's job was to subvert the Hindus from the bottom by telling the sweepers and other "*dalits*" that if they were to renovate their religious orientation, then they would magically become "equal." They were taught that since "man is the prince of all creatures" that other creatures are placed here for his enjoyment, which was a way of telling them that it was actually all right to overstep Hinduism’s final taboo of never eating beef. However, after conversion, many sweepers were disappointed to find that they were expected to have their own Dalit Christian sweeper's church once the foreigners left. Now there are many churches for "lower caste Christians" that higher caste converts simply will not attend. And neither are the sweeper converts welcomed at the churches--or the cemeteries--of upper class Christians. Old habits die hard, or not at all, here in of caste by birth.

The ingenious methods used to convert the lower castes to the Way of the Lamb are another story. Missionaries would invite unsophisticated and illiterate tribals to a big picnic, taking them along in the rear of a truck. In those days many of them had never been in a truck and were understandably unfamiliar with its workings. Half way to the picnic, the driver would turn off the engine. Trucks in those days had a button on the dash that would turn the engine over, which could not ignite unless the key was also on. As the driver pushed the button with the key in off position, the guests were asked to chant "Shiva, Shiva" or "Krishna, Krishna". You guessed it, Bubba, the truck just wouldn't start. However when the *dalits* were asked to sing out "Jesus, Jesus", lo and behold the engine would fire up quite miraculously as the driver remembered to turn the key.

Personally, I do not bewail the sweeper lady's fate for the simple that she doesn't either. In fact in a country where there are countless who are much poorer than her, she feels lucky to have a job. Westerners who attack the caste system forget that the West has its own caste system that is as rigid and harsh as that which is seen in India. You who are reading these words know many things that I do not, and therefore I cannot be equal to you.

The analogous body of the social order described in the Vedic literature likens the intellectual to the head; the soldier to the arms; the businessman to the belly; and the worker to the feet. Known properly as *varnashram dharma*, the caste system in its unpolluted form was actually a very good way to organize society according to individual merits. Caste by birth as seen today is actually a perverted reflection of the original. *Varnashram* was based fully upon individual merit rather than your father's occupation. In Vedic India members of lower castes could overcome social boundaries and become elevated to higher levels. There is story of a lad who wished to become a *brahmana* disciple of a sage. The *guru* asked him, "What is your caste, my son?"

The boy answered, "I do not know."

"Then tell me about your father."

"Sir, my mother was a prostitute and I do not know my father's identity."

"Well then, your father must be a *brahmana* because the quality of a *brahmana* is truthfulness, and you are truthful. I will accept you as my disciple."

Corrupt social leaders--or shall we call them call them "misleaders"--have imposed today's rigid and seemingly irreversible system of caste-by-birth. All over the world you will find teachers (*brahmanas*), soldiers (*kshatriyas*), businessmen (*vaishyas*), and workers (*shudras*); each position based upon quality of work and not upon birth. Arguments against Indian society's corrupt caste-by-birth system are very logical. Consider the following example. Say you are on a plane, and you see the pilot drinking and socializing with the stewardesses.

"Who’s flying the jet?" you ask.

You are told, "Nothing to fear, the pilot's son is in the cockpit."

"Is he a licensed pilot?" you ask.

You can’t believe your ears when you hear, "Sir, he may have no training but the son of a pilot must also be a pilot."

You now know that soon the plane you are on will be out of control, just as India has flown out of control due to the false imposition of the caste-by-birth. Neither is the son of a *brahmana* qualified to be a *brahmana* simply on the basis of birth. In the Vedic Age, even great kings had no reservations about removing their crowns and kneeling before the true scion of the first order, the *brahmana*.

Today too many so-called *brahmanas* are nothing more than useless descendants of a once noble line. The fallen position of the modern *brahmana* in India has been made abundantly clear to me on a thousand occasions, but two incidents stand mind. It is for these reasons of social corruption that great reformers like Guru Nanak sought to eliminate casteism. My *guru maharaj* took up an even greater task: the re-establishment of a once-noble society based quality and not birth. With this in mind in April of 1969 he initiated me into the order of *brahmanas* with strict orders to walk the walk before talking the talk.

While the lowest caste is called untouchable, some *brahmanas* wrongly consider themselves untouchable due to being above reproach. The tragic fact is that unfit *brahmanas* have only made recognition that much harder for India’s many worshipful and noble *brahmanas* who to this day carry the torch of *dharma* onward against all odds. That visitor to India who gets the rare association of a genuine *brahmana* should consider himself blessed. India's true *brahmanas* would like to see the demise of the corrupt ones more than anyone else. They understand that a condemnation of a warped social order in no way impugns them. The baby should not be thrown out with the bath water.

Because I have not come here to change anything but myself, I have finally acquired the knack of survival in this difficult country. Unlike the moralist who seeks to superimpose upon a foreign landscape a standard tailored for another time and clime, I have no dreams about fixing India. An Indian journalist once quipped that if he were to become Prime Minister, his first act would be to commit suicide. I am nothing more than an atomic spirit soul begging for mercy at the feet of my *guru maharaj*. The monsoon will not follow autumn without winter, spring and summer in between. If there is any lesson to be imbibed from the practice of *motorcycle yoga*, it is that as riders of our own roads of destiny, we are merely insignificant.

What will certainly come to pass in the future is that the world’s thinkers will realize the uselessness of divisive and dogmatic belief systems that do not awaken love of God. The true conception of religion is non-sectarian and universal because the Supreme Lord is one without a second. He is the father of all living entities. In this age of *kali* Lord Chaitanya Mahaprabhu has decreed that the only way to achieve the ultimate goal is to reject divisiveness and embrace the holy name of the Lord. The humble sweeper lady has inspired a spiral of thought, as the road continues.

As the Enfield takes me past the cloth bazaars, I move onward through the lanes where ladies come to buy grains from Sindhi merchants. Each housewife knows that Lakshmi, the Goddess of Fortune, famous for her fickleness, will not stay in the house where grains are not stored. The young wives are gorgeous in their colorful saris and bangles. The women of India in their piety and chastity are among the world’s greatest beauties. And, though a *yogi* is sworn to remain detached from the beauties of this world, beauty is a subject that *yoga* does not shy away from. From the *bhakta’s* point of the view, all opulence belongs to the Bhagavan, the Supreme Lord. Therefore, beauty belongs to Him as well. Hence, beauty is not false, though these attractive, elusive baubles that dazzle us from day to day are temporary. Anyone who has been granted great beauty of form by the laws of *karma* has earned the privilege due to past piety. But beauty of this world is a temporary reflection of the eternal beauty of He Who owns all beauty, wealth, strength, knowledge, fame and renunciation. Therefore, in this world of temporary treasures, one’s best hope is to be a temporary caretaker of a reflection. *Yoga* means to affix the eye upon the Eternal Beauty while remaining detached from that beauty which will suffer decay and death. Temporary beauty is as dazzling as a burst of lightning in the sky, stunning for an instant and then gone. That which is beautiful here only serves to remind the true *yogi* of the eternal splendor of Krishna.

I end up at the place where items for worship or *puja* are sold; incense, brass lamps, brass bells, copper *yantras*, Deity garments and the like. As in the rest of India, merchants in Damoh's bazaar’s cluster together. Some of the things that they sell I can't fathom, yet it will all fall into place when I find the right person to ask. Sometimes finding where to begin inquiry is the biggest challenge, for the ability to unlock even open secrets is the skill of but a few, and even fewer can communicate the obvious. When finding where to begin the inquiry becomes the biggest challenge, I simply turn the throttle, for there will be more brain teasers around the next corner. Somehow India will always offer more questions than explanations.

As I enter narrow lanes hardly six feet wide, no one gives me a second glance. Every day is laissez-faire day here in the heartland of the world's strangest country. Passing old temples that have stood where they now stand since before roads were built, it is obvious that in their obstinate dignity they have forced the tiny by-ways to be carved around them. Skinny boys perched upon 125cc Hero-Hondas or 100cc Bajaj-Kawasakis whiz around me from all sides. Those that zoom headlong towards me with a driver and two passengers appear for a moment like young, three-headed motorcycle demigods.

## 5. The Devi Road

## "The Eightfold Path on Highway Seven"

TEMPLES OF THE MOTHER GODDESS DURGA DEVI are found in every part of India. The goddess is worshipped upon remote hilltops and on peaceful riverbanks; within wild forests and in crowded bazaars that have grown up around her shrines. She is Vindhyavasini, "goddess of the Vindhya Hills"; Trinayani, "the three-eyed"; Banjari, "the forest-dweller" and Kanya Kumari, the "virgin goddess" of the three oceans.

Generally she is worshipped by materialists who regard the Mother Goddess the same way a child looks to his mother, as an order supplier. Hence her temples are very popular. Another explanation of why there are so many temples to her is told in the *Bhagavat Purana*. Once upon a time, while Lord Shiva meditated in his Himalayan abode, his wife Sati, who is none other than the goddess herself, observed that the *devatas* were flying overhead. The demigods were on their way to celebrate a gala festival--a *yagna*--in the foothills alongside the roaring Ganges. The sponsor of this grand religious affair was none other than Sati's father Daksha. Sati pleaded with her husband Shiva, master of the *yogis*, "My Lord, it is natural for a daughter to miss her family. Please do me the honor of escorting me to my father's gala festival." But Lord Shiva, recalling the misbehaviour of his father-in-law at an earlier *yagna*, admonished, "One should not go to anyone’s house, even on the consideration of his being a relative or a friend, when the host is disturbed in his mind and looks upon the guest with raised eyebrows and angry eyes." (*Shrimad Bhagavatam* 4.3.18)

Sati's father Daksha was narrow-minded and could not appreciate the greatness of Shiva. Although Sati knew that her husband would never consider attending a function where he would be disrespected, her mind was perturbed by material attachment and anger. Material attachment is quite difficult to transcend. She left her husband’s side for the *yagna* riding upon Shiva’s bull carrier and attended by thousands of his followers who played celestial instruments along the way.

When Sati arrived at the sacrificial site in Kankhal she was ignored by her envious father Daksha who had also failed to set aside any oblations for Lord Shiva. Therefore in a fit of rage she declared, "I shall no longer bear this unworthy body, which has been received from you, who are blasphemous of Lord Shiva. If someone has taken food which is poisonous, the best treatment is to vomit." (SB 4.4.18) Then, in the presence of Daksha, her mother and her sisters, as well as all the demi-gods and sages assembled there, Sati assumed a meditative posture. Moments later an all-consuming fire sprang from her extremities. As her body was reduced to ashes in an instant, Sati's spiritual essence, her *atma*, flew off to become reborn as Parvati, daughter of the god of the mountain god Himavat.

Through his mystic vision, Shiva, who is known as Trinayana or "three-eyed," witnessed all that had taken place. He called for his bull carrier Nandi. Soon he appeared at the site of the *yagna* accompanied by a terrible entourage of ghouls and goblins, laughing hysterically like one gone mad. Lord Shiva’s dark associates set about destroying the *yagna-shala*, cursing the priests and beheading Daksha.

Other *Puranas* (beside the *Bhagavat*) describe what happened next. Lamenting, Lord Shiva tenderly picked up the charred remains of his beloved Sati. The Supreme Lord Vishnu, the Enjoyer of sacrifice, appeared at that time and asked Shiva why he was lamenting for a body from which the soul had departed. Lord Vishnu then sent His razor sharp *chakra* slicing Sati's lifeless body into many pieces. These body parts fell at various places throughout the Indian sub-continent and became the fifty-one *shakti-piths*, or "places sacred to the goddess of prowess and energy." Eventually temples of Devi worship would rise each site.

At the topmost *shakti-pith* of Mathura, where the hair of the goddess fell to earth, she is called Pataleshwari. At Naimisharanya, the center of the Universe, Sati's heart fell and there she is adored as Lalita. In Guwahati, Assam, her private area fell to earth. The river turns red there for three days each year, and she is worshipped there in a great temple as Kamakshi Devi, "fulfiller of desires." Four toes of her left foot fell to Earth at Calcutta where she is revered as Kali, the "black" goddess. Her earrings landed at Manikarnika Ghat on the banks of the Ganges at Varanasi where she is worshiped as Vishalakshi. The Devi’s tongue became the shooting flame of fire called Jwalamukhi, which springs from the snow-bound slopes of Himachal. At Ujjain the goddess' elbow is worshipped as Harsiddhi, "the power of Shiva or Hara." There she is adored as the benefactress of godly emperors, like King Vikramaditya from whose reign the Indian year is measured. At Puri the goddess' navel fell where her devotees revere her as Vimala, or "faultless." On a hill above Pushkar Lake in Rajastan the Devi's wrists fell to Earth where her devotees adore her as Gayatri, "deliverer from sin." At Prayag, one of the five great holy places, the fingers from both hands of the goddess fell to Earth beside the *triveni-sangam*, where three holy rivers meet.

Some of these fifty-one *shakti-piths* are very well known. For example, each year millions visit the temple at Kali Ghat, which gave its name to Calcutta. Others have been neglected. Still others have been altogether forgotten, awaiting re-discovery. Many of them are in Nepal and Shri Lanka whereas those that lie in Pakistan and Bangladesh were lost to the partition of India. The locations of some are vague, while others are sometimes claimed at two or more sites. *Shakti-piths* mark both ends of National Hwy 7, the Devi Road, which runs from Varanasi, to world’s oldest city, to Kanya Kumari where three oceans meet.

Two hundred miles north of Jabalpur, in the heart of India and alongside the Devi Road is the temple of Sharada Devi at Maihar. Maihar is translated locally as "the Mother’s necklace," indicating this is where Sati’s necklace fell to Earth. Goddess Sharada's popularity has grown in recent years and her magnetic pull can be felt in all parts of middle India. Indisputably, the heart of this land certainly belongs to Sharada Devi.

Noticing the portrait or deity of Goddess Sharada in hundreds of homes, places of businesses and temples I visited around Central India, I developed a desire to pilgrimage to her hilltop abode. One day in early *vasant*, the spring season, and a day that the stars shone favorably, I cranked up the Enfield, destination Maihar. By the Hindu system of *jyotish* or astrology, I was born under the earthy feminine sign of Kanya *rashi*, Moon sign Virgo, since Indian astrology lays stress upon the Moon sign and not the Sun. Kanya, or Virgo, is said to be the same sign that governs India. Ruled by Mercury, we travel-loving Virgos prefer not to take the direct route to any destination, whether physical or mental. Instead our choice will often be an out-of-the way route that provides fertile ground for thought and discovery. Thus I have charted the Virgo's way, a short two day's trip visiting other temples and friends along the way.

Sun rays shoot from behind the Vindhyachal peaks as I turn north onto to Devi Road. On my right a crow, stolen berry within his beak, swallows and caws twice. A young housewife holding a full pot of yogurt between her hands, cautiously checks the traffic from every direction, then darts in front of me. These omens tell me that the trip will be a successful one as morning smells of cooking, of *puja* and of a world greeting a new day invigorate the ride.

Not ten miles beyond the Chungi Choki crossroads, I am forced to slow way down to almost to a crawl. Road work ahead, a stretch of National Hwy 7 is being paved for the hundredth time by tribal men and women. Workers queue up in a semi-circular line scooping up rocks that have earlier been dumped there. They carry these rocks on their heads to the newly-graded roadway, hurling down their load with an effective twirling motion. Each worker is paid about Rs.45 daily. Mothers set their babies on the rock pile while they toil, until the little ones grow hungry and need to be nursed. Doubtless each of these road builders likewise slept upon gravel in their own infancy. It is upon the shoulders of these *adi-vasis*, the so-called "backward class," that rests the responsibility of knitting a sub-continent together with a maze of asphalt. How they arrive at their job is another story.

In the morning a huge tractor made by either HMT Hindustan Machine Tools or Eicher (which now owns Enfield India) ambles through fields and dirt tracks into the jungle to fetch several generations of able-bodied men and women. About thirty can load into the trailer behind the tractor providing they all stand, and in this way they are brought to the site. By the same means the *adi-vasis* are returned to the jungle in the evening. It is this plodding, never-ending method of road construction that accounts for the ever changing conditions upon a single stretch of highway. Few of these dark-skinned tribals own even bicycles, hence their hand crafted highways are meant for an India that is entirely different from the one they know.

The din and chaos of the roadwork test the limits of my patience in the hot Sun, but then India is nothing but a series of tests. Whosoever enjoys India must be the type of person who enjoys being tested. With a few daredevil moves that the average scootering schoolgirl would consider ordinary, I slalom through the dusty roadwork in less than half an hour. Soon the wind created by the speed of the bike is drying drenched cotton garments. An hour later later, I turn east onto the unpaved countryside road to Majholi, a village that lies ten miles off the Devi Road. Standing sociably by the roadside a pair of red foxes spot me and, turning quickly, disappear into the tall grasses. In a tree above me female black-faced Hanuman monkeys care for a young member by turns. Soon I enter the village.

Majholi has grown around an extremely rare temple of Vishnu Varaha, the "boar incarnation of Godhead." Sometimes the Lord is impelled to take form of one of the species of His own creation. Once He became a turtle named Kurma Avatar, and on another occasion He became a fish named Matsya Avatar. How He decided to descend in His boar *avatar*, as Lord Vishnu Varaha, is described in many of the *Puranas*.

Many, many years ago, due to the exploitation of the Earth by the demon Hiranyaksha, the world fell into the nether regions at the bottom of the Universe. Only Shri Vishnu, "the all-pervasive," was capable of uplifting the Earth from the accumulated muck. Yet how could Lord Jagadisha, Lord of the Universe, be expected to retrieve the planet from such an impure place? The solution was found when Lord Vishnu Himself manifested His divine form as Varaha, the boar incarnation. After slaying the demon Hiranyaksha, "golden-eyed," in a pitched battle lasting many years, the Lord Boar went all the way down to Patala-*loka* and lifted up the Earth by supporting it upon tusks.

The huge stone *murti* worshipped in Majholi is said to be self-manifested. Locals tell the story of a mysterious wild boar that used to run through the fields here hundreds of years ago. The farmers were upset because the rampaging boar was ruining their crops. Not long after that a fisherman found a small stone Deity in the form of a boar in a nearby lake, which he took home. However, this peculiar Deity soon began to grow by His own will. Eventually Lord Vishnu Varaha reached His present immovable size weighing several tonnes. Locals hailed the miracle by building a temple around Him. As the village of Majholi grew up around the temple, a place of pilgrimage was born.

Through unpaved dusty lanes the bike carries me to the temple of Lord Vishnu Varaha. Fortune is with me and I have arrived at the time of the ringing of the *aratik* bells and clanging of gongs that are calling all nearby devotees to the noontime service. Rushing inside I listen meditatively as village ladies sing while the white robed pandit conducts the service. I try not to be surprised that the Deity, which would appear as a mere stone statue to the uninitiated, emanates a feeling of joy and victory which surrounds all present. Although in the form of a boar, He is worshiped here as none other than the Lord Vishnu, Supreme Personality of Godhead, the Ocean of bliss, and shelter of His devotees.

When the ceremony ends, I am pleasantly surprised that Panditji, the *pujari* or priest, remembers me from an earlier visit. I am honored by the offer of lunch with his family. At his modest house nearby, we sit upon the cement floor, eating the standard fare of *dal-subji-roti*--soup, vegetables and *chapattis*--on *patals* or disposable plates made of leaves. As I take my last bite, fully satisfied, I notice that Panditji, a perfect gentleman, has timed the finishing of his meal with mine. It is through little details like this that Hindu hosts--those who still believe in their *dharma*--honor their culture which dictates *atithi devo bhava*: "the guest is God." After dinner, outside, his youngest daughter pours water over my hands on instruction from her mother, and I do the customary mouth rinsing.

It is early afternoon now and most villagers will rest for an hour or two. The Devi Road is calling so I turn down Panditji's invitation to rest upon the porch. I steer into the direction of the town of Sihora that has been built around an extinct volcano, and upon this volcano is a temple to the goddess Jwalamukhi, the "fire-tongued." The temple has closed for the afternoon so that the deity can rest. To honor the goddess of the volcano, I simply bow my head in the direction of the *mandir* as I slowly roll by.

A few miles north of Sihora I rejoin National Hwy 7, the Devi Road. Just ahead an excited procession catches my eye. Beside the road is a man rolling over and over slowly heading northbound, smiling beatifically in the dust. Beside him a middle-aged woman is wheeling a cart upon which an altar of pictures to the goddess, along with incense and flowers, has been placed. On the altar cart sits an old lady, likely his mother. Suddenly I remember an article in the *Jabalpur Chronicle* that I chanced to notice a few weeks earlier. This rolling man is from Indore some three hundred miles to the southwest. He had taken a vow, for what reason I do not recall, to roll along the roadway the entire distance from Indore to the doorstep of Goddess Sharada at Maihar. Some six or seven months of rolling down this highway are behind him, yet this pilgrim has before him at least two months until he reaches the lotus feet of the Mother of the Universe. His interpretation of rolling down the road to the door of Devi is different than mine because my rolling two wheels have but two hours or so before they reach Maihar. But then in the world of *yoga* there is also room for personal interpretation because each soul is unique. Each entity is singular in the quest for liberation. Achieving transcendence is an individual endeavor since the element of personality remains in the state of the soul freed from matter. God can grant any desire; therefore the supplicant must carefully consider those things for which he asks.

Placing the Enfield on its side stand, I walk over to the procession buzzing about the rolling man. He has achieved a celebrity status. I cannot hide my curiosity mixed with admiration for his crazy austerity. As I make a small offering to his altar to help his entourage with their daily expenses, I am surprised that he looks up to smile at me blissfully, a smile that says that he is doing exactly what he has chosen to do. Sometimes in the vast, great, austere and strange world that is India all you can do is step back with wonder. Certainly this man and the people walking with him are doing something only few men have ever done. Yet would the goddess have been any less pleased if, say, he had gone by bus or had just hopped upon a motorcycle? The answer does not lie with me. Great austerities aimed at material advancement are a waste of time while small sacrifices aimed at loving God are never lost. We are each motivated by whatever we hold dear, and with very little time at hand we must decide upon finding that which is most valuable. As far as I am concerned, the search must end with *bhakti* or it is a wasted endeavor.

Half an hour beyond the rolling man I find my faithful wheels rolling through Sleemanabad, a town with a very unusual history. Known as Kohka until the mid-1800's, Sleemanabad is one of the few places in today's India named for a European. Col. William Sleeman (later Maj. Gen Sir WH Sleeman) was a British officer stationed in Jabalpur in the 1830's. During that time there was a gang of murderous dacoits, called *thuggees*, active in Jabalpur District. In fact unchallenged *thuggee* gangs had been waylaying unsuspecting wayfarers for several centuries. They were past masters of deception; they would cook and entertain pilgrims, yet when the leader called "bring the tobacco" the end was near. Each dacoit would then whip out a yellow scarf weighted with a silver coin and slowly strangle his chosen victim to death. These *thuggees*, or *pindaris* as they called themselves, surrounded their blood-thirst for death with a concocted form of goddess worship. The *thuggees* were, of course, no more devoted to the goddess--the Mother of all life-- than, say, American Satanists that perform ritual orgies and blood sacrifices before a cross are devoted to Christ.

The *thuggees* were an entrenched problem that when Col. Sleeman was appointed District Collector on behalf of Her Majesty's government. The colonel took strong measures to root out the *jindari* cult to the extent of even having his own spies infiltrate their inner circle. The novel by Calcutta-born John Masters--and later Ivory-Merchant movie, *The* *Deceivers*--was loosely based upon the life of Col. Sleeman who was played by Pierce Brosnan. In his day Col. Sleeman was hailed as a genuine hero not only by the British, but by the Hindus as well. Over 3600 thuggees, many of whom had murdered hundreds, were arrested through his efforts. Of these nearly 500 were executed. One tree where many were hanged still grows in Jabalpur, and another in Sleemanabad. But Sleeman reserved the most brutal form of punishment for the most vicious of the gang. He had their skulls crushed like an eggshell under the foot of an elephant while the public looked on in satisfaction and in horror. Col. Sleeman wrote several books on the *thuggees* and spoke seven languages including the *thuggees'* secret Ramasi dialect.

Sanskrit lore offers scriptural proof that Kali cannot be supplicated by murderous scoundrels. Rather, the black goddess is the enemy of evil as seen by the narration of her history from the *Markendeya Purana*. Once there was a demon named Raktabija, or "blood-seed," who received the boon that should any drop of his blood touch the ground, then that drop would arise as a duplicate of himself. In this way Raktabija, feeling that he had become invincible, set about waging war against the demi-gods. As the arrows and spears hurled by the gods against Raktabija pierced his body, drops of his blood only served to create a new army of Raktabija duplicates, each as fierce as the original. Goddess Kali, "the black," then appeared as an expansion of Goddess Durga, who in turn is an expansion of Shiva's wife Parvati. In order to stop the attacks of Raktabija, she was forced to drink his blood before it hit the ground. In this way the black goddess wiped out the army of thousands of duplicate Raktabijas, furious at his audacity in challenging the *devatas*.

Still her rampage continued. In order to pacify the anger of this incarnation of the Mother of the Universe, Lord Shiva lay down before her. As quite by accident she placed her foot upon the chest of Mahadeva, her tongue protruded from her mouth in horror and atonement. Raktabija had been defeated, and now Kali too became pacified. Peace again reigned in *swarga*, home of the demi-gods.

From the history of the appearance of Mother Kali, we can understand that the black goddess has no interest in the death of innocent people. Neither is she interested in the offerings of goats that are "sacrificed" at some of her temples, for Kali Maa herself is a vegetarian. It would be unjust therefore to hold the Hindu world responsible for the concocted and fanciful exploits of a gang of thugs, even those who try to disguise their brutality behind a veil of religion. Col. Sleeman actually made offerings at a temple of the goddess near present-day Sleemanabad, after which his French Mauritian wife was blessed with a son, whom the couple named Arthur. It was after the birth of Arthur on the sixth day of January 1833; that he gave land for the upkeep of the temple, and the town of Kokha was renamed Sleemanabad. Descendants of Gen. Sleeman have visited Sleemanabad and likewise have been blessed with sons. Today, in the goddess temple here in Sleemanabad, an "eternal flame" remains lit in the honor of a British civil servant who did so much for the people of India.

A crowd of laughing village school children attracts my attention near the highway. Curious, and hardly noticed, I make my way into their circle. The afternoon ride from Sleemanabad to Maihar, though fatiguing and hot, is peaceful--even blissful--and blessedly uneventful. True *yogis* can be recognized through their simple and clean appearance, cultivated through temperance, sense control and austerity. Therefore at religious gatherings or *melas* the imitators are easily spotted like crows among swans. The poser *yogis* do not observe any of the prescribed restrictions, nor do they practice the gentle tolerance of the followers of *ashtanga yoga*, the eight-fold path.

Defeated, the black *yogi* accompanies me outside. I now find him truly despicable, the opposite of everything that has ever attracted me to India. Yet I hide my disgust and still remain polite. Accustomed to a television-dominated society in which virtually everyone, including the pampered household pet, belongs to varying degrees of the middle classes, the first-time Western visitor becomes astounded by the extremes he witnesses. ## 6. The Discovery of Parashurama Kund

## "The Avatara and the Axe"

THE ARYAN INVASION THEORY imagines that India’s lighter skinned Northerners originated from around Europe. It alleges that an entire civilization migrated en masse and completely displaced the earlier Dravidian culture driving it south, leaving no trace from whence it had come. Borrowing a word from Sanskrit, the indologists called these imaginary new arrivals "Aryans" pitting them against the supposedly re-located Dravidians. The colonialists thereby nailed a wedge between India’s north and south in a brilliant politically-motivated move of divide and conquer. There are several flaws with this fantastic theory. First, no civilization in history has completely transplanted itself leaving behind no trace of its origins. Second, whenever mass migrations have occurred, re-settlement always resulted in a blending of cultures, and not a wholesale displacement. Supposed evidence for the Aryan invasion is a linguistic one, which traces similarities in language between India’s Sanskrit and many of the so-called Indo-European languages.

Vedic heritage offers a reverse explanation for parallels in language. The European civilization actually has descended from a Vedic origin long before relatively modern names like "India" or "Hindu" were coined. According to that point of view, there was an exodus of the warrior class from to Europe from India, when the land was called Bharat, many, many thousands of years ago. This explains not only root similarities in Indian and European languages, but also demonstrates the parallels in the so-called "Pagan" culture of the pre-Christian era and what is called "Hinduism" today.

The catalyst for the exodus from India was an *avatar* of the Personality of Godhead Lord Vishnu named Parashurama. *Parashu* means axe. So fierce and adept with his axe was he that he successfully uprooted twenty-one generations of *kshatriyas* or warriors. Those warriors who did not meet their end in battle escaped and created the great empires of the European and Mediterranean regions from Egypt to Skandanavia. That is why essentially the same deities as in Hindudom were worshipped under different names in the near East, Europe and Northern Africa.

There are a few temples to Lord Parashurama in various places of India where he is worshipped with the understanding that since God is complete, the sum of total of the Absolute must also include killing. A god who is simply love and kindness personified cannot be complete, and therefore such an incomplete and one-sided personality could not be the Supreme Lord by the very definition of God.

I was first made aware of this revelation in a lecture by my *guru maharaja* in 1971. Yet he admonished that even when killing is necessary, as in the case of defending a country from marauding invaders, still killing is not good. Therefore, Lord Parashurama himself underwent penance and austerity to counteract his "sin" of killing, even though there can be no such thing as sin associated with the Lord and His incarnations.

Later I would find that the *Puranas* explain how Lord Parashurama underwent his austerities or *tapasya* in the Vindhya Hills, a low mountain range of Central India that roughly follows the Narmada. Therefore, whenever I was to visit this region, it became my habit to inquire after the location of his *tapo-bhumi*. The story of Lord Parashurama is told in the *Shrimad Bhagavatam*.

Once, millions of years ago in the age known as Treta Yuga, the great sage Richika approached Varuna, lord of the waters, and obtained one thousand horses, each as lustrous as moonlight and each one having one black ear. Richika Muni presented the horses to the great King Gadhi as a dowry for his daughter, the beautiful and qualified princess Satyavati.

After the marriage, Satyavati naturally wanted to give her husband a son, so with prayers and *mantras* Richika Muni created an oblation imbued with his powers of *yoga*. By her eating the oblation before procreative union, Satyavati would have her desire fulfilled. Her oblation was imbued with *brahmana* *mantras*, thus ensuring that their son would have the qualities of a peaceful, contented, and forgiving *brahmana*.

Satyavati’s mother, King Gadhi’s queen, requested her son-in-law to prepare an oblation for her too. Richika Muni agreed, but he prepared the Queen’s offering with appropriate warrior *mantras*. This would bless the queen with a son of bold, courageous *kshatriya* qualities, a prince willing to fight for truth and righteousness.

After preparing the two oblations, the sage repaired to the river for his ritualistic bath. Meanwhile, the queen, assuming that a husband out of natural affection would put a greater effort into the oblation meant for his wife, exchanged the two oblations with her daughter’s permission. When Richika Muni returned from his bath, he discovered the switch and scolded his wife. "You have committed a great wrong," he said. "Because of this your son will be a fierce *kshatriya* capable of punishing everyone. And your brother will be a scholar learned in all the spiritual sciences."

Satyavati then begged that her son not be a *kshatriya*. Pacified by her charm and devotion, Richika Muni adjusted the reaction. "Since the *mantras* must have an effect," he proclaimed, "your grandson, rather than your son, will have an invincible *kshatriya* spirit."

Satyavati became the mother of the austere and tolerant Jamadagni while his son was Parashurama, or "Rama with the axe (*parashu*)." *Shrimad Bhagavatam* (1.3.20) says, "The sixteenth incarnation of the Supreme Personality of Godhead (as Parashurama) annihilated the *kshatriyas* twenty-one times, being angry at their rebellion against the *brahmanas*."

At that time long ago in the Treta Yuga there was a great warrior king named Kartaviryarjuna who had obtained a thousand arms by worshiping the *avatar* Dattatreya. He had also received other opulence and mystic powers that only served to further inflate his ego. Once Kartaviryarjuna was sporting in the company of many young women in the Narmada River, at his capital called Mahishmati. This area incidentally, is today known as the town of Mandla, a pleasant ride some 90 kms. south of Jabalpur. To show off his prowess, King Kartaviryarjuna dammed the Narmada’s flow with his thousand arms. Upstream, where the demon-king Ravana had halted with his army, the water overflowed its banks, flooding Ravana’s camp. The powerful Ravana challenged Kartaviryarjuna over the insult, but he proved no match for the thousand-armed king, who captured and then neglectfully released Ravana as one might trap and then set free a wild monkey. King Kartaviryarjuna now considered himself all-powerful and invincible.

Some time after this incident, Kartaviryarjuna traveled north and met the sage Jamadagni at his forest hermitage. With the help of his *kamadhenu*, a celestial wish-fulfilling cow, Jamadagni sumptuously fed the mighty king and his vast retinue. The envious Kartaviryarjuna, unable to tolerate a mere hermit’s owning an opulence surpassing anything of his, ordered his men to steal the cow along with her calf. Kartaviryarjuna then took the weeping *kamadhenus* to his capital Mahishmatipur.

When Parashurama, the youngest son of Jamadagni, heard of this great offense, he became as angry as a trampled snake. Although born of a sage, Parashurama had been influenced by the *kshatriya* oblations, as predicted by his grandfather Richika Muni. It is understood that the Supreme Lord and His plenary expansions are ever above the material world and cannot be influenced by the modes of material nature. Therefore, all these activities are simply understood to be His *lila*, or pastimes with the aim of attracting us into His pastimes.

Parashurama took up his axe, shield, bow, and arrows and pursued the thousand-armed king just as a lion chases an elephant. At Mahishmati, Kartaviryarjuna gasped in fear as he saw the sixteenth *avatar* of the Lord swiftly approaching. Kartaviryarjuna sent hundreds of thousands of troops to battle Parashurama, but the Lord slaughtered every one of them by rushing here and there as swiftly as the mind. The battlefield at his feet turned to mud from the blood spilling from the headless and armless torsos of slain warriors.

Then the courageous Kartaviryarjuna decided to personally confront Parashurama on the field of battle. Taking up five hundred bows fixed with five hundred arrows, he issued a challenge. With only one bow, Lord Parashurama cut to pieces each of the king’s hundreds of weapons. Furious, Kartaviryarjuna uprooted trees and hills and rushed at the axe-bearer, intent on killing him. With great force and speed, Lord Parashurama sliced off each of the king’s thousand arms, and then beheaded the horrified and helpless king, who followed his fallen army to a grisly doom. As the ten thousand sons of Kartaviryarjuna witnessed their father’s defeat, they ran away in fear. Lord Parashurama then gently released the stolen *kamadhenu* cow and her calf and returned them to his father’s *ashram*.

Bowing before his father and greeting his brothers, the warrior-*brahmana* explained every detail of the war and the heroic death of Kartaviryarjuna. Still Parashurama’s father, the tolerant Jamadagni, scolded him. "O great hero," he said, "you have unnecessarily killed the king, who is supposed to be the embodiment of all the demigods. Thus you have committed a sin. We *brahmanas* are worshipable by others only due to our quality of forgiveness. It is through this quality of forbearance that Lord Brahma has achieved the post of master of the entire universe. The Supreme Personality of Godhead Lord Vishnu, the remover of obstacles, becomes pleased with those who are forgiving. Forgiveness is illuminating like the Sun, and cultivation of this quality is the *brahmana’s* duty."

Rishi Jamadagni then told his son how to atone for the sin. With a father’s love he instructed, *tirtha-samsevaya chamho jahy angachyuta chetanah*: "O son, expansion of my very self, you must worship the sacred places to atone for this sinful act. You must become conscious of the Infallible Absolute."

With that, the sage sent his son on a pilgrimage and ordered him to visit the holy cities, *ashrams*, rivers, hills, and lakes throughout the land. In truth, Lord Parashurama had committed no sin in the execution of his divine *lila*. He had awarded liberation to all the warriors who died valiantly before him. His pilgrimage was to purify holy places and establish new ones blessed by his lotus feet. But to teach that killing is generally improper, sage Jamadagni ordered Lord Parashurama *tirtha-sevaya*, "serve the holy spots." Many visit holy places just to bathe in a sacred lake or river and consider their sins thereby washed away. But the real purpose of *tirtha-sevaya* is to associate with holy devotees who reside at these places for the sake of human welfare. Lord Parashurama’s example of obedience to his father was exemplary. He told his father, *tatha iti*: "Let it be so." He then undertook a tour of India for a full year after which he returned to the *ashram* of his family.

Meanwhile the miserable sons of Kartaviryarjuna had been nursing a grudge against Lord Parashurama since the death of their father. One day while Parashurama was roaming the woods with his brothers, the vengeful sons of Kartaviryarjuna invaded their hermitage. They murderously approached Jamadagni, sitting in yogic trance by the sacrificial fire. Renuka, Parashurama’s mother, entreated them not harm her husband, who was absorbed in meditating upon the Supreme Truth. Despite her pleas, the invaders, devoid of noble *kshatriya* qualities, beheaded the sage and absconded with his head.

Deep in the forest, Parashurama and his brothers heard their mother shouting, "O Rama! O Rama!" They rushed to the *ashram* and found her frantically beating her chest over the loss of her husband. Seeing that she had struck her chest in anguish twenty-one times, Lord Parashurama vowed to rid the world of all devious members of the *kshatriya* class twenty-one times.

Parashurama departed to Mahishmati, which was doomed for the murder of a peaceful *brahmana.* There he extracted a terrible toll. Just as Kartaviryarjuna’s sons had mercilessly beheaded his father, Parashurama created a mountain of heads severed from their bodies. Other arrogant kings who also lacked respect for *brahmanas* saw the river of blood created by the Lord and trembled in fear.

Lord Parashurama returned with the severed head of his father to the *ashram*. There, with the power of mystic arts and *mantras*, he rejoined the head to his Jamadagni’s body, which had been preserved by his brothers. By the touch of the Lord, Jamadagni returned to life as if waking from a restful sleep. The all-victorious Lord Parashurama then completed his vow by taking his ritualistic bath in the Saraswati River. After ridding the earth of the burden of defiant kings maddened by a lust for power, Lord Parashurama came to penance in Madhya Bharata, Central India. Though the incident of annihilating the *kshatriya* class occurred millions of years ago, Lord Parashuram is said to still penance upon the earth. He is known as one of three *chiranjivas*, or holy personages who will live as long as the earth shall exist.

**\* \* \***

While walking in the Vindhyas near Jabalpur I encounter a *sadhu* who knows the location of a *kunda* or bathing tank called Parashurama Kunda formed from the Pariat River, a tributary of the Narmada. "It is not far," he tells me. "You will find it in the Khamaria area here in Jabalpur District itself. That area is part farmland and part jungle. There is also a hill nearby called Parashurama Giri upon which there is a rock with the imprint of the *avatar’s* wooden footwear." I thank the *sadhu* who responds with "Rama, Rama" and walks on his way. My guide Gyan Prakash and I discuss the possibilities. Access to the *kunda* will be through fields and down jungle paths of sun-baked earth which--except during the rainy season when they are turned to mud--are easily navigable by 375 pound motorcycle.

The next day we are off exploring. Gyan is riding his ancient Indian-built Lambretta and I am on the Enfield. Turning off Hwy. 22 at Khamaria, we pass through the village of Matamar which consists of a few mud-and-straw huts. There, some gentlemen sitting beneath a shade tree point us towards the *kunda*, about a mile away. Beyond the village, in the surrounding jungle, dwell tribal Gonds, who live either by fishing and hunting or by working menial jobs that pay a day-to-day wage. The Gonds have their own primitive religion, language, and customs that separate them from mainstream Hindus. Indeed, language and customs vary from tribe to tribe.

We find our own way into the fields and jungle paths until we are soon overlooking a *kunda* circled by *tantaniya* boulders that form the Vindhya Range. These gigantic stones are named for the sound caused when the wind blows through them. The Vindhyas are revered second only to the Himalayas and are an extremely old range. Low hills supporting incredibly large rocks are a geological testimony to their age. Rising half a kilometer beyond the *kunda* is the large isolated Shri Parashurama Giri, the hill upon which sits the stone marked by the *avatar’s* footprints. Beside the *kunda* is a tiny and somewhat desolate Parashurama Mandir. Such temples to the axe-wielding *avatara* are rare in India.

Near the edge of the *kunda* is a straw hut and alongside the hut is a small field of vegetables. From the hut emerges a *yogi* dressed in an orange kilt of extremely rough cloth resembling burlap. We greet the *yogi* and I move forward to bow before him. He makes a sound like *phhht* and responds by striking me hard on the back, both a test of tolerance and a blessing. He examines my features to see if there is any sign of objection on my part. There isn’t as I thank him with the words, "*Dhanyavad*, Babaji."

We are surprised that he speaks English. He was once Shri M.K. Rana born in Nepal, and who until his 60’s worked as a policeman in Jabalpur. Rather than retire to the care of his family, he says that the Lord Parashurama has called him to care for his holy bathing place. He is now Mahankala Baba, named for Lord Shiva, the "lord of time." He offers us some coconut *prasada*, which Gyan and I accept and pocket respectfully We postpone eating it because murderous tantrics who mix *dhatura* poison with food offerings to waylay strangers are still quite common in remote areas. Our excuse for not eating the offering is that we need to bathe in the sacred *kunda* first. After prayers and meditation, we enter the lake.

The waters are inviting and fresh. After about fifteen minutes, I notice a local tribal, a dark Gond, squatting timidly behind some tall grasses. Keeping him in the corner of my eye, I pretend not to notice. Obviously, he is merely curious and poses no threat. Refreshed, I emerge and dress, careful not to catch his eye while donning my clothes. The trick in India is never to be completely undressed in front of anyone. Dry garments cover wet ones that are dropped from underneath. Then the wet ones are wrung in the *kunda*, and draped over the handlebars to dry. That done, I offer *pranams* with a polite nod to the tribal who continues to gape from the grasses. The Gonds have their own primitive religion and the Hindu method of respecting another with folded hands is not part of their culture. He continues staring as Gyan and I proceed to the temple. The Deity is a stone *murti* of a smiling

figure, *parashu* resting on his shoulder, and offering a blessing with his right hand. By the design it appears as though the Deity is well over a hundred years old. After some minutes of *darshan* and prayer we return to meet Baba Mahankal at his hut. He knows the area quite well, which is a clue that he is trustworthy. Tantrics never stay in one place for very long for fear of their lives. Now we are pleased to honor the offerings he gave us earlier.

Pointing toward the direction of the village we had passed through, he says that local industry is eyeing the land to manufacture bricks from the soil. His duty, he says, has been deputed by Lord Parashuram to preserve this holy place. "If I don’t stop them from desecrating the holy *bhumi* (land) of Lord Parashurama, then who will? " he asks.

When I inquired whether any other foreigner had visited the *kunda* in recent memory, he chuckles. "Are you kidding? Even local city-dwellers don’t know of this place!" He explains that a festival is held here each year on Makara Sankranti, when the Sun-god, enters Capricorn by Vedic calculations, thus starting his six-month trek through the celestial regions. This is the only *mela* observed here, and it attracts a mere handful of villagers. When I ask about the rock atop Parashuram Giri, he admonishes, "This *shila* (sacred stone) has been seen by very few," daring us to find it.

It has grown too late in the day to make the search, so I promise myself I will return there some other day to behold the imprint of Lord Parashurama’s feet. During subsequent visits I find an alternate route via the Panagar road from which the *kunda* can be approached from the right bank of the river. On that side of the hill lives a one-legged *yogi* in a cave near the ruins of a great and ancient old temple. The *yogi* is maintained by the villagers who daily supply him with some rice and vegetables.

The history of the old temple whose carved stone pillars lie here and there half submerged in the ground is shrouded in time. But it very likely that this was a temple to Lord Parashurama. Since ancient times it has been the custom to erect temples of particular deities at the sites of their *lilas*. Central India or Gondwana was conquered by the Mughals who defeated the forces of the Hindu queen Rani Durgavati in the 1564. Rani Durgavati, who commanded an army of Gond tribals, was killed upon her elephant in battle some twenty-five miles south of here. Later in the 1600’s Akbar’s intolerant grandson Aurangzeb wreaked havoc here in the Gondwana region with his policy of temple desecration and destruction. This is the most plausible explanation for the ruins.

**\* \* \***

Nowhere in India is there a proper temple to Lord Parashuram. This would make an ideal location since the axe wielding *avatara* is believed to still display his *lila* at this very spot. With the patronage of one wealthy businessman here a magnificent Parashuram Mandir could rise once again for the glory of Mother India. Along National Hwy 7, a motorable dirt trail a few kms. south of Panagar provides easy access to the area. Thousands of pilgrims travel this route each month to visit the Hill of Devi, Goddess Sharada of Maihar. Each pilgrim bus or van would surely stop to see the temples of Shri Parashurama. The lost jungle shrine of the empowered incarnation Lord Parashurama could be turned into a place of pilgrimage once again, before horrendous industries, shopping centers, and megalithic apartment complexes encroach upon a spiritual treasure. Which will arrive first, bulldozers or devotees?

I have returned several times, but have never found the Lord’s footprints in the sacred stone. Yet the Enfield has had its finest hour in the "discovery" of Parashurama Kunda for without the rugged bike I could never have chanced upon this hidden and forgotten *tapo-bhumi* of the axe-wielding *avatara*.

## 7. At a Stoplight in Nagpur

## "A Moment to Reflect"

NAGPUR, IN THE STATE OF MAHARASHTRA, is a huge sprawling middle-of-nowhere megalopolis at the crossroads of Central India. It is right in the heart of the other side of the world. If you were to mark an "X"--Delhi to Madras and Calcutta to Bombay--Nagpur would fall right at the crux. It is India's hot spot, sweltering in the sub-continent's central plains with summertime temperatures sometimes rising above 50º (C). Once a forested area of serpent worshipping Gond tribaIs, Nagpur literally means, "snake town."

The British arrived here in the 1820's and with little ado wrenched power from the ruling Marathas: the Marathi-speaking Bhonsle kings. After emerging victoriously from the fierce Battle of Sitalbuldi in 1827, the new British rulers built the fort atop Nagpur's highest hill and surveyed their empire. That old fort, today home to the India Army’s Grenadier--or commando--division, now enjoys a 180 degree view of endless third world urban sprawl. Here the Grenadiers respectfully tend to the graves where their ancient enemies lie buried. This is remarkable since for eons the Vedic civilization has cremated its dead. Yet for the Hindu *kshatriya* or warrior, it is a sacred duty to honor the worthy enemy, in life or death even to the point of following traditions not his own.

Right here at the crossroads of India, *samsara*, or life's ever-turning wheel, has been momentarily riveted by a red light. Straddling a 1980 twin pipe single 250cc Yezdi, India’s version of the two-stroke Jawa "Californian," I stand at the line frozen in time bombarded by the impatient sounds of jammed traffic all about me. Drowned by the beeping horns of drivers who apparently think that honking will make the light turn green; the angry noises of the larger vehicles, trucks and buses; the commands of farmers to their oxen; the small arguments among other two-wheelers who vie for space, this is a spring-loaded waiting game. Here he whose mind is fixed upon the task at hand can alone survive.

The Jawa motorcycle like the one I am riding came to India from Czechoslovakia in the early 1960's. It evolved into the Yezdi in the mid-70's and unfortunately ceased production with the Japanese invasion by the big four (Honda, Kawasaki, Suzuki and Yamaha) in the mid-80's. Despite the fact that the Yezdi factory closed down shop some decade and a half-ago, used specimens are still common. Today a solid Jawa or Yezdi can be purchased and entirely rebuilt with durable new paint for around $600, a real bargain for a practically new bike capable of negotiating 100,000 thrilling kilometers of mountain, jungle and desert terrain. My Yezdi is the perfect Third Worldmobile; it is responsive, quick and simple to ride...or would be if the light would turn green.

Before me, the lop-sided and soot-encrusted red light that has obliged me to halt looks as though it must have been dangling here before the first motorized vehicle ever came to India. In fact, the traffic signal is a relatively new imposition upon Nagpur's crowded intersections. I seriously doubt that any of the bicycles, bullock carts, pedal and motor rickshaws, scooters, mini-cars and mini-vans, imitation 4X4's, trucks and buses would have halted for a mere dangling light if it were not for a snarling, dark-complexioned, brown-uniformed policeman standing grimly in the center of the crossroads. A gang of his fellow cops, obviously his seniors, their dark, sweaty brows furled to disguise the fact that they are loafing, huddle together off to the side. Doubtless their presence insures the honesty of the impatient motorists, and helps guarantee the safety of their junior colleague facing off precariously before tense and spring-loaded rows of chaotic traffic from hell.

Police here can look more taciturn, threatening and tough than the thugs they are underpaid to control. Though I have counted many cops among my friends in India due to necessity, curiosity or genuine respect, I am not especially well disposed towards Nagpur's men of the uniform at this particular junction. Ten minutes ago a menacing group of Nagpur's finest formed a human barricade across the road and forced me to pull over. My international driving license kept my bike and me out of their clutches, but there was one detail that had to be sorted out. The cop pointed to the lane in which I was blissfully moving along, minding my own business, and said, "One way."

"No sign!?" I responded quickly, looking around at others moving in the same wrong direction.

"Yes, no sign. You pay 150 rupees."

I answered, "Correct price is 50 rupees. Here take 100." As he gingerly took the note to the approval of his fellow cop-thugs, he began bobbing his head side to side indicating with a pre-planned measure of reluctance that I was free to go, bargain accepted. The other cops on the roadside made a great show of not seeing the monetary exchange. No doubt they will get their share soon...and in some next life. A receipt would have been out of the question, yet had I insisted one could have doubtlessly been issued from the police station where the outstretched hands of one dozen more brown-uniformed extortionists would be waiting.

An Englishman I once met backpacking in the Himalayas told me of the time that a kindly cop invited him to lunch. The two of them strolled over to a *dhaba*, a peasant's eatery, and sat down at a table overlooking a spectacular valley. This cop, who appeared cultured and high-ranking, graciously asked the guest what he would like to eat, and then placed his order as well. After lunch and a Thums Up the policeman politely excused himself due to pressing engagements, while the waiter handed the Englishman the bill for two on cue.

The light is long, very long, and the dust and exhaust fumes choke me.

Many of the riders have wrapped up their faces under lengths of gauze-like cotton cloth to keep out the fumes. Slits of dark eyes peering mummy-like from behind cotton masks give the two-wheeling motorists the determined appearance of terrorists on a suicide mission. I look down and check to see that my toes are pointing straight ahead, drawn alongside my bike. Space is at a premium here where three lanes have magically split into eight or nine. Underpaid and under-tested bus drivers from remote villages demonstrate a might is-right complex by jockeying up next to the two-wheelers and then slowly rolling their huge tires dose enough so that the rubber's heat is felt emanating in waves. And neither being a foreigner assures me any protection. Having a sunburned and smiling pink face is fine while you walk through the bazaars and the merchants seek to woo your rupees from your wallet with flattery and the offer of a Thums Up. But once you sit upon a bike you have joined India's swirling, rushing whirlpool of well-tanned humanity, and survival becomes the name of the challenge. On these roads survival means stepping down from the royal tourist's throne as you climb into the saddle of a motorcycle, for India's roads have no room for the poser.

The amazing thing is the huge number of near misses that you witness--or almost take part in--per second, here upon the world's most dangerous roadways. When something does go horribly wrong and an accident occurs, a mob will quickly form. In short order the supposed guilty driver is summarily ferreted out and unceremoniously beaten to death. This is not hearsay: I actually witnessed this once while riding through Agra. Hearing an "explosion" like the popping of a huge grape, I glanced over my shoulder to see in horror that a small boy had just stuck his head under the huge, slow-rolling wheel of a bus. Immediately as splattered brains shooting in all directions fell to earth, a swarm of angry men descended upon the rolling megalith. As I threw back the throttle, I could see in my rear-view mirror the loudly protesting driver going down at the 1,000 hands and 10,000 fingers of a wild gang of no less than 500 cursing, screaming men. I did not wait around to witness the driver's fate in the center of that vicious wolf pack. If, however, the offending driver escapes the mob, then their anger is just as likely to turn upon the next witless victim who happens to drive their way.

On three other times I have narrowly escaped the savagery of an Indian mob when peaceful, friendly--even kind--men go berserk at the drop of a hat like milk boiling over. After an Indian riot, a night out with the boys, nameless dead, lives wasted for naught, are picked up by burning ground *chandalas* as the mob, its blood lust satiated, wends its way home for dinner with the family.

Perhaps citizenry discontent would be better aimed at a city hall that carelessly builds unplanned roads and then neglects their maintenance. Or directed at the state government which hands out licenses to drive without adequate testing or education. Whereas the challenge of riding India's deserts, hills, mountains or jungles is exhilarating and uplifting, biking her city streets even at a stoplight, is a battle for the very right to live. A puppy that appears rabid has somehow wandered into the mix and, foaming at the mouth, gags his last.

A woman in her twenties walks through the moving traffic on my left holding the hand of a small child, and crosses casually without looking either way. Apparently she is coming from her astrologer who for ten rupees has informed her, "You will have long life." On the average, nearly four hundred are slaughtered on the streets of Nagpur each year.

Here straddling my Yezdi, the light is painfully long, so I spend a moment sizing up individual faces of the particular crowd I'm standing with. Perhaps there is a "destined crossing" at this particular juncture of time and place, one of those eternal friends you just bump into and feel like you've always known each other. India is the best place to meet such people, due to the average Indian's acceptance of the fact that we are all part and parcel of the same spiritual whole anyway. What is called deja vu in the West is here called *jatasmara*, memory of a past life. Here the explanation why some people become bound as instant friends is a simple one; they were friends in some forgotten previous scenario.

Once in Madras I turned around to find a *dhoti-clad brahmana*, his forehead neatly marked with *tilak*, his *shikha* tuft of hair well tied, smiling upon me. When I smiled back, my "new, eternal friend"--whom I was to know for less than a minute yet will remember for the rest of my life--simply said, "Do you know that feeling of sudden joy that you get, filling you up from within? It means that someplace, someone dear is emanating fond thoughts of you." He went on his way, but doubtless we'll meet for another second in some future birth, at least if neither of us achieve the all-transcendent goal of *yoga*. For he who achieves the goal of *yoga* no longer has any use for this world with its temporary affections of society, friendship and love.

On another occasion thirty years ago, a tall-dignified Mussalman strolled up to me as I was purchasing a pair of Kohlapuri sandals at Kalbadevi. He reached down and gently seized the second toe of my right foot and noted that its length exceeds that of my big toe. He casually said, "This shows that you are on a spiritual mission and will succeed in your search through India." Sometimes obscure and ordinary-looking strangers speak with the eternal voice of prophets here in the mystic East.

All around me are dozens of sinewy laborers pulling ancient wooden carts, or lightning-eyed college girls on their way to class delicately perched upon bright step-throughs like the 50cc Luna or 100cc Scooty. In front of me there is a balding husband, his coconut-oiled brown dome baking in the morning sun. Four members of his immediate family are balanced with him upon his 175cc Bajaj, India's incarnation of the Vespa. Here and there are tinny stick shift econo-boxes that only the most frugal American would drive, yet in this land many are navigated by full-time chauffeurs. There are Indian jeeps and vans all stuffed to bursting with three or four generations of over-population. Additionally there must be fifty restless, Indian-built 150cc Indo-Japanese motorcycles piloted by ambitious young men on Indian standard time off to a late start for work. Yet I am alone in a vast crowd today, an isolated twig in a tsunami wave of mankind. Ambition, anxiety and the desire to succeed in a modern world whose tenebrous goals are feverishly chased but are nowhere understood ensnares each of my stoplight neighbors as they charge blindfolded into the twenty-first century. At this particular crossroads at least, the rare and serendipitous jewel of an eternal friend is not found.

On my right is a three-wheeler, a scooter rickshaw, which has a condom ad pasted on its rear, a taboo subject just twenty-five years ago. In the ad a model smiles temptingly from behind a gigantic rubber that is so big the man to use it would have to be around forty-five feet tall. Like so many actors whose careers have died with their character, it strikes me that this pretty young hopeful has already torpedoed her calling by allowing herself to be typecast. In a rigidly structured society like India's, overcoming the stigma of "condom girl" will be no easy task. I imagine that the ad's Hindi motto says something like, "If Dad had used one of these I wouldn't be here."

As the burning morning sunlight filters through the smog and din, heightening the chiseled features of austere brown faces, I note a bleak seriousness engraved onto each countenance. How true it is that traveling upon the treacherous, rocky, dusty boulevards of the man-made urban hells of India is never the same as negotiating her country roads. There, contemplative brows reflect a more submissive obedience to the Eternal Will personified. In India's countryside, on her fields, around her villages and upon hidden dirt paths, everyone you meet is a yogi of some sort.

Whether child or old man, it does not matter, India's rural folk radiate a sort of transcendental happiness. Only a half hour or less from this stoplight, the temporary business of eating, sleeping and procreating is secondary to the duty of preparing for that anticipated ride upon the eternal road. Here in Nagpur, as in any other metropolis, life has decades ago been turned 180 degrees on its head. In the cities spiritual fulfillment is just too distant to reflect upon: to keep the belly full a modern city dweller in India must toil for twelve full hours daily, and slog through this traffic for another two. This is progress; the gift of municipal perdition whose abysmal rush on all sides of me has been unremittingly barricaded by this cursed light.

I reflect back to my first Indian two-wheeled vehicle that I rode for any appreciable distance, a Bajaj-Vespa scooter. One day there at a tea stand, a young gentleman approached me to inquire about my activities in his country. I told him that I wanted to learn about the local holy spots, the *punya-bhumis*, his interest piqued: "My name is Ram and I am a *brahmana*. I will guide you." Out of the crowd had emerged an eternal friend. In fact Ram Mishra became one of this lifetime's best friends ever. Together we'd scooter about to places where gods played in previous *yugas*, along the *ghats* of the sacred Narmada, hiking up to temples hidden behind colossal boulders in the hills. We visited *ashrams* where *yogis* perform miracle cures, sat and meditated with pious sages who had never before come into face to face contact with Western society.

Once upon the city's narrow lanes, my eyes filled with horror as a huge truck was bearing down upon us. Probably less than a second from under the wheels, I yanked the handlebars obliquely to one side, a left face that doubtless saved our lives. I remember once seeing a picture from the epic *Mahabharata* in which the fierce son of Bhima, Ghatotkacha, by the powers of his *rakshasi* mother, would grow to a huge size each sundown, uprooting helpless warriors upon the Battlefield of Kurukshetra. That's how I felt before the unforgiving, snorting truck--that base iron chariot of Kali Yuga--that day with Ram. Why had this happened? Why were we not killed? How is it that we had been saved? Was it I that turned the handlebars, or was some unseen Force looking out for us? Had we escaped a destined moment, or more precisely, had our destined moment not arrived? Plainly, for a second, the great, cold hand of death had suddenly appeared to snatch us away, but just as suddenly had withdrawn into the clouds as if the god of death Yamaraja had changed his mind directed by an even higher Authority. In this world death reigns always supreme, but apparently even Yama must obey Higher Commands. Without much thought, we immediately returned to our mission of searching for holy places that day. Minutes later the incident was forgotten, as we found ourselves lost in the serenity of village byways.

About two weeks later, Ram's parents suddenly arranged his marriage. It was not done in the usual festive fashion, but rather like some big secret coming to light. Tongues of bewildered neighbors must have wagged at the suddenness of the event. Not long after the embers of the ghee-soaked fire of the marriage *yagna* had turned cold, it was discovered that the bride was not as youthful as her falsified and ill-matched horoscope had shown. Her parents had made a hurried arrangement to offload a daughter with health problems. The bride was no longer young; she was now approaching middle age. "Although without flames, an unmarried daughter burns the Hindu father like fire," it is said, and the marriage to Ram had been an easy solution.

Ram, too, had had a chequered past, rumours had it, due to the vagaries of youth. On the day that I had met him, I had noticed some of his friends were rough types. But he was a *brahmana* with a good heart and by showing me around the *punya-bhumis* of his native Jabalpur, it occurred to me, he was trying to rectify past youthful mischief.

Now the misery of Ram's bride was boundless. She had been forced upon her younger husband as much as he had been forced upon her. Due to her chronic female problems, she could not render the service to her husband every Hindu wife would love to give.

A few days after the marriage, she went into the bathroom, doused herself with kerosene, and lit herself on fire. Ram tried valiantly to put the flames out with the very arms that could not embrace her, but he instead became consumed by the holocaust. The couple, who could not live as one here, left this world as one in a pyre.

A week or so on, Ram's father came to me and we solaced one another as confused and blind mortals try to do. He knew that I had studied the Vedic astrology of the Hindus, and he brought Ram's horoscope with him. Mostly I couldn't look at the stars that day, which requires an impersonal detachment, so we spent some hours talking of the vagaries of life, destiny and most of all my personal affection for his gentle son Ram. I recalled how he had guided me to so many hidden places like Siddheshwar Cave, Shani Tirtha, Gupteshwara, or Gwari Ghat. I still enjoy visiting these places today and in a way Ram is still with me when I do. I still see his father, who never recovered from the tragedy, Hindus having no pretense to the politically correct pretension of "closure" as we have in the West.

**\* \* \***

Despite the poisonous exhaust fumes, the taciturn cops, the blazing Sun, the bus trying to roll over my toes, or my grim memories, the thought somehow never leaves me that somehow I am lucky to be here. True, I've met my share of cursing and complaining foreigners who cannot wait to fly out of India, who worship the jetliner as a saving god from the sky that will carry them back to the predictable security of America or Europe. I once heard of a traveler who landed at Calcutta, and caught a taxicab that got lost in Bengal's torrential overflow of humanity. After a few hours of ensnarled third world traffic he had had enough. The traveler's pioneer spirit bruised, the would-be explorer ordered the driver back to the airport where he caught the next plane out of the City of the Black Hole.

I knew a fellow American here who after a month in the exotic East had had enough. Blubbering like a baby, he climbed a tall building in Bombay and threatened to jump--unless a representative of his embassy, Big Brother with a Ticket--would come to fly him back home to the States. It is true that while some travelers go crazy in India, others say that they were crazy to come here in the first place. But for the determined wanderer with the single-minded concentration to peer beyond the curtain that veils Vedic India, suddenly that which is most ancient appears sane. And that which the people at this stop light are pursuing--the fast-food and toilet paper, defecate and wipe, civilization--is recognized for the insanity it is.

Perhaps one of the problems with urban Indians, at least the ones I see here at this infernal stoplight, is that they have no appreciation for the richness of their venerable heritage. They are waging all their stakes upon a future of vacuous promises in exchange for a rich culture older than any other on Earth. Of course, it is understandable after the imposition of more than 1,000 years of foreign rule, beginning with the Afghan Mahmud Ghazni. Even the word Hindu is a foreign term, given to them by invaders who could not correctly pronounce "Sindhu" the sixth of the seven holy rivers. There is a joke amongst religious scholars that says the word "Hindu" is nowhere in the Hindu scriptures. For most Indians, their religion is so long forgotten that they do not even know its proper name.

The ancient name of this land is Bharat, not India, and the religion is *sanatan* *dharma*, "eternal service to God," and not Hinduism. Whether the Persians, the Dutch, the French, the Portuguese, the British, the Christian missionaries, or powerful "friends" like the Americans and Russians, the *modus operandi* of the colonialist and religious missionary has been to impress upon the Indian the inferiority of the richest and longest-lived culture on Earth. With their sacred cow milked dry and readied for slaughter by imported steel blades, generations became apologetic and even ashamed of their birthright and religion.

To rationalize themselves and their heritage, Indian intellectual apologists began to compare the salient features of their vast sub-continent with acceptable foreign places they were taught to admire. Kashmir became the Switzerland of India. Poona, or now Pune, became the Oxford and Cambridge of India. Lord Krishna's birthplace at Mathura has been compared to Jerusalem, though Krishna appeared thousands of years before Jesus. The great historical epics the *Ramayana* and *Mahabharata* became the much more recent *Iliad* and *Odyssey* of India. Indians have even claimed that the *dasha-avataras,* Vedic India’s ten incarnations of God, represent a sort of early tribal understanding of Darwinian evolution. Bombay, where more invidious films are made than any place on earth, has become Bollywood, to the chagrin of the aging traditionalists who, longing for the rebirth of the more sober Vedic Age, point to many current Hindu revivalist movements. As I survey this town through hot dust and exhaust fumes, it occurs to me that nobody has ever had the temerity to compare this Nagpur, this Snake Town, to anywhere else.

Here on India's roadways, in her villages, towns and cities, in her forests and jungles, I am often reminded of the poem about the six blind men of old Hindustan who tried to describe an elephant to each other. The one who grabbed the tail thought the elephant to be like a rope, the one who latched onto the trunk, thought it resembled a snake, and so on. For one traveler India is an adventurer-rich paradise; and for another it is a dishonest, crazy, diseased and poverty-stricken hellhole. For one it is a journey into new regions of thought, philosophy and enlightenment. For the next India is a trip back in time to the Dark Ages. For me, who has derived so much from this gentle, brutal land; it is impossible to look without condescension upon those foreign travelers who come here expecting the homage that their greenbacks certainly bring at some tourist resort. True the amateur tourist unceremoniously discarded from India like a reject from boot camp deserves pity as doubtless many foreigners pity me just for liking it here. From my point of view, those travelers who succeed and thrive here comprise the highest echelon of world wayfarer. They are not your Club Med, planned vacation types. The proper wayfarer or pilgrim here is like Mark Twain who wrote, "India is the land that all men long to see, and having seen by so much as a glimpse, would not trade that glimpse for all the other sights of the world. This land has an irresistible appeal to alien prince and alien peasant alike."

Lowell Thomas, another great American adventurer-author, wrote, "(India), I realized, was by far the most fascinating of all the countries I had visited...when I saw India I knew that I was witnessing the greatest show of all. I had found Australia and New Zealand fascinating, Africa full of thrills and surprises, and the Far East absorbingly interesting. But India towers as far above them as the Himalayas tower above the Alps. As a spectacle, there is nothing like it. Variety, we are assured, is the spice of life. The country that interests us the most is the one where there is the most variety...And India has variety to the nthdegree. It is supremely the land of the startling contrast. The southern tip touches the equator. Central India is in the Temperate Zone and is not only a land of mighty rivers and fertile valleys, but also includes vast deserts similar to those of Arabia, Arizona and the Sahara. Along the northern border of India loom those towering mountain ranges, the Himalayas, loftiest of all the peaks of earth, their summits clad with ice and snow since the dawn of time...Who holds India, holds the world. As a theory of world-politics you can pick it to pieces, but you cannot escape its seductive glamorous persuasion...India is a land of splendor and magnificence far, far surpassing anything to be found in either Europe or America and at the same time it is also a land of squalor and misery so terrible that the mere mention of it makes me shudder. I had thought of India as a country one could see, and be satisfied to come away from, in a month or two. But instead of two months, I stayed for two years. Even then I was not content. I wanted to stay on and on."

Lowell Thomas continues: "Every traveler from the West who visits India should have a *guru*, a wise man, to lead him about and explain the strange things he sees and hears. Without a *guru* the European or American who sojourns for a little while in India wanders as in a daze and comes away as bewildered as any Alice in Wonderland. But to find your *guru* when you get to India, ah, that may not be so easy." Considering that these words were written in 1929 before the rush of Western *yogis* to the mystic East, they are strikingly profound.

Lowell Thomas, who was the world’s most famous adventure-traveler in the 1930’s and 40’s, is echoing a spiritual sentiment that has been handed down for millennia in India. In the supreme scripture of *yoga*, *Bhagavad Gita* (4.34), Krishna offers Arjuna similar advice: "Just try to learn the truth by approaching a spiritual master. Inquire from him submissively and render service unto him. The self-realized soul can impart knowledge unto you because he has seen the truth." But the *guru* must be genuine, and therein lays the challenge. Even Arjuna wanted to know how to spot the genuine *paramhamsa*, or supreme mystic. Arjuna said: “What are the symptoms of one whose consciousness is thus merged in Transcendence? How does he speak, and what is his language?? How does he sit, and how does he walk?” (BG 2.54) In fact Shri Krishna never answered this question; His divine example as Arjuna’s charioteer was silent explanation enough.

Yet even for the pilgrim on a quest full of hopes, there is another sort of pitfall. Like crocodiles in the Ganges or serpents in the sandal tree, there are many "business *gurus,*" ordinary, unqualified men who enjoy posing as extraordinary spiritual masters to make a cheap living off foolish disciples. The simple formula used to cheat their victims is usually based around a predictable formula of verbal legerdemain. Bob Dylan did not mince words when he summed up this formula as "kill them with self-confidence while poisoning them with words." Usually, the slick pretender *swami* begins with something like: "Yes, yes! You are God. But now you have forgotten your supreme divinity. I shall make you God again." Invoices are distributed just prior to your right to advertise yourself as the latest incarnation.

**\* \* \***

Although there are thousands of such saffron-clad flim-flam men sermonizing similar facsimiles of the same humbug, duped followers become so hypnotized that they remain loyal to their own particular charlatan. Each clique has their own God. In reality, the *shastras* teach that truth can be found in many places without compromising devotion to one’s own spiritual master. That is the logic behind the words *diksha guru* who initiates the disciple, and the *siksha guru*, who offers instruction that does not contradict the primary *guru*. It is the test of a worthy disciple that he must remain staunch in his vows and convictions before the temptations of the world. If any and all of the business *gurus* are the same Supreme God, and the message of becoming God is one, then it should make little difference which one is consulted. The naive students explain: "I am not there yet. Everyone is God, but for now only my *guru* is God." Or could it be that your *guru* just needs the money more than some other god?

**\* \* \***

Bewildered disciples of yogadom's con artists are easily recognized by their blissful glow, their smug and all-knowing conversation and theatrical walk. Many have neo-Sanskrit names, which they themselves find unpronounceable. The more money they have spent on becoming God, the more exaggerated their histrionics. The more money that is "donated" to line this or that *bhogi yogi's* pocket, the more the disciple becomes yogafied in feel-good self-promotion to a staged trance. The poser *guru’s* own lack of confidence in his little gods is seen by the fence he surrounds his disciples with like barnyard animals within the *ashram* walls. Sometimes, even this is not enough. Recently when one so-called god-man of India was accused of child molestation, the "master" ordered his disciples to observe vows of silence---to keep them from discussing the news--and to avoid the internet at the risk of ex-communication. For some strange reason no such restrictions were in place before the scandal broke, as long as the Godman could hear the singing of his glories from all corners.

At India's great religious fairs like the Kumbha Mela or Magha Mela, it is possible to have the *darshan* of many genuine spiritualists who practice various forms of *yoga: jnana, hatha, ashtanga, or bhakti*. However understanding these true ascetics and the land of Mother India that has given them birth, requires a radical mental adjustment for most Westerners. My own *guru maharaja*, while surveying the vast congregation of *yogis* at the Kumbha Mela, observed that the majority of them were genuine. But the problem for the Western seeker is that it will likely be the remaining minority who canvass him with flattering and wise-sounding quips about how to become God with an eight-minute meditation.

Once while traveling through the Khyber Pass on a bus, I noticed that a French couple in the seat in front of me had just discovered the loss of their gold pen. They were animatedly discussing each poor person they'd met, and which one could likely have stolen it. I leaned forward, "Did you meet anyone else?" "Yes, just one other young gentleman. But it couldn't have been him. He was rich and his father owned a bank." So I replied, "Then he is the culprit who stole your pen." It is often quite impossible to determine who is the actual poor man in South Asia. Generally, those who are well-to-do never boast. I continued, "Your smooth-talking acquaintance was probably a skilled con artist who targets innocent tourists." The way of the imitation *guru* is no different.

The truth is that invariably many posers will step forward just to cheat the innocent aspirant. What they usually have in common is an oversimplified method of instant *nirvana* combined with an urgent need for money. *Yoga* for suckers. Follow this logic: one such bogus incarnation told his disciples that the lamb is the symbol of Christ, it goes *baa baa* and since he himself is called Baba, he is therefore the Christ incarnate. As long as there is no end of *yoga* suckers, there will be no end of *yoga* cheaters.

It is a mistake therefore to assume that once having decided to adopt a spiritual path one will be automatically safe and protected. He who carelessly dives headfirst may find shallow waters. A few years down the road, abused and dejected spiritual dropouts are found reciting the usual pitiable *ashram* horror stories. The exact offenses of imposter *swamis* and holy mothers are too innumerable to recount. Many of their ridiculous antics in the theater of playing God would be funny except for the human life and dignity that has been trampled upon just to keep one quack *guru* propped up on his flimsy throne. Invariably at the root of it is a pretender's insatiable lust and greed, manifested as a desire for sex, money and power over vulnerable souls. Unfortunately, it is almost impossible to help others who will pay money to be flattered and lied to and turned into ten-minute incarnations of God for a price. At the end of the day, he who wants to be cheated will find a suitable cheater.

Out of a thousand such crimes that come to mind, the single most egregious assault upon *dharma* by a robed serpent of *swamidom* occurred around 1982 while I was in Lucknow. There was at that time a bogus *guru* with an even more bogus name. "Pilot Baba" was attracting a wide following upon his hollow promises of divine powers. This scoundrel adopted an innocent deaf and dumb man and, robing him in the saffron attire of a *yogi*, ceremoniously lowered him into the ground and buried him. Then Pilot Baba amidst much festivity announced that he would un-earth the mute some weeks later at which time he would peacefully awaken from his yogic trance. This unlicensed Pilot offered his foolish following a chance to sacrifice their hard-earned rupees at the burial site, promising that whatever they gave would be returned many times over. Word spread quickly and soon there was a queue of illiterate yet greedy blockheads eager to throw whatever rupees they could afford in hopes of future wealth. Eventually a bad smell began to rise from the subterranean meditation chamber which even Pilot Baba with his cunning silver tongue had a hard time explaining away. Soon an angry mob of ponzi scheme disciples took a shovel to the site. To their horror found that the deaf mute *''yogi''* was in an advanced stage of decay. Yet the murderous Pilot Baba was nowhere to be found; he had absconded with every rupee.

I have come across many of the imitation variety of *yogis* over the years, but the most comical was the *baba*--make that buffoon--of Sai Land. Sai Land was a desolate portion of beach north of Bombay that had been roped off to rope in fools. Situated not far from the boats of fishermen, this beach was an unlikely site for a *yogi* in meditation. But the Sai Land Baba was a fisherman in his own right looking to hook suckers. As I was strolling along the shore with a friend sharing a morning *japa* walk, the *baba's* English-speaking cohort approached us, and in broken syllables he urged us to have *darshan* of the Sai Land *baba*. There on the other side of the rope, the empowered Baba sat pensively before cold embers. In hushed, confidential tones the henchman communicated to us that *baba* was such an astute *yogi* he could tie his penis to the bumper of a car and pull the car by walking backwards. As proof of the Baba's mystic abilities, he would condescend to cut an unpeeled banana inside its skin simply by chanting *mantras*.

The *baba* huffed and puffed, tossed some ash from his dead fire over three carefully laid bananas, and whispered a few unintelligible incantations. Then with a somber glance and black finger he sternly ordered me to peel one of the bananas. Lo and behold, the banana inside was actually indeed cut in three." So when will the *baba* pull the car with his *lulu*?" I asked the stooge using an Indian colloquial term for the male member. By now I was having difficulty keeping a straight face, but--blinded by greed--my sarcasm had flown over his head.

"Yes, yes. Baba is eager to demonstrate his power of *yoga*," came the equally straight-faced reply. "But it will take one month of rigid *tapasya*. You now give baba 200 rupees. Funds he will use for maintaining Sai Land as he readies for strenuous exercise."

By way of replying, I pointed to two pinholes in the bananas' peels by which the two fruits had been earlier cut with a needle through the skin. No longer could I contain myself, and laughing out loud like one who had just enjoyed a good joke, I put down the fruit casually refusing to eat a piece as "*prasad*." The two hucksters that day were left without even a rupee for their grand show. The *baba's* head was now beginning to swiftly bob side to side, his detached pose now having been replaced by an angry let's-get-back-to-the-drawing-board expression. A few weeks later I chanced to walk up the same wide, palm-lined beach and found no sign of Sai Land. The old fraud must have known that the show must move on. He had pulled up stakes for good and the monsoon high tide had washed away any traces of the imposter.

We read in the *Ramayana*, that when the demon Ravana came to kidnap the Goddess of Fortune Sita, he did so in the saffron attire of an ascetic holy man. Today in every corner of Earth cheating in the name of religion is a common practice. Even true renunciates have been waylaid by worldly desires.

The quest for the Absolute Truth--inner transcendence and spiritual bliss--rises above mundane religion, dogma, mere morality and altruism performed for the benefit of other conditioned souls. The ultimate satisfaction of inner illumination, as stated in the 718 verses of *Bhagavad Gita As It Is*, rests in humbling oneself in a mood of surrender before the Supreme Lord in an attitude of service and devotion. The *yoga* of pure love of God is far above ill-considered sectarianism: it is the eternal force and function of a soul who shares in the dedicated quest for service to the One without a second. That *jiva* soul who has achieved an affectionate, personal reciprocation with the spiritual whole, the Supreme Personality of Godhead Krishna, is the true knower of *yoga*. Above the *yoga* of work called *karma*, or the *yoga* of asceticism called *hatha*, or the *yoga* of wisdom called *jnana*, is the *yoga* of ecstatic devotional love called *bhakti*.

Finding a true, disinterested and transcendental *guru* and guide on the path to *samadhi*, wherein the mind becomes lovingly fixed upon the Lord's service is the only key to unlocking the gate on the royal road to enlightenment. In China this path is called Tao, "the way" or "the path." Thousands of years ago Chinese monks made the arduous and hazardous trek through the valleys of the Himalayas and came to India, the Motherland of civilization to study *dharma*. Over time, this word *dharma*, the spiritual path, became mispronounced in China as Tao.

In fact, the concept of *dharma*, loosely translated as "religion," actually explains the original transcendental work of the anti-material being. Just as fire stays always hot and ice remains cold, so the awakening of *dharma* is the unchanging and sustaining nature of the soul now hidden behind the curtain of Maya. *Dharma* is sometimes confused as one sectarian religion or another, but superficial concepts of religion can never satisfy the quest of the *atma* particle searching for the ultimate union-in-separation with the Spiritual Whole. True *dharma* is *sanatan-dharma*, the never ending religion of ecstatic love of God.

Likewise, the Chinese monks were aware of the system of *dhyana* or meditation, which they mispronounced as "Zen." Hence "Tao of religion" or "Zen meditation" are repetitive phrases. Just as these transcendental concepts spread to China from India, so were they carried thousands of years ago to the Near East, to Northern Africa, and on into Europe. The archaeological evidence relating to India's cultural gifts, though profound, has been ignored by scholars who are unwilling to put aside bias. Not only do India's Sanskrit histories, the *Puranas*, give accounts of ancient Vedic civilizations far from what is today known as India, but ruins of old temples in the near East, and the Sanskrit inscription at the fire temple near Baku, Azerbaijan confirm the practice of *sanatan dharma* in distant regions. Although Vedic civilization has been narrowed, India's torchlight message of *Bhagavad Gita* lives on.

The *Bhagavad Gita* has seen as much abuse as any other scripture of the world in the hands of self-interested men. To give the appearance of piety, politicians even today enjoy waving the *Gita* at rallies. University scholars both in the East as well as in the West seek acceptance by continuing to offer novel, self-serving interpretations, which have nothing to do with Lord Krishna’s actual message to Arjuna: *mam ekam saranam vraja*. "Just surrender unto Me." Posers still twist the purport of the *Gita* just to waylay the aspirant into their prehensile grasp. Though truth is everywhere, truth can be difficult to find like light that takes on the color of glass through which it filters. To know India's culture, one must learn the *yoga* of the *Bhagavad Gita As It Is*, and this is no easy task.

There is an allegory about a sea turtle that comes up for air only once each year. Somewhere floating upon the great sea where the turtle lives is a piece of driftwood with a knothole. The allegory asks, what are the chances that the turtle's head might poke through the hole in the wood? The answer: exactly the same as a seeker has of meeting a genuine spiritual master, a realized devotee of the Lord. The odds of stumbling upon the Absolute Truth in the forest of delusion are few, but if the aspirant is sincere, the indwelling Lord will give guidance from within.

As mentioned a while back, it is the difference between the way that life's stage play is acted out here in India, and the routine a foreigner calls normal, that can literally cause a Westerner to go mad. All this serves to underscore the would-be *yogi's* need for a *guru* and guide. Flip-outs are quite common among wayfarers here who expect their trip to glide along in some predictable routine.

I once saw an American actually go berserk right inside a police station, in front of the commanding officer of a Bombay precinct. While I was having a chat with the cops, in walks a relic of 60's America, protesting everything that, according to his view, was wrong with India. Junior officers bristled as they were forced to watch their commanding officer brook a barrage of verbal assaults. Finally the crazed hippie ripped off his shirt and prepared to throw it at the cops. I decided to do what I had seen so many gentle Hindus do in the face of one of their own offensive brethren. I stood up, turned the startled hippie about, marched him out the door, and tossed his shirt in his direction. Then I turned the cops, and with my best Hindu manners said, "Please forgive my fellow American for his misdeeds." As my blanket apology was accepted, I reflected that had I had a Hindu mother, she would have been proud of me.

Had I not intervened, the bewildered adventurer would have spent a maybe a month in a dungeon-like jail before anyone from his embassy got wind of it. But the point is that without a guide India and its ways are confusing and even flabbergasting to the uninitiated. Just as Lowell Thomas was grateful for his guide in the 1920's, half a century later as I straddle my Yezdi at the stoplight in Nagpur I am no different. Without the grace of Shri Guru all that is to be seen in India would appear as mad and ridiculous as the antics of mental patients. But there is a method to the madness of India, a sublime reason for its ways. Appreciating that there is order in the chaos of Mother India requires not only an open mind, but eyes, too, that have been forcibly opened by the torchlight of knowledge.

Self-realization is the purpose of this human form of life. The word "man" has been derived from the Sanskrit *manasa*, meaning "mind." The lucky soul who has achieved this rare human form of birth should live thoughtfully, considering the consequences of mindless sense gratification. It is this ability to think and to reason that separates man from the animals. Perfection of reasoning and intelligence is found in the achievement of *yoga*. *Yoga* does not mean a mindless twisting of the body or elaborate enjoyment of sex, a so-called pleasure which is freely available to dogs and cats. *Yoga* is final emancipation from birth and death through linking to Krishna.

And so it is that I have usually been able to feel fortunate while traveling in India, but here I must emphasize "usually." For even the toughest, shrewdest and most savvy travelers, India can be a very daunting challenge at best. Forget about trying to solve the myriad of India's problems; just by coming here you are going to invariably find a new set of your own problems that you never knew existed. Fledgling travelers are quickly separated from the pros in short order. Enlightenment, spiritual elevation and *samadhi* come with a stiff price tag. Tolerance is the key. When nagging problems arise--or even if disaster strikes--the lone wayfarer must repeat to himself *mantra*-like, "This is all happening to me due to my own *karma*. Had I not caused difficulties to other living entities in my past life, then today I would not be suffering the reactions to my transgressions. My own arrogance is the root of my own pain. I will pray to the Supreme Lord to protect me. I will try to be humble in the future."

Nonetheless, it is better for beginners here to travel first class, to stay in proper hotels, to associate exclusively with the cultured and educated, and as a general rule to avoid diving headfirst into murky waters. If and when you get your wheels in India, go practice riding on the left side of the road on some out of the way country lane. For the motorcyclist, India is the speed freak's nightmare and the lane-splitter's paradise. In the final analysis, India is a very, very dangerous place, a razor's edge from which you can easily slip and crash at any moment.

Extensive travel in India has left me with a bulging portfolio of horror stories. I usually do not discuss them simply because they will not be believed in the coddled and nourished West. Here in the land I have somehow grown to love I have been at the center of three major riots and have been held prisoner against my will thrice. I'll never forget the first time that I saw a dead man laying on the road, flies allover him, waiting patiently for the *chandalas* to cremate him. People walked by pretending not to notice. In Ayodhya, while having a refreshing dip in an inviting pond I noticed a friendly stranger who had built a fire on the on the shore. Swimming to that side to warm myself I spotted a leg protruding from the fire. I was unwittingly attending a funeral. No mourners were present. Having died alone in this world, this gentleman had nonetheless died gloriously, for he had left this world from the city that was once ruled by Lord Rama.

On another occasion I toured the birthplace of Lord Krishna, Mathura, with a God-brother. The next day he continued the tour intending to visit the temple of Govindaji in Jaipur six hours to the south. On the way his bus crashed, and my friend from that day began the rest of his young life in a wheel chair, paralyzed from the waist down.

In this wonderful, horrible country I have suffered many a serious illness. I was in an accident in here I was treated by a quack doctor with sugar pills for a week while hemorrhaging, an injury that later had to surgically corrected at Jaslok in Bombay by the able hands of Dr. Thakurlal Motwani. Once along with my *gurubhai*--Godbrother--Vaikunthanath, we decided to jump into the flooded Yamuna River at Vrindavana and let the current carry us the ten miles am to Mathura. We were wearing *gumchas*, small waist cloths, and the plan was to get a cycle rickshaw at the other end. The river was very deep; we were excited to be swimming over the tops of trees, yet the current was not treacherous. At times villagers would notice two foreigners dog paddling in a place where we should not have been. They would come out and shout warnings to us in Hindi. I thought they were cautioning us about the current, but we discovered later that what they were saying was this: "Cobras have been washed out of their holes, they are quite perturbed, and you are swimming with them!"

In Bombay I once witnessed a man beaten to death within arm's length. with a silly half-grin on his face, falling like a felled tree, his head bouncing upon the cold concrete like a rubber ball. It would have been comical and cartoon-like, if it were not for the stark reality of life's final curtain falling before me with all its irrevocability. When I was asked by the police to my version of the events as a witness, I was instead accused of the murder, for every Indian knows that all white men are filthy rich and will bribe everyone in sight. I hardly had two rupees to rub together, so I was thrown for some weeks in a horrible jail with several others who were foolish enough to come forward as witnesses. We spent the next year on trial for murder. Can you believe that in Bombay jail, this so-called "lock-up," 120 men lived in four small cells? There was water for only one hour daily, and the lone toilet was a hole in the middle of the floor. By midday a 24-inch fetid mound of fecal matter had arisen above the hole. The worst part was that to get to the "toilet," you had to wade through a 6-foot wide 3-inch deep puddle of urine. No, there was no exercise room, legal library or television. When ordinary citizens didn't have these things, why should prisoners? If many Indians must go without water, then certainly prisoners should, too, guilty or not. In America if a pet is treated the way Indian prisoners are treated, then the owner himself would be jailed, but at least he would have a clean cell with a bed and his own toilet.

At the murder trial, six foreigners and two Indians stood accused. I was aghast as the prosecutor produced blood-soaked garments of the many "victims." "Where did those come from," I asked our attorney. "They are soaked in chicken blood," he explained. "It is a very common practice." At one point in the trial, an agitated witness pointed at me, accusing me in Marathi. Later I was narrated a translation of the "testimony." The witness had said under oath that I had beaten him on the back with a stick pointing to the chicken blood that was spilled all over the back of the shirt. My lawyer then asked him, "Since you were being beaten on the back, then how can you identify whoever struck you?" Eventually we six foreigners--"the Bombay Six"-were found not guilty. Adhikary, a Bengali *brahmana*, was not so lucky. He was found guilty of the murder, and in proper Indian legal fashion, he was the wrong man. Within a few weeks, I was on my way out of India to Bangkok, though the memory remained as haunting flashbacks of post traumatic stress syndrome: years later I thought that I spotted Adhikary, at a Bombay train station. I grabbed him and hugged him, kissing his face emotionally. Then I realized that I, too, had the wrong man: it was some friend of a friend coming to greet me.

During the trial I had met a cargo pilot in his fifties who back in America had been fired as an airlines pilot due to his age. He now flew a DC-3 full of goats from India to Arabia. He offered to smuggle me out of India as part of his crew to escape a murder conviction. Had I accepted, and it was tempting, then the other five non-Indian *guru-bhais* on trial with me would have all been rounded up and immediately imprisoned. So I put my faith in God and stayed, and that faith was rewarded. This pilot told me that "the world ends at Istanbul and begins again at Bangkok." But that is the great thing about India, you are not exactly in the world. You must seek the shelter and protection of Krishna.

In the 70’s the Maharashtrian State government wanted to change the name of a certain college. A huge riot ensued in which hundreds were killed. Perhaps most of the dead had never been to college. But this is how riots can happen in this volatile country and the wary visitor must understand this.

The worst riot I ever "attended" was in one of the world's most over-populated places, at the most crowded place and time. It was Calcutta's Sealdah Station during evening rush hour. Apparently some of my fellow passengers waiting for the 8:00 pm train did not like Americans, for the local government was officially Communist then, and a riot erupted out of nowhere. We ran out of the station and jumped into a taxi, which the crowd of hundreds proceeded to block and destroy in short order, smashing every window. We decided to confront the mob so we exited the taxi, the windows of which were now in little pieces.

Just a few days earlier, I had read an account of how Gandhi had quelled a mob simply by chanting the name of "Rama." At least if it did not work, I figured, I would die hearing the holy name of God, so I began shouting "Rama" at the top of my voice. But my chanting only served to infuriate the mob all the more. Things had changed in the half since the time of the Mahatma.

At the time of the riot, Indian cars still had a long steel rod with which to manually crank over the engine in case of a dead battery. The mob opened the trunk with the express purpose of getting something with which to beat us to death. Eventually I was stripped bare naked before the crowd of thousands. The rioting went on for forty-five minutes

Eventually, the police showed up and formed a "*lathi*-charge," by weighing into the crowd with long hard wooden sticks called *lathis*. Hundreds of chappels, or leather sandals, were left on the street as hundreds of cowards ran home barefoot to their mothers. Bengal, home of Calcutta, is the extreme zone in a land of extremes. At the other end of the spectrum, some of the world's greatest writers, artists, philosophers and *yogis* have been Bengalis.

I am more cautious now in my view of India, though I have been drawn back here time and again over the years (though I have no plans ever to return to Calcutta). What is the mystical lure of India? Why does the lotus grow in the mud? I know first hand that this country can be cruel and violent, terribly so. But I am neither complaining nor bragging. Complaining would mean that the humble message of *mea culpa* was not learned, thereby inviting more unwelcome visitations of past bad *karma* until the lessons sunk in. Undeniably, that which is worth having is worth the fight and the risk. Is this not the message of *Bhagavad Gita*? There upon the Battlefield of Kurukshetra, Shri Krishna did not instruct His warrior-disciple Prince Arjuna to become a monk and retire to some mountain cave. The Lord ordered, "Fight the good battle, Arjuna, the win the good fight. Do your duty and do not be a coward. Unmanliness does not become a *kshatriya*. I am with you."

Would-be Western *yogis* who are unhappy in India and were lured here by a storefront *guru* do have my sympathy. Many would be better suited to a packaged adventure in Disneyland, which is what most *swami's* in the West offer anyway. In India if something goes wrong, you are at the mercy of your own fate: you do not consult an attorney or insurance agent, you consult your *karma*. Still, for me at least, the good experiences have edged out the bad ones. The real *yogis*, whom it has been my rare privilege to meet, the Vedic scholars who have instructed me, the tens of thousands of holy places and temples unto which I have bowed, the sacred lore that has opened up its mysterious yet universal truths to me, and everywhere the brown-eyed children that have called me "uncle:" all these have all made India a wonderful and memorable ride. Neither is there anything in this world for which I would trade my India experience. My regret is that I do not have enough years in this body to relish the divine truths of this land and its ways over and over again.

Besides, living in America and Europe is not always easy either, though modern technology is ever working to make it as humdrum and predictable as possible. The old book of moral tales *Hitopadesh* of Narayana relates the story of a bald man whose scalp was baked by the sun. He escaped to the shelter and shade of a coconut tree at the same moment a coconut fell. Impelled by destiny it landed upon the man's head. *Karma* will visit the doer for better or worse sooner or later, no matter where he tries to hide. Pointing an accusing finger at India for her supposed affronts to me would only point three fingers at myself. Pausing for a moment, I would remember such times as when I have seen people shot while seated next to me in North Carolina, or have died in front of me of stab wounds in San Francisco. Or of the several times I have been attacked and beaten in New York, or robbed in California. The near shipwreck on the English Channel, or the 7.1 San Francisco earthquake I survived. The suicides I have chanced to witness on two American bridges, or the headless corpse of my friend who seconds before was resting beside me in the speeding van. How is it that when these things happen in India, we blame it on a third world lack of development, and when they happen back home we close our eyes and pretend that we will still be somehow protected by fallible science, our bipartisan government and top heavy health plans?

*Karma* will follow the doer to any part of the world, or material universe, or even into outer space. That place where we can hide from the reactions to our past transgressions over the rights of other living entities has yet to be created. Each of our acts is engraved into the ethereal or *akashic* record to be read by the King of Death, Yama, at some future date. Like seeds that will fructify in due time, that which we have done today has indelibly created our tomorrow. Temporary enjoyment or suffering, happiness and distress, all these are results of that which we have done before. Noting the transitory state of joy and sorrow, the true *yogi* eschews both and prays only to sip the nectar of eternal knowledge. The middle road of detached renunciation is the transcendental way to the eternal world hidden beyond all reactions to our transgressions. Whenever I see the *dhobi* washermen pounding out the dirt from white *dhotis* upon the river's shore, I am reminded that the purpose of the pilgrimage, the reason I turn the throttle, is to pummel the layers of my sins buried for lifetimes and render this soul clean again.

It’s amazing what thoughts can roll through your head at an Indian stoplight. During the reign of Mataji, the formidable Indira Gandhi, India manufactured only two cars, the Padmini and the Ambassador. The Padmini was-and is--an Indian Fiat. The Ambassador, the preferred vehicle for government officials, is a 1951 Austin Princess still made this day. Like the borrowed motorcycle cycle designs of the Czech Jawa or the Royal Enfield, so have cars been neatly Xeroxed.

But with the booming silicon chip based economy of the 1980's, many Indians became relatively rich by heading to America. By that time Prime Minister Indira Gandhi had been gunned down at her Delhi home by her disgruntled Sikh bodyguards. By that time, too, her son Sanjay, who had been groomed for the post of Prime Minister, had died while performing a loop in his private plane. To fill the dynastic void, Indira's younger son Rajiv gave up his career as a commercial pilot and was called to enter politics, becoming Prime Minister from 1984 till 1989. This grandson of Jawaharlal Nehru was the son of a Zoroastrian father and Hindu *brahmana* mother. He was married to a Cambridge-educated Catholic beauty from Italy. Before Rajiv followed his mother to martyrdom, this time by Tamil separatists from Shri Lanka, the new India of foreign goods and cars he created, combined with a flow of silicon-based American dollars, created a formula for crowded highways. Families that once could barely afford a scooter now motored about in chauffeured cars. The problem was, the home government had failed to keep up and there were not any real highways for all the new automobiles. Except around Delhi, Rajiv and his family had lived; India had no decent roads to speak of.

By the 21stcentury, cars upon India's roadways included domestically built Fords, Hyundais, Mitsubishis, Suzukis, Daiwoos, and even Mercedes. Truck companies like Tata, Fairdeal, and Hindustan were now manufacturing roomy utilitarian wagons and sedans. Yet despite the tax-base advantage that the government has received from the sale and import of so many cars and trucks, relatively little has been spent on either roadway improvement or driver education. Neither has the issue of passenger protection been given much thought by politicians whose only real job is to hold onto power.

Just as in the old days when overloaded bullocks were worked to death despite Jain posters in the marketplace warning farmers of sorrowful next life retribution, so today trucks and buses are heaped with goods and human cargo until their suspension systems simply give out. Encumbered behemoths carelessly wobble down worn-out roadways at terrific speeds, leaning obliquely to one side because of broken springs and dead shocks. Buses with welded iron bars that block windows jail-cell-like roll off bridges, regularly drowning fifty or sixty at a time with no hope of escape. Mothers walking their toddlers meander in and out of traffic as though the street was their dowry gift at the time of marriage. While overworked gangs of jungle tribals widen roads, utility poles are often left where they are. You can be blissfully buzzing down a strip of virgin asphalt only to find a steel pole like some forgotten ancient relic coming at you inches from your unhelmeted head. India's vehicles are her PCD's, her "population control devices."

Now, the technology that is employed by the PCD is of a two-pronged, dual-function design: within and without. The incredibly unsafe Maruti Suzuki van, pioneered by Sanjay Gandhi before his plane crashed, appears to have been designed for this purpose. In this tiny, lightly metal led rectangular box balanced upon slight 12-inch wheels, a casual fender bender could turn fatal. Even if there were seat belts which Indian cars just do not have, they would be of little use to front passengers whose faces hover mere inches from the windshield and the next life.

With the novel thought of pollution control merely a recent phenomenon, Delhi has become a world-leader in diesel cancer. Neither clogged lanes, nor clogged lungs, were factored into India's formula for prosperity. Perhaps the awful polluted, crashing mess that is India's urban boulevard was indeed created by design. In the 70's when India's population was around 750 million, Mrs. Gandhi forced stringent population control measures upon her unwilling subjects. Obligatory abortions, sterilizations and sex education were the order of the day in a society for whom discussion of sex had been all but taboo just a few years earlier. Despite all this, one fifth of the world became Indian when this land's population crossed the one billion mark in 2000. Perhaps it is no coincidence that India's population increased by a quarter billion after Bollywood beauties began aping Michael Jackson's gyrations with the air.

Could it be that the politicians have now just quietly turned to India's cars, her PCDs', for a solution? Perhaps it is by design that India has the most dangerous roadways in the world. After all, Indians are highly intelligent and form a huge percentage of the world's professionals, especially in the fields of medicine and engineering. Indians in America enjoy reminding their neighbors of the high odds of meeting an Indian medico should they ever wind up in an emergency room. Since it is unlikely that such intelligent people, or anyone else for that matter, could have allowed such chaotic thoroughfares to develop accidentally, therefore India's roads must be part of some master plan of the government to control population. There can be no other explanation.

From the saddle of indigenous motorcycles and scooters, I have studied India's roads first hand and might hazard a few suggestions to streamline the program. First, I would submit that all four-wheeled vehicles totally eliminate braking systems. The "out and about" members of India’s emerging middle class do not fancy braking their cars anyway. For them, a pleasant drive means careening from side to side through intersections causing hapless pedestrians to scatter like bowling pins. Here in India, the unwritten rule is that he who by good *karma* has achieved the elevated status of automobile ownership has the divine right plow past every un-policed traffic light, run over every rule of the road and crush every human sensibility beneath his tyres. To maximize the value of an Indian automobile, braking system should be officially designated not only a waste of money, but a nuisance. Brakes constitute a useless and expensive luxury upon which no frugal India driver should be forced to throwaway his hard-earned rupees.

An Indian car is an unstoppable object piloted by an upper caste man over the lower members of a society whose demeaning status has been ordained by birth. His car is unstoppable, that is, until the destined moment arrives when the vehicle and all its tightly-packed passengers must confront their fated immovable object. This eventuality becomes a time not for mourning, but for a meditative and reflective celebration of the completion of another round of *samsara*, life’s endless cyclic journey, when the car's remains are sent for their round of recycling and the passengers' remains to the burning *ghats*. Through the clever removal hydraulic braking system, the inevitable date draws nearer, and so much the sooner is the ultimate purpose of the PCD achieved.

In Delhi, the nation's capitol, there is a "population clock" which calculates the number of Indians via a birth rate vs. death rate formula. In the fall of 2000, on the day before 999,999,999 was to turn into a family of one billion Indians, the clock broke down sending repairmen scurrying to fix it in time for that magical birth in some God-forsaken slum or royal palace. One can only imagine the delight of the population clock-watching doomsayers, the modern pro-abortion and pro-sterilization denizens of this varied land, who, upon seeing the brakeless PCD plan in effect, watch the clock count backwards. What spaying and neutering have magnificently achieved for America’s pet population, so the brakeless PCD can do for India. Just as genital surgeries were forced upon men in the 70's to limit daddyhood, so it could be decreed that every upwardly mobile family in India be forced to own a PCD. Forced abortions have only created a vicious cycle whereby the conspiring politicians, doctors and their henchmen have, after their own deaths, only returned to wombs destined for fetal disposal. In other words, the 1970's abortion advocates have not gone to their Maker, but are now repeatedly the *karmically* reincarnated abortions of this century. The PCD solution to the very real problem of too many Indians and not enough chiefs should be given a thorough examination. Through its impletion, India will go from third world hell-hole to a heaven on earth with and room and breathing space for all Indians, at least those who don't own a PCD.

A huge, stinking cloud of choking exhaust fumes from the bus next to me, whose tail pipe is aimed directly at my nose, suddenly shakes me from my reverie. PCD's indeed! The light has turned green and the unstoppable charge of humanity, like dirt bike kids at the Lake Elsinore rally, has begun its great rush forward. In this Oklahoma-style stampede, he who falters or hesitates could get ground into the asphalt of a Nagpur roadway before he can mutter Hare Rama!

Suddenly it appears that I have grabbed the attention of the angry-looking, sweaty cop in the crossroads. I see the dark slits of his eyes focus on my Yezdi and me. He is trying to second-guess my every move. I turn my head slightly, pretending to be more involved with the tide of motorized humanity encircling me, than his dire need for one more hundred rupee note. The cops at this particular outpost have neither jeeps nor motorcycles to chase down any motorized vehicle. As I begin to flank widely around him, his obvious anger intensifies as his two sinewy arms helplessly flail the dust clouds swirling about him ordering me to halt. At the last moment I pretend to have just noticed his gymnastics, and then do what any civilized Indian would do. I twist the throttle with my right hand and wave with my left. I delight at watching his stern look turn to disappointment as he realizes he will have to explain to his half dozen superiors why he let Rs 100 ride away. Passing him, I look back and give him a big, toothy grin and shout, "Namaste! Jai Hind! Hari Bol!"

## Epilogue

## "Yoga Is"

SAGES, HAVING ACHIEVED TRANSCENDENCE, teach that every activity should be directed towards achieving *yoga*. Whether breathing, eating, working to raise a family or taking the daily motorcycle ride, the quest for *yoga* is paramount. *Yoga* is realized when one is not only in harmony in Nature, but is transcendent to Nature. Many points are crossed in the great climb towards *yoga*, yet the goal of the practice is one.

The true *yogi* aspires neither to make the Sun rise nor set; he is more concerned with the mastery of his senses than mastering the Universe. In his heart of hearts he adores the Supreme Person whose inconceivable energies are the creative force behind all harmony and disharmony of the cyclic worlds. He who understands that the strings of illusion by which we are now controlled must be exchanged for the transcendental strings that are manipulated by the Supreme Puppeteer, alone knows *yoga*. Although the achievement of *yoga* grants freedom from Nature even while living within Nature, it can never grant freedom from the spiritual moorings, that to which we are heirs, as parts and parcels of the Supreme All-transcendent Spirit Personified.

Yet the knowledge of higher consciousness comes with a stiff price tag. In the quest for *yoga,* liberation in spirit is awakened to the degree that transitory pleasures are sacrificed here in the body. It is not possible to go to sleep on the lap of illusion and wake up in the arms of God. Since higher consciousness cannot be purchased at the mall, we have nothing to offer the All-transcendent Master of Yoga but our *bhakti*, loving service, in reciprocation for His supreme love. The goodness of India, the land of *yoga*, lies hidden behind a curtain of dogs and hungry pigs. In the same way, the true *yogi* can be impossible to recognize. Many *yogis* appear to be as ordinary as the next man, yet they silently tread a thousand steps upon the path of piety in the course of a day. In the world of *yoga*, the true adept may be hidden.

Although the goal of *yoga* is one, and the consciousness is fixed, the passage to *yoga* is fluid and flexible. Since each one of us is an individual, each path and relationship with God must be unique. There exists individuality of each soul or living entity because personality exists in the Supreme Individual. For the *moto-yogi*, the daily ride can be a trance-like appreciation of the Hands that control Nature in all direction, including the the deputed agents of the Supreme Lord that control the sky, the wind and the breath that nourishes the soul. Taking control of the handlebars also means we can pilot ourselves for a time away from the insane world we are told to accept as normal. On a motorcycle we must confront our own selves with our own balance in some new place we might never before have seen:

*Yoga* is that force which is communicated like some very subtle electricity through the wire of initiation by the bona fide *guru.*

*Yoga* is achievable only in this human form of existence*.*

*Yoga* is the only means to know the Yogeshwar.

*Yoga* is the performance of every deed and activity for the satisfaction of the spirit.

*Yoga* is that force that re-links the fragmented infinitesimal spirit soul with the Supreme Spirit Whole.

*Yoga* is the knowledge that you are consciousness, or rather that your consciousness is the symptom of the *atma*--your very self.

*Yoga* is an agreement to accept the shelter of He who shelters the Universe.

*Yoga* is an awakening of the inner principle, a slow and steady movement towards transcendence.

*Yoga* is knowing your place in the cosmos and beyond the cosmos.

*Yoga* is the transcending of *maha-maya* to dwell in *yogamaya*.

*Yoga* is vertical, whereas illusion is horizontal.

*Yoga* answers the questions: Who am I? Where have I come from? Where Am I going?

*Yoga* is the ride of a lifetime, the ride into infinity.

*Yoga* is achieved through the *maha mantra* in *kali yuga*.

*Yoga* is that which Arjuna found riding in the chariot driven by Krishna the Battle of Kurukshetra.

*Yoga* is becoming Krishna conscious.

## Afterword

We, who are such slaves to time, to knowing it, to saving it, to managing it, to chasing it, to not having enough of it ...

We, who are so obsessed with getting and spending, making and breaking, compiling wealth and defiling our health, having it all, and dreaming of more ...

We, who are so afraid to be alone that we can't complete a frantic commute without holding a cell phone to our ear...

We, who will starve whole populations to teach them democracy and wreck entire nations to advance the cause of capitalism...,

We, who think that might is right, greed is good, cash is king, time is money, speed is keen, bigger is better, yesterday is gone and tomorrow is another day ...

We, who are so confused by ego that we actually believe we shall drag our wretched personalities into the next life to be swathed in peace and tranquility, which we so desperately avoid in the here and now ...

We, who are so busy contemplating our Roundel, tattooing ourselves with the bar and shield, ever seeking more speed, better handling, higher tech, goofier gewgaws and louder pipes ...

We, whose idea of meditation is chasing our own tails until we disappear up our... deep breath

Perhaps we could learn something by boarding a little low-tech Bullet and riding slowly and alone into the vast, timeless, mysterious, and frightening sub-continent of our own souls, with no GPS to guide us, no ABS to save us, and nothing driving our progress except the impending monsoon season of our own death.

As chance would have it, I had the privilege of reading the final proof pages of this compelling book during a week when I periodically returned to my computer screen to follow reports of the 2003 Iron Butt Rally where it seemed that men and women were literally and deliberately chasing Samsara.

Oh my, what a purple contrast! Pavandas, the motorcycle *yogi* aboard his little Enfield, made me understand and grieve the frantic lives we lead.
