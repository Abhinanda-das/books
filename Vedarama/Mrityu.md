# Mrityu

## Introduction

Yama is a diety who inspires dread. In India, talking about him or simply pronouncing his name is avoided even today. Paradoxically, the phenomenon ofdeath doesn't cause a similar fear in the soul of Indians. Accepted with what Westerners would define an attitude of'fatalism', it is considered a natural event, a part oflife, just like poverty, sickness, and old age. On the contrary, the god of death is the mysterious principle of transformation, not to be taken lightly, especially when the physical and metaphysical laws which he governs are unknown. This is probably one ofthe reasons why traditional authors and Indian scholars have carefully avoided a study ofYama and the deities of his retinue; whereas, on the contrary, they have devoted themselves to a detailed description of various posthumous destinies.

In the Western world the opposite tendency has prevailed. Death is an abstract, philosophical subject of study, which is tackled from a speculative point of view, and to which no immediate efficacy is attributed. Actually, the curious illusion underlying this intellectualistic approach, originates from a morbid desire of bodily immortality. If death is undeniably a universal phenomenon, it is constantly exorcized and shunned in our daily experience. Various interesting studies have been written on death in the West, and quite revealingly they are all focused on sociological, ethnological, and psychological analyses. These studies confirm what we ascertain in everyday life, in which hospitals are viewed as 'warehouses' of the dying. This approach spares Westerners from experiencing death in their own homes. Such an aseptic death brings with it the simplification offuneral rites and the abolition ofmourning. Of great importance is the unlimited trust which Westerners place in medicine. Medicine is perceived as the instrument ofachieving an indefmite prolongation oflife, in the irrational hope for the discovery ofan elixir of eternal youth. This attitute is symptom of the fear of death, not to be disjoined from the horror — even ofan aesthetic kind — that the West manifests towards poverty, sickness, and old age.

We thought it would be interesting to present this study, intending to expound and analyze the phenomenon of death from the Indian point ofview. In this regard, we must make clear from the outset that what has been examined is contemporary Hinduism, studied on the basis ofits living rituals and beliefs. The briefreferences to the Buddhist or Jaina have been utilized only as useful explanatory devices. After all, both Buddhism and Jainism have such great relevance in and of themselves, that to study their specific outlook on the subject would have required a separate treatment. The material gathered — both written and oral — has been read in the light of the plurimillennial living tradition ofthe Hindus, having their roots in the oldest times oflndus civilization and in the Vedic Texts. It must thus be clear that the interpretation of the old texts confided to us by our informants is the contemporary one. In other words, the method ofresearch has been that examining the phenomenon of death in today's India, registering its influences on the social environment, and then searching, when possible, for its oldest cultural roots. For this reason, we have verified the texts and our informations from their original Sanskrit. On the other hand, we have utilized historical translations ofworks which were doctrinally less relevant. Our research has been carried out primarily as an Indological enterprise rather than an ethnological one, although to be sure, ethnological considerations have often been employed. This has been done precisely in order to study the origins of the speculations about death. The result of this study has been gratifying and surprising: in fact, the thought ofmodern Hindus appears to be very close to that of their ancestors of proto-historic times. Indeed, although in today's India Western mentality sometimes has affirmed itself, especially in urban centres, in rural areas traditional life — with its rhythms, rituals, and beliefs going back to time immemorial — has been maintained,

*Mrtyu: Concept of Death* cannot of course end here. Our research has taken into consideration only the moments from conception to the death ofthe body, passing through the phases of birth, growth and ageing. We have also shortly examined the journey which the soul makes as soon as it leaves the body. It is however evident that the speculations and beliefs concerning the posthumous destinies, and the issues of and liberation require further exploration. A lot of work awaits us in the future.

In our research we have taken into consideration all those studies on the subject which have appeared both in India and the West. We have utilized these sources, adapting them to the perspective of our enquiry, going back in time and comparing present beliefs with the literature and the artistic documents of the past. Thus, we have not thought it appropriate to take into account, in this first inquiry, those studies following a purely philosophical perspective: we shall perhaps utilize these materials for our future research. We have avoided taking into consideration those popular texts dealing with 'apparent death', 'reincarnation', and 'talking with the dead'.

The Sanskrit texts used during the research appear in a special list in Bibliography. We utilized also the historical translations, quoted in the second list of Bibliography. The system oftransliteration in internationally known and used in specialized works. We didn't decline the Sanskrit nouns, using for their plural the English form.

We hope that this study on death in Hindu civilization stimulates a comparative reflection even in non-specialist Western readers. For this reason, we have reduced technical language to a minimum, avoiding the flexion ofSanskrit terms and mimmizing the use of Indological parlance. The aim has been to maintain maxhnum scientific rigour with readability. We have adopted the system of Sanskrit transliteration most widely in use, known nowadays even to the non-specialist reader, the pronunciation of which is found in all works of Indology.

## 1 - The Human Being (manusya) and the Other Beings

Nevertheless, such a statement, due to anexcessive schematism, appears too generic, since the points ofview which have developed over the centuries are many and complex.

Rather than opposing or relating to each other, death and life tend to superimpose in pan-Indian thought, appearing as two aspects ofa single phenomenon considered from two different viewpoints. Thus, it can be said that whenever someone or something changes in state, then death takes place with respect to the preceding condition and birth with respect to the new condition. The most common scriptural example to illustrate this beliefis that ofwater thrown on fire, changing into steam. This change shows the death of water at its liquid state and shows the rebirth at its aerial state. We shall later see how this conception is central in the ritual ofthe corpse's cremation.

The change ofstate is ritually connected with what is known as the theme of 'passage',' the 'door' through which it is effectuated being viewed as both an entrance and an exit. As the **descent** and the ascent, the entrance and the exit are determined by the understanding of the movement operated. The door in and ofitself is but an opening which permits passage through theborder or limen which separates two environments. It'sthe consciousness ofthe person performing the passage through the **door** which establishes when and what is the phase ofentry and **the** .phase of exit In general, in the different religious and philosophic conceptions oflndia, the womb is considered as the **tomb** ofthe preceding state. An immediate consequence ofwhat has just been said is the following. If the passage death-birth is one and the same phenomenon simply considered from two different perspectives and in temporal succession, not the same thing can be said ofthe reverse pair birth-death. These two terms in fact do not coincide, since between them is interposed a duration, namely life, in the course ofwhich an existence develops.

When one enters a Hindu temple passing through the entrance door under the signs ofdeath and rebirth *(kāla-kñrti mukha}* this symbolises the dying to the external world and the being reborn to a more sacred condition. One thus finds himself **in** a vestibule *(mandapa)* which must be walked through in all its length in order to pass, through another door, into an even more interior and sacred hall. This continues for two, three, or even five halls or rooms in a row, imtil one reaches the *sanctum sanctorum {garbha-grha}.* As said, all doors show on the architrave and on their posts the sculptured signs of death and rebirth; it is thus evident that the visitor is introduced to the supreme mystery by passing through a sequence of halls representing different and successive existences. It is here that for the first tiine the doctrine of transmigration is manifested, that is, the doctrine of purification, through subsequent births. From this example we derive one more element ofthe theorem we are analyzing, namely action *{karman},* in the absence of which no movement or change can take place.

Becoming *(kāla}* is the moving principle ofaction, which forces all things and beings to go through all the inaumerable rooms which make up the temple ofthe universe Beings who have obtained birth in a determined existence and in a well-defined world — a definite room of the temple — develop a multiplicity of actions which will lead them step by step and fatally to the extreme limit oftheir existence in that particular world.
