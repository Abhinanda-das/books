My Days with Prabhupada is the true story of Wally, a young man who runs and walks and stumbles along the mystical path of the Hare Krishna Movement desperately searching for the absolute truth. It follows him from the marijuana parties and psychedelic sessions on Manhattan’s Lower East Side through a haunted storefront in the mountains of New Mexico to the street corners of Paris’s Left Bank and finally to the serene holy places of India.

But most of all, we see Wally as he finds the teacher he has been looking for: Prabhupada, the founder of the Hare Krishna Movement. Our focus shifts to Prabhupada as he defeats Wally’s arguments with brilliant, stunning logic and the teachings of the ancient Vedas. We watch as Prabhupada gives Wally a new identity as Umapati and pulls him out of a life of sensual indulgence into a new world of spiritual peace, all by compassion, patience, and a surprisingly clear and simple explanation of the Bhagavad-Gita.

And we see the effect. We see Umapati’s metamorphosis from his first reluctant bowing at Prabhupada’s feet to his days of mourning after Prabhupada’s passing from this world.

Written by one of the first American followers of the Hare Krishna Movement, My Days with Prabhupada offers a brain-tingling journey for the spiritualist and the seeker and the skeptic as well.

# My days with Prabhupada: A Young Man’s Path to God in the Hare Krishna Movement

## Dedication

This book is dedicated to His Divine Grace A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, my eternal spiritual master. He taught me the difference between being an enjoyer of the material world and being an object of enjoyment for Krishna, the God of gods.

## Introduction

Go for God Whenever

I am hard-pressed for a quick explanation of Krishna Consciousness, my thoughts turn to a true story from India about the troubles of a five-year-old prince named Dhruva Maharaja, who lived millions of years ago. The universe was younger then and the world was clean and fresh, but royal intrigue had already entered.

Dhruva stormed out of his father’s palace one day, breathing heavily, outraged that his right to become the future king had been taken away. He would do something about it. On his mother’s advice he started for the forest to find God, as was done in those days, and then he would ask God for a kingdom greater than that of his father, even greater than the whole universe. Nothing less would appease his anger.

As Dhruva Maharaja was leaving, he met the great sage Narada. “But you’re just a little boy,” said Narada. “What will you do in the forest? Better to go back to your father.” “My humblest apologies, O great sage,” said Dhruva, “but I cannot take your advice. I am the descendant of kings, and I must right this injustice against me.” Narada, the spiritual master of many great sages, had himself gone into the forest as a boy. He taught Dhruva Maharaja how to meditate on God’s name and told him to go to the forest known as Madhuvan by the River Yamuna in what is now north-central India. And nothing could stop Dhruva Maharaja. By his intense meditation, he developed amazing powers of sense control. He ate only dry grass and the leaves that drifted onto the forest floor. He took a breath only every few days. He would find God no matter what it took and ask Him for the kingdom.

But after just six months, he forgot about the kingdom when he opened his eyes and saw God standing before him. God — the soul of the universe, the origin of the self in every living being, the eternal companion we have all been searching for and dreaming of through millions of lifetimes — was now standing before Dhruva Maharaja and smiling.

Dhruva wanted to speak but could not find the words. Then God held out His conch and touched it to Dhruva’s forehead. Dhruva Maharaja began to speak. “I was looking for broken glass,” he told God, “but I have found a diamond.” My spiritual master, Srila Prabhupada, told me how to find the same diamond. You won’t hear it in any sermon these days. You won’t read it in any inspirational book. “Go for the broken glass!” they tell you. They spur you on, cheer you on to squeeze as much as you can out of God. But Prabhupada showed me the other way, the way of Dhruva Maharaja: Don’t run after gifts from God. Go for God Himself.

How did Prabhupada communicate this truth that escapes so many? Some years ago, my godbrother Yogesvara Das started pestering me to write about Prabhupada and the times I had spent with him in the early years of the Hare Krisha movement. Yogesvara even drove me to his home in New York, sat me down in the basement, and shoved a microphone in front of my face. “OK,” he said, “start talking.” That should have been the beginning of this book, but it wasn’t. It took me a few more years to actually sit down and write.

The secret is in the Bhagavad-gita, where Krishna says that He Himself is the highest truth. If Dhruva Maharaja gave up his dream of a kingdom, it was not for the sake of a void or a shining light or an undefinable something. In spite of what many yogis have suggested, Krishna is not a literary device for those who cannot meditate on the undefinable. What Dhruva Maharaja saw standing before him was a real person. That is the message of the Bhagavad-gita: Stop looking for something other than Krishna. After Prabhupada pointed this out — and it is clearly stated in many places — the mysterious, esoteric scripture became clear and easy to understand.

But understanding is one thing; taking it to heart is another. The tendency to want things from God, both gross and subtle is deeply ingrained in us, and that includes me. Dhruva Maharaja gave himself as an example: “Foolish persons, such as me, desire benedictions from You for sense gratification, which is available even for those who live in hellish conditions.” We turn to God asking for the same pleasures that we already know: money, health, what we imagine ecstasy to be, but God Himself is the source of a pleasure that we do not yet know of.

Serving Krishna with devotion is both the goal and the means to attain it. “By the process of devotional service,” said Dhruva Maharaja, “I shall surely be able to cross the nescient ocean of material existence.” Krishna is the goal, and for one who has attained that goal, the mind’s desire is to love Krishna and serve Him. For one who has not yet attained the goal, serving Krishna is the means. “Therefore,” said Dhruva Maharaja, “for one who abides in Your devotional service with no other desire — for that person, worshiping Your lotus feet is better than becoming king and lording it over a kingdom.” But devotional service does not come cheap. It has to be taught by someone already performing it. “O unlimited Lord,” said Dhruva Maharaja, “kindly bless me so that I may associate with great devotees who engage in Your transcendental loving service constantly, as the waves of a river constantly flow.” Prabhupada was one of those great devotees. This book is a chronicle of how I saw him present this profound science in the early years of the Hare Krishna movement. I must admit that sometimes I cannot remember exactly when or in what order the events took place, but the important question is what Prabhupada said or did, not whether it was Monday or Tuesday.

In some places my account differs from the accounts of my godbrothers Satsvarupa Das Goswami and Hayagriva Das, but I have written about the events exactly as I remember them. In the beginning we called Prabhupada “the Swami” or “Swamiji,” and I have used these names in the first few chapters of this book I have also tried to describe the era where it all happened — the hippie movement, the drug culture, the war in Vietnam, the first moonwalk. I have talked about my own difficulties and conflicts, my reluctance to leave a social setting where I felt comfortable. But mostly, I have tried to communicate a taste of what it was like to be with a great soul like Prabhupada. And no description of Prabhupada is complete without the intoxicating beauty, the ever-increasing sweetness, and the magnificent, undefeatable logic of Krishna consciousness.

## Chapter 1 - Growing Up

This book is about Prabhupada, but I thought I would first say something about the person who is telling the story. I needed Prabhupada from the day I was born, but it took me twenty-nine years to find him. He alone answered all the questions and doubts that accumulated in my mind as I went through life. Everyone else gave me false information.

From my point of view, it all started out well. The first thing I remember is the sweet feeling of sunlight pouring in, surrounding me as I floated in it, basked in it, breathed in it. Mom had put little Wally, her new baby, to sleep on a sun porch in someone’s house. It was my first awakening to the outside world, and for my whole life I have thought of the sun as a friend.

But I became cynical at a young age. I was a Jewish kid in a Christian neighborhood on Chicago’s Northwest Side, so I learned about prejudice when I was still too young to understand why that mean man chased me away from his house: “Go home across the street where you belong!” But most of the neighbors treated us like friends, gossiping with Mom and joking with Dad. Still, Mom didn’t trust them. “They all hate you because you’re Jewish,” she told me, “but some of them are polished and don’t show it.” But she was also polished and never showed her real feelings to the neighbors.

As soon as I could read, Mom gave me a book: Bible Stories for Very Young Children. I read the book again and again, drawn into it by the feeling of peace and mellowness that ran through it although the Old Testament is neither peaceful nor mellow. It was my introduction to religion. I had been to the synagogue and I had seen an occasional ritual practiced in the family, but none of it seemed to fit with the peaceful feeling of the book.

And I felt peaceful till the day a man came to our front door from the city’s water department.

Dad hadn’t paid the bill. “We mailed a check this morning,” Mom told him. The man left, and mom turned to me. “He came with a key to shut off the water,” she said. But he took Mom’s word and left our water on.

I said nothing to Mom, but I felt my blood turn hot. “Those dogs! Those rotten pigs! How can they shut off people’s water? Those rats!” I imagined Mom crying and Dad being humiliated and the whole family having to move in with relatives till we got the water back. In my mind I ran after that man and knocked him down and punched him and kicked him and pushed him off a cliff and threw him under a train. From then on, I snickered whenever I heard my teachers brag about wonderful America.

What a joke! What kind of government would deprive a family of water? Mom never worked on a job. Nor did most other moms. They stayed home and took care of the kids in those days. Every day after school I walked in the kitchen door to the smell of Hills Bros. coffee bubbling and sputtering in the percolator. Mom drank coffee throughout the day, but she never gave me any. It was bad for kids, she said. I never did understand how something bad for kids was good for grownups, but I liked the picture on the coffee can, a wise man from the Middle East sporting a turban and a white beard, striding forward in his long flowing robe. He reminded me of something from the long faraway past, though I didn’t know what. Maybe some day I would find out.

When Dad wasn’t working on his job, he was fixing up the house, hammering and sawing to make a swing for the back porch, tearing down walls and putting up new ones, painting the living room bright pink (it was supposed to be “old rose”). He loved working on the house but not working at his laborious job repairing machinery. Still, whatever Dad did, he did well, and any boss should have been glad to have him, but Dad always worried about losing his job, and it did happen several times, though not from any fault of Dad’s. It was always because the company was running short of money or some other such problem. There was no protection for a poor working man like Dad. In my life I have seen many grateful employees, but I have never seen a grateful boss. No matter how much you sacrifice for the company, no matter how much free overtime you put in, no matter how many times you come in when you are sick and should be at home, as soon as there is some little problem, the boss will throw you out on the street. Nor does the government care about you either. If you don’t have enough money, the man with the key will come to shut off the water.

Poor Dad! Whatever pleasure he got in life could not be worth all the anxiety he had to suffer, so I decided to spend my life single and childless. I would live without a family, I would never own a house, I would never own anything I had to worry about losing. I would never put my life at the mercy of some boss or some government that cared no more about me than about the lamppost on the street. I learned how to wash my own clothes — easy when you had an automatic washing machine and a dryer — and I became a whiz at scrambling eggs so I would never need a wife to feed me.

Dad’s younger brother, Uncle Dave, studied hard to become a lawyer, but he was never allowed to practice law. He was refused entrance to the state bar because when he was in school he had had a casual friend who later became a communist although Uncle Dave was never a communist.

When Mom told me about this, I decided I would never take up a profession that required a license. I knew, of course, that when you work you have to please people, but I decided that I would please people by the quality of my work without having to get approval from some bureaucrat.

In my early years, America was at war, World War II. Dad and the neighbors grew vegetables in the back yards and empty lots in “victory gardens.” I learned from the movies that the Americans (that was us) were fighting the Germans who looked like us but talked funny and the Japanese who had yellow skin and slanty eyes and talked even funnier. A young man named Roger who lived across the street went off to fight in the war and never came back. That wasn’t funny.

Then Mom gave birth to a girl, but I didn’t really need another sister because I already had one.

Natalie was Dad’s darling and pretty enough to make Mom proud. With her sense of humor and quick wit she could liven up any situation. She was older than I by six years, so we didn’t do much together except for the accordion lessons we took for about a year till Dad had us stop. I guess he couldn’t afford to keep it up.

Still, I was delighted when Cecelia was born. As soon as she was old enough, we played together although there were also six years between us, and we have been friends to this day. When she wasn’t in school, she ran and played all day until we got a television set. Then she played a lot less and watched cowboy movies instead.

My brother, Ralph, was three years older than I. He spent hours in the basement at Dad’s worktable gluing together model airplanes, but he was at his happiest outside, swinging a baseball bat. He even swung an imaginary one inside the house when he would put a stack of 78-rpm records on the phonograph in the pink living room and practice the movements of baseball in front of the large mirror on the wall.

When I was eight the war ended. America had won. We would not have to worry anymore about being invaded from the Atlantic or the Pacific. Boys like Roger would not march off to war anymore, never to come home. My brother laughed when he saw the newspaper headlines talking about baseball and not about casualties. Everyone stopped growing victory gardens and grew flowers instead. We had had our victory. We didn’t need to help the economy by growing fresh vegetables any more. Now we could waste our money buying stale ones from the store. We felt justified, we felt we had done something right when we learned about the horrible things the Germans and Japanese had done to people. The Americans had also done something horrible by dropping two atomic bombs on Japan, but nobody worried much about that except, I suppose, the Japanese.

The government built a temporary housing project nearby for veterans coming home from the war, so our lily-white Christian neighbors now had to deal with blacks living in the area and black children in the school, as if Jews weren’t bad enough. One day I was talking with the principal’s secretary when an older white boy walked in leading a little black boy who was soaking wet from falling in a puddle. The principal came out of the inner office. “Come on,” he said to me. “Let’s go for a ride.” We drove to the little boy’s house in the project, and I waited in the car while the principal talked to the boy’s mother and gave her the child. But the mother could just as well have been white.

She acted the same, not like some sort of “negro” mother. What difference did her color make? I never forgot this lesson.

When I was a little older, I asked a rabbi about anti-semitism after I had been insulted by some teenage boys in a candy store — ”Here comes this little Jew.” “They resent the Jews,” the rabbi told me, “because the Jews gave them Christianity, but they don’t really want to be Christians. It’s too hard for them.” I liked that answer. Instead of blaming Jesus, as many Jews did, the rabbi put the blame where it belonged: on the people. He countered prejudice with reason. But I began to wonder, What was the fundamental flaw in people’s thinking that led them to judge others by their race? The blacks and the Jews should have known better, but they were as prejudiced as anyone else.

But there was a lot more to my life than prejudice, and I had friends among the Christian neighbors. In my teens, my thoughts turned to a career, and I decided to become a recording engineer.

I liked recorded music so much that I wanted to become part of it. I would find the meaning of life through pleasure. I had briefly studied music with my accordion lessons and I had an interest in electronics, so it seemed like the perfect career. And there was something intriguing, a little mystical even, about capturing a moment of time on a record and being able to play it back. Somehow I got a job at Universal Recording as an assistant to Bill Putnam, one of the top recording engineers in the country. The best thing about this job, as I look back, was that I got a chance to see professionals at work, how they paid attention to the smallest detail. I learned from Bill Putnam that repairing the equipment was as important as making the records and that recording advertisements was as important as recording pop music. I saw singers and musicians work for hours on one song just to get it perfect, yet I never heard anyone say, “I’m tired,” or “Do we really have to do this again?” School was not my world anymore. I flunked out of my first year at the University of Illinois, a disgrace for most Jewish boys but a delight for me, and spent the next year happily working at the studio. And when some friends gave me my first puff of marijuana, I thought I had taken a big step toward finding the greatest thing in life. But I never told anyone at the studio about it. You had to keep it a secret in those days.

At Bill Putnam’s urging, I tried school again, at an electronics school in Indiana. Then one day I saw a long-distance bus going down the road. “New Orleans,” said the sign in the window. I remembered lying in bed at night as a kid, listening to a train whistle in the distance, picturing myself sitting on that train going to some distant, unknown place. Now I wished I were on that bus, and when the school year ended I joined the army for some adventure. I would have liked a different adventure, like going to New Orleans, but every young man had to take up military service for at least two years.

I volunteered for an extra year to get benefits.

Now the young recording engineer was at Fort Leonard Wood in the Ozark Mountains of Missouri, being made over into a United States soldier, crawling through mud, doing push-ups, running with an M1 rifle in hand shouting, “Kill! Kill! Kill!” I saluted officers, I shined my shoes, I scrubbed the bathroom sinks and cleaned the toilets. But when the day was over and the scrubbing was finished and the running was finished, there was one thing I liked about Fort Leonard Wood: I would stand and listen to the whippoorwills calling at sunset. Recordings don’t do it justice. You have to be outside and hear them calling all around you. It’s like the Mona Lisa. No photo can reproduce the effect. You have to see the original.

Finally I found myself on a plane bound for the Army Language School in Monterey, California.

I took up French because I liked the sound of it, and I had dreamed of going to France ever since the time Mom told me how naughty French people were and how everything was allowed in France. (It turned out to be a false promise.) And New Orleans? I never made it, but the army sent me to another Orleans, the original one in France, where I sat at a desk and typed reports. Every weekend I took the three-hour train ride to Paris. Some musicians I knew from my recording work had friends there, so I knew where to go.

I liked Paris. I liked the wide, magnificent Champs Elysīes with the Arch of Triumph at the end.

I liked the cobblestoned boulevards lined with chestnut trees and sidewalk cafīs and stylish boutiques with shoppers going in and out, and I liked the narrow little streets in Montmartre, twisting and winding past old bookstores and little apartment buildings with lace curtains in the windows. My new friends showed me the hippest night spots. There was jazz at La Louisiana on the Left Bank and at the Mars Club off the Champs Elysīes, where visiting entertainers from the United States would spend an evening or two and maybe even sing a couple of songs. I went to the film studios at Boulogne Billancourt and watched Paul Newman and other big names working on a movie called “Paris Blues.” I thought I was learning about life, hanging out with entertainers and composers and expatriates in the most sophisticated night spots in Paris, but I always had a nagging feeling that there had to be something more. I still had to go forward to find something though I didn’t know what. Stayed in France until my discharge from the army in April 1962, but I left with a feeling that some day I would go back.

I settled in New York to explore the different paths of life away from the eyes of my conservative family, and I already had a job offer as a recording engineer at Riverside Records, a well-known producer of jazz albums. But in December 1962, Riverside went bankrupt. I moved in with a friend on Horatio Street in New York’s West Village to save money and enjoy the company of the intellectuals and freewheeling young people in the neighborhood. That’s where my adventure started.

## Chapter 2 - The Lower East Side

One night in February 1964, when I was twenty-six and had been working for almost a year at Roulette Records, putting out pop and Latin music, I left the house on Horatio Street and strolled across Manhattan to the Lower East Side, to Stanley’s Bar on Twelfth Street and Avenue B. A cold wind was blowing, so I buttoned the top button on my coat. I was on a mission.

Stanley’s was well lit, but the paint was old, peeling in a few spots. The place was crowded with young hippies, chatting and laughing over their beer. The warm air felt good on a winter night, but it was heavy with cigarette smoke and the obnoxious smell of alcohol. I hated alcohol. I hated the way it smelled, the way it tasted, the way it made me slur my speech and stagger. I had come to Stanley’s looking for something else. I had run out of marijuana. (We called it “pot.”) With a soft drink in my hand I wiggled and squirmed through the crowd in search of a familiar face. I found Roberto, a tall, gentle young man with a Spanish accent. “Hey Roberto,” I said, “do you know where I can get some pot?” “Wait,” he said and disappeared into the crowd. He came back with a bearded young man.

“Hi,” I said. “What’s your name?” “Keith.” “My name’s Wally. Do you have any pot?” “No.” I shrugged. Well, OK. Disappointment was a normal part of looking for drugs.

“I’ve got peyote (pay-OH-tee).” Wow! Peyote! I had heard of it, a cactus with a natural psychedelic drug. Now I had found it.

Here was a new adventure.

“Can you turn me on?” I said.

“Sure. Let me get my roommate.” He disappeared into the crowd and came back bringing a tall, dark-haired, clean-shaven young man.

“Hi,” said the young man. “I’m Howard.” He had a southern accent.

Keith, Howard, Roberto, and I drove across the Lower East Side to an apartment on Mott Street. As Keith opened the door, a black female cat came to greet us. She looked innocent enough.

Who would have thought that one day I’d be scrambling to get this goddamn bitch of a cat out of my house and out of my life? We walked in. The place smelled like Stanley’s Bar but mixed with the smell of cat. To my left was the bathroom. Keith turned on the light, and I saw a dark liquid in the bathtub.

“We’re brewing beer,” said Keith.

Beer? What kind of guys would be into beer? Beer was gross, something for fat slobs with suspenders. Most people who liked drugs didn’t drink much except for socializing, as at Stanley’s.

And so much beer you had to put it in the bathtub? The mere thought made me gag. Anyway, who cared about the beer? The peyote was waiting for me.

A few steps more and we were in the kitchen. We walked through a doorway into a dark windowless bedroom and then through another doorway into the sitting room. It was chilly, so I pulled my coat around me. Then I saw a sofa covered with a cloth. The hour was late, and I was about to get high. Wouldn’t a sofa be perfect! I heaved a sigh, plopped down on it, and landed on something hard. No cushions, just a wooden board. Keith chuckled at the look of surprise on my face.

So maybe he was into beer — none of my other friends were — but he seemed like a good guy.

Then Keith brought out the peyote. The cactus tasted awful, he explained, but he had found a way to distill it. You just drank some liquid, about an inch and a half in a glass. Keith drank it first so I could see how. Then I did it. First I ate a piece of strong cheese to numb my tongue. Then I took a deep breath and drank the peyote as fast as I could, followed by some water. No problem so far. But now I had to let the breath out of my lungs. That one bitter breath seemed to last for hours, like the nastiest medicine I’d ever had to gulp down.

Roberto said he wanted to get high, not drunk. He was a simple kind of guy, and he didn’t understand when we told him this was something to get high on. He drank the liquid but then left.

Howard didn’t take any. We sat around and chatted till Howard went to bed. Then I began to feel nauseated. “This is a normal reaction,” said Keith. Normal or not, was anything worth such misery? Finally I went into the bathroom and vomited. (I found out later that Roberto vomited in the subway.) I came back and sat on the sofa. “Dig what’s happening out of the corners of your eyes,” said Keith.

I looked and saw beautiful, vibrant American Indian art, the head of an eagle, rhythmically drawn but alive and pushing forward almost like music. It was overwhelming, this beauty I was seeing, and not just seeing with my eyes but seeing, hearing, and feeling with my whole self.

“Yeah!” I said. “This is great stuff.” And so it went for most of the night, visions upon beautiful visions. Toward the end of the night I saw a powerful man of ancient times walking with a powerful stride to the sound of Bach-like music. Was this the real me or was it God or was it someone else? I couldn’t tell. It didn’t matter. But I knew the whole time — and the lingering ache in my stomach was another reminder — that all this was from the peyote, that by the end of the night I would return to normal consciousness, so I wasn’t afraid of the strangeness. I relaxed and enjoyed the high till I drifted off to sleep.

Just after dawn, Howard woke up and turned on the bedroom light, revealing a bookshelf taking up a whole wall, books to make you yawn, boring intellectual stuff by writers I had never heard of. I had stopped reading long ago. Why read what’s on the minds of other people? I’ve got a mind of my own. Let me enrich it by living life to the fullest.

“I’ll go and bring back some rolls for breakfast,” said Keith.

“I’ll go with you,” I said.

The early morning air was cold and clear and smelled unusually fresh. In the soft light, Manhattan seemed to be painted in water colors, or maybe there was still some peyote swirling in my brain. The human voices around me felt clean in my ears, mixed with the sounds of the birds. I liked Manhattan in the early morning. I liked it any time of the day or night. Who gave a damn if people said it was dirty and noisy? It was the center of the world, the most exciting place in the universe.

Whatever was happening anywhere else was happening in Manhattan, and I was right there.

We heard church bells. “Want to go to mass?” said Keith.

I laughed. Cute joke, wasn’t it? Go to a religious service after being up all night on some drug? I liked his sense of humor. But now, years later, when I look back I’m not sure he was joking. I had not seen anything spiritual about the peyote. It was just another high. So I was skeptical later when I heard people say the high was religious.

I had almost forgotten about religion. When I was a kid I went to religious class — we called it Hebrew school — every weekday afternoon in our shiny new synagogue, but I liked the class more when we had it in an older building next door. This old building had a mysterious feeling. It made me think of ancient times and faraway places, like the feeling I got when I found old books in someone’s attic and couldn’t wait to open them. The feeling in the old building also emanated from a set of books. They sat on a shelf in the middle of the room, looking like something from ages past, a trove of hidden mystical knowledge. Too bad no one could read them. They were in Arabic.

World War II had ended when I was eight, but when I entered my teens Jews all over the world were still reeling from the ghastly discovery of what the Germans had done. And who would have thought it would happen in Germany, where the Jews were so well integrated into society? “They were proud of being Germans,” Mom told me. “They would boast. ‘Meir zeinen der daytshish yidden. We are the German Jews. We are Germans first and Jews second.’ But Hitler showed them what was what.” “Don’t think it can’t happen in America,” my brother said. “If it could happen in Germany, it can happen anywhere.” One autumn day in the synagogue, an elder stood up on the stage in the auditorium and spoke about the horrors of the concentration camps. Across the aisle from me, to my left, a frail old woman was sobbing so hard I wondered how her body could take it. Had she gone through it? Why was she alone? Had she lost her loved ones to the gas chambers at Auschwitz? Then the speaker asked for donations for the new state of Israel. The old woman reached into her purse with trembling hands.

Donations for Israel? Fine with me. The Jews needed their own country. We had had enough concentration camps, enough gas chambers. But did someone have to tear the heart out an old woman just to collect money? Was this religion? Now I can see that I was judging the victims instead of the criminals. I can understand the anger that was boiling over in the auditorium that day. The memory of the old woman stayed fresh in my mind through my teens. Anyway, what was the use of religion? What did it do for people? What did it add to my life? And now the hippies were saying real religion did exist. You would find it in psychedelics.

Well, I had my doubts. Granted, American Indians in the southwest had been getting religious insights from peyote for centuries, but what the Indians did had nothing to do with me. I knew nothing of their customs or how they distinguished visions from reality.

The idea of psychedelics and religious experience had been seeping into what people called the hippie movement though it was not a movement, only a fad. It started with writers. As early as 1954 Aldous Huxley, in The Doors of Perception, wrote of religious insights after taking mescaline, the active essence in peyote. In the late 1950s and early ‘60s, other prominent writers like Allen Ginsberg were experimenting with psychedelics and talking about Eastern religions. Then the hippies got it. As they started taking LSD and reading Buddhist and Hindu writings, the mysterious words — consciousness, existence, ego — all became real.

A friend took me to a meeting hall somewhere in Greenwich Village, a popular neighborhood among the young people, to hear a talk by Richard Alpert, a former Harvard professor who had been fired for giving LSD to students.

“How can you prolong the high after the LSD wears off?” someone asked.

“The Buddhist and Hindu writings,” said Alpert.

(Alpert later traveled to India and accepted a spiritual master who renamed him Baba Ram Das. He returned to the United States, where he continued to lecture along with writing books and engaging in charitable works.) Alan Watts, the author of The Way of Zen, compared LSD to Buddhist enlightenment, but he himself did not seriously practice Zen. Genuine practitioners, like the American master Philip Kapleau, said Watts was a long way from the truth. The Catholic monk Thomas Merton wrote that Catholic meditation could also produce visions that to us sounded like LSD, but he called it “the wrong flame.” It was not true meditation.

Keith, Howard, and I sided with the hippies. Now we knew there was indeed religious truth and it would make us happy. Better yet, we could find it in psychedelics without going through the austerities of religion. We could have it both ways: religious truth and the music, sex, and drugs of hippie life. I visited Mott Street often, even living there for extended periods.

Changes took place on Mott Street. The wooden sofa and the bedsprings went out the door, and floor cushions and mattresses came in. Another friend, George, a mathematics professor with a husky build and a Texas accent, painted the sitting room in blue with the window frames and door frames in cream, but the outlines were raggedy, making the place look like an oversized drawing by a child. No wonder I started having age-regression experiences on my LSD highs.

The beer gave way to marijuana, though not in the bathtub. Keith and Howard had already smoked it, of course, but they were not impressed. “You’ve just never had good stuff,” I told them, “but I know where to get the best pot in town.” Thus I converted them. No more slurred speech and staggering and clumsiness, but now laughter and clever, witty, scintillating conversation. At least that’s what we thought. “My wife has never smoked pot,” said a friend. “She says it makes people act silly.” But what about the enhanced musical appreciation? “I smoked it once,” a well-known record producer told me, “but when I saw the notes coming out of the horn and floating around the room, I thought, ‘This stuff ain’t real,’ and I never smoked it again.” But I knew better, and so did Keith and Howard. “You know,” Keith said one evening, gesturing with his marijuana pipe, “sometimes I think I would like to become Johnny Potseed.” (John Chapman, known as Johnny Appleseed, planted apple trees wherever he went in the late 1800s.) Keith was the son of a Baptist minister and quoted the Bible with ease, for better or for worse.

“Maybe I should become a vegetarian,” I said one day.

Keith looked up. “Saint Paul said, ‘Eat what’s set before you and ask no questions for conscience’ sake,’” he replied. I liked that advice. Not that I cared about meat, but it was easier and more like freedom to eat whatever came along without having to make an effort to find the right thing.

Keith always knew just the right words to answer an argument or make you laugh. With his light hair and pink skin and now clean-shaven face, he was the picture of the fair-haired young white American. He walked with careful steps and an exaggerated swing of his hips, a leftover from the polio he had had at sixteen.

Our LSD highs (we called them “trips”) turned into a friendly competition between Keith and me: Who could go the farther out and speak the deeper truths? Keith sometimes spoke in Christian terms, talking about faith in the Father. And he found a special way to show his faith. LSD often came in a “cube,” a sugar cube spiked with a brown drop of LSD and wrapped in aluminum foil. One day Keith wrapped several ordinary sugar cubes in foil and put them in the refrigerator. If he had real faith, the Father would turn them into LSD cubes. Howard and I scoffed at Keith’s bogus Christianity.

Had any of us seen a Father on our LSD trips? Howard was from Florida. Keith called him “the ogre” because he grumbled and grouched in his southern accent if he didn’t get his way. With his flowing black hair and his thick beard now grown long, people mistook him for the writer Allen Ginsberg though he and Ginsberg did not look alike. But there was a similarity. Howard was also a writer, working on a novel about his boyhood in the South, but the novel was never published.

Howard also had a great sense of humor and saw the funny side of any situation, even the time when he was in the countryside with a big jar of marijuana and suddenly panicked at the idea of being caught. He buried the jar in the woods, but later forgot where. The buried treasure is still waiting to be found. He sometimes pulled one of his books off the shelf and started reading aloud in a booming voice as he sat in a chair. I didn’t understand a damn thing. Then he would explain what he had just read: “What he means is...” I knew I could never be a writer. I didn’t know how to write in this lingo that only intellectuals like Howard understood. I was a college dropout. But as intellectual as Howard was, he needed my help to get his master’s degree. One day, I mentioned that I had served in the American army in France.

“Do you speak French?” Howard asked.

“Yes.” “I have to pass a test in a foreign language to get my degree,” he said. “Could you take a French test for me? If you pass, I’ll buy you half a pound of pot.” I took the test, Howard got his master’s degree, and I got half a pound of marijuana.

## Chapter 3 - The Swami

I was lounging on a floor mattress at Mott Street in the blue-and-cream sitting room, leafing through an album of art photos I had just bought — miniature paintings from India to dazzle my mind when I got high. Turned out they didn’t dazzle all that much, but I liked them anyway. As I flipped the pages, I came upon a picture of someone named Vishnu lying on His back on the belly of a gigantic many-headed snake, both of them floating on a cloud, while He created the world.

What? Did I see right? So Vishnu is God and He created the world — OK — but lying on a snake? I leaned back a little more, my head and shoulders against the wall, my lower back against a pillow. The black cat jumped up and sat next to me on my left and started purring. I took a deep breath. Wait. God lying on a snake? It was surely one of those strange creation legends from some exotic part of the world. The book also talked about people in India who said the world had never been created at all. It didn’t exist. It was an illusion. That felt more comfortable.

I turned the page and saw a painting of a young man playing a flute and dancing with girls in a garden of trees and flowers. On earth, said the book, Vishnu incarnates Himself as “Sri Krishna, the hero of countless legends.” I stopped, sat up a bit and looked out the cream-framed window, then back to the book. The hero of countless legends? Then Krishna must be like the American hero Paul Bunyan. But dancing in a garden? Heroes didn’t play flutes and dance in gardens. They fought against bad guys. I reached over and petted the cat. Then I turned the page again.

Another page said something about a book called Bhavagad-gita. I had come across that name a few times before, while casually browsing dictionaries, so the next day I strolled over to a bookstore and bought a paperback version, but I couldn’t make anything of it. It was a jumble of words, sometimes prose, sometimes verse, always confusing, no clear message except that when Krishna said to meditate on Him, He didn’t mean it.

It was now the summer of 1965, and I was twenty-eight. I had quit my job, though it was a good job in every way. The pay was good, the work was fun, and I had plenty of spare time and a chance to go forward in my profession. But I did not know how to reconcile working on a job with my search for truth. I lied to the people at the city employment service so I could collect benefits and told them I had been fired. The employment people took my word for it without checking, to my surprise, and now I was collecting unemployment compensation: fifty dollars a week for doing nothing. It wasn’t much — enough for food and marijuana — but it was free. I was living on Mott Street, and I assumed Howard was paying the rent. I never asked. To collect my benefits I had to pretend to look for work, but record companies and studios hired engineers through contacts in the music business. The folks at the employment service would never find a job for me.

Well, they did. They sent me for an interview at a service where doctors called in for the latest recorded medical news. I did my best to make a bad impression — looking at my fingernails, looking at the floor, shuffling in my chair. I looked over at the boss behind his desk. He’d surely be a nice boss if only I wanted one. He spoke gently and smiled a lot. He was a middle-aged man, surely Jewish, and his pinkish, round face had a scrubbed-clean look. “Call me Monday,” he said.

On Monday I called, hoping against hope. Alas! The boss hired me. “We want you to join our bandwagon,” he said. I sighed. If I turned the job down, I would lose my unemployment benefits. But nobody can know God’s plan. This job gave me my first look at my spiritual master, later known as Prabhupada.

In the late summer, Keith and Howard decided to go to India. More than a year had passed since Howard had gotten his master’s degree, and he had spent the time teaching English at a university in Ohio. (By the way, in Ohio he and Keith rented an apartment on High Street... really.) Howard spoke of his mystical philosophy as much as possible in his classes without revealing himself as a drug user. The students were fascinated. Now, having saved enough money for two tickets to India, he left with Keith and a small bundle of possessions on a freighter. They were going to become holy men. I didn’t know where the rent would come from, but I didn’t worry about it.

Howard must have paid a few months in advance.

I took over Mott Street, just me and the black cat, so I had a lot of time to think about the past year and a half. I thought about the scriptures and the beauty in them and the promise of happiness, but how could I ever become a Zen monk, sitting cross-legged on a straw mat, spine erect for hours at a time with other monks in a tiled hall filled with the flowery-pungent aroma of Chinese incense? If I started to fall asleep, the master would come and beat my shoulders with a flat stick. No thinking about sex or even an occasional dirty joke. No question of picking up a marijuana cigarette or putting some folk-rock music on the turntable or going out for some hot pizza dripping with melted cheese.

No. It wouldn’t work. My world was here on the Lower East Side.

Still, I wanted to find this secret truth, this truth that would come to me, so said the Zen books, in a flash of sudden enlightenment. Maybe I needed a teacher. But where would I ever find one? And how could I tell who was a real teacher and who was not? At my job, I met a co-worker named Sol, a gentle, soft-spoken Jewish man in his forties, and we soon discovered we were on the same path, looking for truth in the Eastern scriptures. Had Sol ever tried drugs? I didn’t ask. But one thing was sure: Sol wanted to find truth in the right way, in the traditional path taken by the great sages even if it meant accepting some austerity.

“How come you’re not a vegetarian?” he asked me.

“I’ve just never gotten around to it.” Sol told me about a vegetarian restaurant nearby. The next day I walked over and paid two dollars for a dry, tasteless sundae cup of raisins with shredded carrots. It tasted more like shredded paper. Vegetarianism? No, thanks.

Sol had a spiritual teacher, a yogi from India named Dr. Mishra. “He’s great,” Sol told me. “He knew my wife was pregnant even before she did. You ought to go see him.” I had never met a real Indian. I later found out that the American government had made Indians and other Asians feel unwelcome by the Asian Exclusion Act of 1924, which President Lyndon B. Johnson was now calling “a cruel and enduring wrong in the conduct of the American nation.” The act, soon to be repealed, barred them from owning property in the United States or becoming citizens.

I went to check out Sol’s teacher with George, the friend who had painted the apartment. The venue was a rented meeting room in Manhattan’s affluent east seventies, with folding chairs set up facing away from the door. I saw Sol and his wife with about thirty others. But who was the elderly gentleman in saffron robes sitting on a bench against the far wall? An Indian monk? The great teacher? I did not know it, but I was looking at my eternal spiritual master.

Then Sol’s teacher, Dr. Mishra, walked in. He was a pleasant-looking man maybe in his late forties, wearing Indian pants and the typical Indian button-up collarless jacket. And his lecture was the typical yoga lecture. I could read the same thing in my books at home.

The gentleman in saffron robes sat still the whole time except for a brief moment when he played with a stapler next to him on the bench. The next day, I asked Sol who he was. “That’s the Swami,” he told me.

Then in the early fall of 1965, George came to see me on Mott Street. Keith and Howard, he told me, were on their way back. After six weeks in India, they came home on a Scindia freighter, bringing lots of paintings, two big hookahs (those Oriental tobacco pipes that filter the smoke through water), and so much other stuff that George had to meet them at the pier with a van.

At Mott Street, Keith and Howard were telling us about India. Keith showed us a calendar with religious pictures on it. There was Shiva with three eyes (I recognized him), and Krishna playing a flute. Then came a picture of three unusual people. I thought they were aliens from outer space, but as I later found out they were the deities known as Jagannatha, Baladeva, and Subhadra. “And all of this is dime-store art in India,” said Keith, emphasizing how mystical the place was.

“Everybody there likes Krishna,” said Howard.

“Krishna?” I said. “Why Krishna? Keith and Howard had also smuggled in something special, and we celebrated the homecoming that night with Indian hashish (we called it hash) smoked in Indian hookahs.

I had recently quit my job, telling the boss that I was going on a spiritual quest. The day after the hash party I headed west to San Francisco with some of the Indian hash and a friend named Michael, a young Jewish guy (another one) who at first doubted that I was also Jewish because I had not graduated from college.

As we drove we smoked hash and listened to music on the radio. The Beatles were in a mellow groove with Michelle, and I was happy to understand the French and tell Michael what it meant. And we heard a record called The Sounds of Silence from a new duo, two guys named Simon and Garfunkel. I couldn’t make sense of the lyrics, but the social comment and the twelve-string guitar and the strong young voices — Yes! It was bringing in a great new wave in a great new era.

Yes! We hippies were doing it right, weren’t we? Yes! And I was glad to be part of it. We had discovered what life was all about, what the generations before us had never known, what the whole world had never known till now. Who cared what the old folks said? Who cared if they wagged their finger and warned us that we were running blindfolded in the wrong direction? Who cared if they told us we weren’t doing anything new, that others had been doing it for centuries? I knew better. We all knew better. And the hash and the music and the sunny blue sky over the road to California — Yes! Life was great, life was fabulous. Add a touch of Eastern religion, and you had hit the top.

San Francisco, built on hills and surrounded on three sides by the Pacific Ocean, was more beautiful than Manhattan though not as exciting. The constant Pacific breeze kept the air fresh, and you could see the sky, the highest buildings rising only thirteen stories because of the danger of earthquakes. Strolling in San Francisco was either an uphill or a downhill walk, and when I got to the top of a hill, I could see all the way down to the ocean. San Francisco was also one of the great sense-gratification hubs of the United States. This trip there was the first time I saw stores legally selling pornography.

We stayed with some friends of Michael’s, a young couple living in an apartment in Berkeley, across the bay from San Francisco. Berkeley had a large beautiful campus, the University of California at Berkeley, a great place for wandering around with its lawns and paths and neo-classical buildings. Berkeley was famous for its hippie population, many of them students at the university.

After just a few weeks, Michael left for New York, and I moved in with a cousin and her husband in Oakland, a town just next to Berkeley. They were both widely read, and we talked about a great variety of subjects.

It was just a short bus ride across the Bay Bridge into San Francisco, and I often spent my days there, lounging in Golden Gate Park. I made only a few friends. I was too much into my own thoughts about the absolute truth. I went to hear a Japanese Zen master, Suzuki Roshi, a jolly, glowing man who seemed always on the verge of laughing as he spoke. “How can you have the religious experience?” someone asked. I leaned forward to hear the answer. Maybe the master could give me a clue. “There is no success in religion,” the master answered, “but when people take up religion” — he started laughing — ”they want success.” Impressive, he was. But he wasn’t my teacher. Something was missing.

Then one night I had an unusual dream. I found myself wandering in a beautiful green forest with lakes and quacking ducks. The breeze touching my face carried the fragrance of wild flowers.

The blue sky was filled with sweet singing. Where was I? I woke up. What was this dream? Was it a mystical message? Then a thought struck me. The beautiful place must be New York. But why New York? No place could be farther removed from a beautiful forest. But somehow I felt that destiny was calling me from there. And it turned out to be true. I started hitchhiking eastward, meeting beautiful summer weather and friendly people along the way — the college boy who put me up for a night in Salt Lake City; the policeman who let me sleep in the courtroom in Cheyenne, Wyoming; the truck driver who bought me lunch. Finally I crossed the Mississippi into my home state of Illinois and then to Chicago, where Mom and Dad bought me a ticket on a Greyhound bus to finish my journey.

Eighteen hours later, the bus came out of the interstate tunnel into Manhattan. I was back in good old New York. Oh, New York, New York, I love you! What do you have waiting for me? One thing waiting, of course, was the Mott Street apartment, now a local hippie hangout.

Nighttime would find a small group of young men and women sitting or lying around the radio, colored bulbs dimly lighting the darkness, incense sweetening the air, people inhaling their marijuana with a soft whooshing sound that almost seemed like part of the music — the Rolling Stones singing “Mother’s Little Helper” and Dusty Springfield with “Believe Me.” Indeed, the music was as much a part of the high as the marijuana.

Mott Street ran north and south. If you left the apartment and strolled north to the corner of Houston Street and then started east, you would soon come to Third Avenue, also known as the Bowery, then continuing east, you would come to Second Avenue, a busy shopping street, made famous some months earlier in a hit record by Barbra Streisand, Second Hand Rose. I enjoyed riding in a car sometimes on Second Avenue while the radio played Barbra Streisand singing about the very street I was on. Then east of Second Avenue was First Avenue and then Avenue A.

Sometimes I stayed at my friend John’s place, just off Avenue A. John was also a young seeker, but he worked and always looked neat and clean. With his handsome boyish face, he looked the perfect match for his girlfriend, Ellie, herself the image of New York sophistication, a southern accent adding an extra bit of charm. Ellie was another person who always knew just what to say even in the most awkward situation. I hadn’t seen John in a while, but Ellie had given me the key to his apartment. One night, as I lay in bed there, I looked out the window at the full moon rising in the clear sky above the fire escape. Something wonderful was going to happen soon. I felt it, something spiritually wonderful. Or was I kidding myself because I wanted it to happen? Maybe. But the thought kept coming back. Something spiritually wonderful was going to happen.

And it happened. Keith and I were sitting and talking on Mott Street when Howard came in. “I just met this man on the street,” he said excitedly. “He was wearing robes, and I said, ‘Are you from India?’ and he said ‘Yes, and you?’ And he’s going to be giving classes on Second Avenue.” I groaned. I had already been disappointed twice, by Dr. Mishra and by Suzuki Roshi. “Another spiritual master,” I said sarcastically, “just what we need.” A few days later I was in a car with George, the friend who had gone with me to Dr. Mishra’s. And here was the Swami again, on the street walking with a bearded young man. George poked me. “Remember him?” he said.

I was still living at John’s place when something new on Second Avenue caught my eye: a store made into a yoga center, some kind of “Krishna” place. Over the door, you could still see the old painted sign, “Matchless Gifts.” “Lectures on Bhagavad-gita,” said the sign in the window.

Then early one evening, walking down Second Avenue, I heard music: ching-ching-ching, ching-ching-ching. What was it? A Puerto Rican church? I wanted to hear more, so I followed the sound... over to the Krishna place. The door was open. The Swami and a few young men were sitting on the floor, chanting a mantra. Could this be what I was looking for? I started to go in, but the Swami was turning his head from side to side as if to say no. He didn’t want me to go in. Oh. He was just moving his head in time with the music.

I walked in and sat down. I tried to join the chanting: Hare Krishna... Hare Krishna... Krishna Krishna... Hare Hare... Hare Rama... Hare Rama... Rama Rama... Hare Hare. I knew the word “Krishna,” but I did not know whether to say “Ha-ray” or “Ha-lay” nor “Rama” or “Lama,” but I did my best.

Then the Swami lectured. He was different. Hearing him speak was like hearing the same beauty I had seen in the scriptures. He had a strong accent, pushing the words out one by one, but I understood him clearly. I looked at him as he spoke. His hair was close-cropped, his facial features large on a toasty-gold skin. I had the feeling that I was looking at someone powerful, but his frame, clothed in saffron-colored robes, was light and smallish. Would he say something more than the others? Would he give me the clue? Suddenly he said something I had never heard before, something that made my mind sit up with a gasp. He said it with clarity and emphasis: “The supreme absolute truth is a person.” That was it! That was what I had been waiting for! It was the most intelligent thing I had ever heard. Of course! The truth was a person. Here, finally, was someone who told me what the truth was.

Had I found my teacher? Suddenly I began to hope that my teacher would be the Swami.

The next day I told Keith and Howard what had happened. “We were there,” they said. “Didn’t you see us?” Keith, Howard, and I began to go regularly, every Monday, Wednesday, and Friday evening. But soon a doubt came into my mind. How could I be sure the Swami knew the highest truth? I couldn’t settle for less. But the Swami dispelled my doubt during an evening lecture. “I teach only what is in scripture,” he said.

Scripture. It was only because of the Buddhist and Hindu scriptures that I even knew there was such a thing as Truth and that I could attain it. If he was teaching from these writings, if he gave me the essence, then there was no danger. The Swami could give me what I was looking for.

## Chapter 4 - The World Will Think I Am Crazy

What had been a gift shop at 26 Second Avenue was now an empty storefront. On your right as you looked in from the street, was a display window and on the left, the door. As soon as you put a foot inside, you were struck by that feeling of mysterious peacefulness that fills empty rooms. Just behind the door was a space to leave your shoes, and the room was bare except for some floor mats for sitting. As you walked along the left wall you came upon a door that opened onto a hallway and a little farther back, an added-on room with a door that opened onto a toilet. On the outside of the little room was a sink, and high up on the back wall of the storefront was a small window that let in light from a courtyard. There was just one problem: The hook-and-eye lock on the toilet door was too small. A couple times people innocently pulled on the door and the lock came undone, exposing someone inside using the toilet. At least it was always some hippie guy who took it with a laugh.

Howard and I were standing near this little room talking one afternoon, when Ray sauntered up to us. A young man with light brown hair, a neatly trimmed beard, and a soft-spoken New York accent, Ray was a writer, often working on comic books. “There’s a class in the Swami’s apartment every morning at 6:30,” he said. “Why don’t you come?” “What’s the name on the doorbell?” I said.

“Bhaktivedanta,” said Ray.

“Bhaktivedanta,” I said. “That’s nice.” I felt a little proud of myself, having understood a Sanskrit name. Bhakti meant devotion to God. Vedanta meant Vedic wisdom.

The next day as I walked over to the temple, the early morning magic was waiting for me on Second Avenue. I treated my eyes to the soft morning colors, took a deep whiff of the fresh clean air, listened to the sound of the birds behind the hushed traffic. But today was different. Instead of having been partying all night, I was just waking up, with the whole day in front of me. Still a bit sleepy, I opened the door next to the storefront and walked down a hallway. Halfway down, on the right side, was the door that led into the temple. At the far end, the courtyard door was locked. I pushed the “Bhaktivedanta” doorbell button and waited till I heard the buzz of the electric lock opening the door.

I walked across the courtyard into a rear building, up to the second floor, and into the Swami’s apartment. I heard people chanting softly as I stood in a little space just inside the door. On my left was the kitchen. On my right was the bathroom. In front of me were two rooms. The Swami was in the right-hand room sitting behind a floor-level desk along with a few students also sitting on the floor. I took a few steps and stood just outside the room, looking in. The walls were bathed in daylight coming through the window while a clean, faint smell of mustard oil and incense made me wonder whether I was still in New York. Or maybe I had walked into an ancient world far from the chaos of the Lower East Side, a world I almost remembered from long, long ago, before this lifetime.

I stepped gingerly into the room, found a place, and sat down with the others. They were chanting with the Swami, but softly so as not to wake up the neighbors. The Swami sat on a floor cushion behind the little desk, gently playing a pair of kartals, Indian cymbals a few inches wide, traditionally used with chanting. Then he gave a class on the Bhagavad-gita.

I was watching the Swami as much as listening to him. His eyes were large and bright, his ears had hairs sticking out, his long golden-brown fingers ended in perfectly manicured nails, and his bearing looked relaxed yet intense, a contrast matched by the contrast in his words: soft in tone but carrying a feeling of power and conviction. Was he really that sure of what he was saying? I knew he had something, and I wanted it, but the Swami was declaring war on all I had ever read or heard, even the widely cherished theory of evolution.

“Have there always been people?” Howard had asked one evening.

“Yes,” answered the Swami.

The soul, the Swami had been telling us, had always existed and was the same in all species of life, but we had to wait for a human birth to understand that each of us had an eternal relationship of love with Krishna. This was the Swami’s message. I had never before seen it in any book or heard it in any lecture. In my Jewish upbringing, I had never once heard about loving God, nor did the scowling, punishing God of the Old Testament seem lovable. But here was Krishna, God as a smiling, warm-hearted young boy sporting with friends and playing a flute, the hero who danced in a garden.

Suddenly the class ended. Ray came up to me. “Would you like to stay for a class on Sanskrit?” he asked. I was new and not quite comfortable, so I declined and left.

But chanting was the way. One afternoon Howard and I drove out to the beach with our friends Larry and Barbara and their two-year-old son, Chris. The swimming season was finished, the weather had turned just a bit cool, and the beach was almost deserted, just a few people here and there.

Howard had a pair of kartal cymbals (I didn’t know where he had gotten them. Had the Swami brought some extras from India?) so while Barbara and Larry strolled along the shore and Chris played in the sand, Howard and I sang the mantra as loud as we could — no neighbors to worry about — till late afternoon. But how was it that I chanted all afternoon without getting tired? Was it the rhythm? or the way the word pattern changed? The next afternoon, I went upstairs to see the Swami and found him sitting behind his floor- level desk typing. I always found the Swami doing something, maybe writing or chanting on beads or talking with people, never just loafing around. As I walked in and sat down, he looked up. “Have you been chanting?” he asked. I wondered how he knew.

“Yes,” I said. “And the mantra changes just enough so that you don’t get tired of it.” The Swami’s eyes opened wide. He leaned forward. “Yes!” he said.

So I took up the chanting, but soon I decided to enhance it. What would it be like on LSD? The Swami had wasted no time telling us about the rules: no sex outside of marriage, no gambling, and no fish or meat. What about eggs? We weren’t sure. One evening Keith and I came upon a young girl we knew from the temple, sitting on a bench outside munching on a preserved duck egg from Chinatown.

“We’re not supposed to eat eggs, are we?” I said.

“It’s OK if it’s something the animal gives,” said Keith.

And no intoxicants, including drugs, alcohol, and tobacco.

Soon the Swami posted a written notice about the rules. Eggs were out, and so were coffee and tea. But I hadn’t made any commitment, so late one night at John’s place I swallowed an LSD sugar cube. I timed it so I would still be high, but not too much, when I went to the morning class. Surely I would have an astounding revelation, maybe even see light shooting out from the Swami’s head. But as soon as I walked in the room and saw the Swami sitting behind his desk, the high vanished. I was sober — no revelation, no rays of light. I never tried it again. Nor, as I later found out, was I the only one.

Keith, Howard, and I told people about the Swami, and our friend Joanie came to the storefront for the evening program, immaculately dressed as usual, her long never-been-cut hair in one braid.

Although she was part of the Lower East Side, she kept one foot in the straight world, always managing to look like a nice Jewish girl from the suburbs. After the others had left, Joanie and I stayed. The Swami was sitting on the floor behind a little table, and we sat in front of him, wanting to say something, wanting to resonate with this magnificent person, but unable to find the words. The Swami opened a guestbook. “Would you like to sign our guestbook?” he asked gently.

Wasn’t it a little strange, though, that the Swami asked me to sign a guestbook when I was one of the regulars? But I signed it along with Joanie. Suddenly I felt at ease, a little child befriended by a kind-hearted person. Then Joanie left, and we did not hear about her for some weeks.

On Mott Street, Keith made up a morning schedule for everyone: “5:00 rise, shower, meditate “5:30 tea, meditate...

But only Keith followed it. He complained, sometimes angrily, about not getting enough sleep because people were always coming and going, so he moved into the temple. He and a few others slept in the storefront. Everyone showered in the Swami’s apartment, and the Swami waited in line for his turn.

One day Keith came to Mott Street to talk with Howard and me in the sitting room. “The Swami is such an amazing man,” he said, still standing. “I’m going to become his disciple, and he said my name will be Krishna das brahmachari. ‘Krishna das’ means ‘servant of Krishna’ and ‘brahmachari’ means ‘one who never marries.’” Although Keith had started cooking every day and some of the other men had also taken up a regular service, I still didn’t want to commit myself. It wouldn’t be freedom. But then, what if the Swami were to take a plane somewhere and then a bus and then walk through thick forests with just a few disciples to a remote mountain cave and whisper the secret teachings? I wanted to get in on it, so I went upstairs to see the Swami one evening as he sat at his desk, typing. I spoke first: “Is there something I can do for you?” “Yes,” said the Swami. “You can take notes in class and write them up.” He answered without hesitation, as if he had already known even before I asked. It sounded perfect for me though I had never written much. “This will be good for you,” the Swami added. “And you can come and eat with us.” There was a typewriter in the storefront I could use. I started the next morning, but I did not know what format to put the notes in. I came up with a question-and-answer style.

At lunchtime, I went upstairs and joined the Swami and the others sitting in a circle on the floor in the room next to the office, the pots of food and some paper plates in front of the Swami. The room was almost bare, just a small table next to the window with two pictures: one of Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Saraswati (The Swami’s spiritual master) and one showing five men standing side by side with arms upraised. I didn’t know who they were.

Keith had now learned basic Indian cooking, but, said the Swami, this was not ordinary food. It was first offered to Krishna, and then we accepted it as prasada, Krishna’s mercy. “Even if this kind of food falls on the floor, you can eat it,” the Swami said. The Swami made up the plates and passed them around. But we had to wait. First the Swami recited what seemed like an endless Bengali prayer, phrase by phrase, and we repeated after him: “Sarira avidya jal...” Me, I just wanted to eat. I felt like this ancient Greek guy whose punishment in hell was to always have a feast set before him but just out of reach.

We ate with our fingers, Indian style, and I started by tearing off a piece of chapati, a brownish flatbread giving off the irresistible aroma of freshly toasted whole wheat with butter. Then I went for the vegetables, diced and stir-fried with spices that made my gums tingle. I took in a handful of rice with them, bland white grains that soaked up the taste and pushed it everywhere in my mouth. And finally, the dal, a lightly spiced soup of tiny split yellow mung beans. At the end of the meal, my mouth felt hot. The Swami mashed some of the soft Indian rice pudding on his plate together with a piece of banana, using his fingers, and gave some to everyone. Just one bite and my mouth felt cool.

That evening, as I trudged up the stairs to show the Swami my day’s notes, I felt humiliated, having to turn in my work like a schoolboy. True, I also had to please people when I worked as a recording engineer, but I could show off my professional skills. “How can you remember what all these dials and knobs do?” people would ask. “Oh, well,” I would say, “you know, there’s a certain logic to it,” and dismiss it all with a nonchalant wave of my hand, big genius that I was. But now I was a little boy handing in an assignment, sitting in front of the teacher’s desk.

“Here is what I’ve done today,” I said, reaching over and handing my work to the Swami. I watched him as he turned his head down to read my work. I hadn’t noticed before, but the shape of his lips reminded me of classical Indian sculpture, and now the downward tilt of his head made his cheeks puff out slightly, giving his face an angelic look. There was, in fact, a certain innocence about the Swami, almost like the innocence of a vulnerable child, though the Swami was not a child. He could speak profoundly on any topic, and he had come by himself from Calcutta to New York. Then what exactly gave him this air of innocence? I could not tell. It was not the way he talked or acted or sat or stood or any other outward sign, though it seemed most obvious at moments like this one, when he had a serious expression.

While I was lost in my thoughts, the Swami took a paper from his desk and reached over to hand it to me. It was a page from an essay he was working on, “Who Is Crazy?” And now I knew how to write up the notes: like an essay. Again, I suddenly felt at ease, even complimented. The Swami had taught me just by showing me something, as if he had seen some intelligence in me. “We will make a book out of these,” said the Swami, “and we will call it Practical Theology.” One day, standing in the prasada room after lunch, I challenged Keith. “No matter how hot you make the soup, I’ll still be able to drink it.” It was a challenge made of bodily pride. Although Jewish cooking is not hot like Mexican or Indian cooking, it is spicier than what the Christians from northern Europe are used to. I could show off my cultural superiority. I looked at the Swami — lunch had just ended, and he was still sitting there — and he smiled. Keith said nothing, but I knew he had accepted the challenge. Day by day, the soup got hotter and hotter, but I always drank it. Then one day it was back to normal.

“What happened?” I asked Keith.

“It got so hot you were the only one who could drink it,” he said. My pride was satisfied. The Jews had won. But the Swami quashed my Jewish pride one day as a few of us sat with him in his office. When I started to mention that Jews cover meat with salt to draw out the blood, the Swami stopped me in mid-sentence with a wave of his hand. As Howard told me later, “The Swami just doesn’t like to talk about meat.” One time, Howard and I came into the apartment and saw the Swami in the kitchen, standing at the stove deep-frying pakoras, vegetable slices coated with chickpea batter. Howard and I thought it looked like fried chicken. “Oh,” we said laughing, “you are making fried chicken.” The Swami looked up. “Not fried chicken,” he said in a serious tone.

Another time as we sat in the prasada room after lunch, I asked the Swami a question. “If we’re outside and we want to eat something, how can we offer it? By chanting Hare Krishna?” “Why do you want to eat outside?” said the Swami. “You can eat here.” I squirmed a little. I didn’t want to feel dependent on the temple, to have to be somewhere at a certain time every day just to eat. It wouldn’t be freedom. “What if I have to?” I said. “Can I chant Hare Krishna?” “Yes,” said the Swami.

The Swami was busy translating scriptures, and Howard worked in the room where we took prasada, polishing the English on the manuscripts. One day I decided to work upstairs next to Howard. It was quieter. I could get more done. But once Howard and I sat together, we could not resist chatting. The Swami walked into the room. “You must work, not talk,” he said.

So we went back to work, but soon we started talking again. And again the Swami walked into the room. “You must not talk,” he said. “If you continue to talk, then Wally will have to work downstairs.” Eventually I had to go downstairs.

“Look at this,” said Howard one day as he showed me a passage he was editing. “It’s far out.” There it was again: Vishnu creating the world while lying on a gigantic snake. But now I could accept it. When I saw it in an art book, surely written by someone who did not believe it, I thought it to be some ancient Oriental legend. But when I saw it in the Swami’s book, in the context of the scriptures, I understood it. God is not an idle, bad-tempered old man who sits all day on a throne surrounded by angels. He is a handsome young hero. He fights against bad guys and plays a flute and dances in a garden. If He wants to lie on a gigantic many-headed snake and create the material universes, who is to tell Him He can’t? But could I tell these things to others? Could I ever walk into a recording session and announce that God created the world lying on a snake? People would say that this young man, once proud and sophisticated, had now become a mindless unblinking follower of some hocus-pocus religion. I would feel crushed under such a reputation even though I knew I was right. I would just have to mentally hold my breath for now and put the conflict into suspension. Maybe some day I could resolve it.

## Chapter 5 - It’s Krishna

I sat on the floor with a few others in the Swami’s office repairing something one afternoon while the Swami worked at his floor-level desk. I looked up or a moment. The room was light and sunny, so much so that I wondered how it all came through that little window. Suddenly my hand slipped, and the blade of my screwdriver hit my fingertip. I felt a sharp stinging pain. Had I cut myself? I looked. It seemed all right at first, but suddenly a little round drop of blood appeared.

Better to wrap it with something. I looked up at the Swami, but he was busy. I shouldn’t disturb him.

On the other hand, I might smear blood on something. “Do you have a piece of cloth?” I asked. The Swami tossed me an old typewriter ribbon as he continued to work. I hesitated: an inky ribbon on a cut? I put it down.

Then the Swami looked over at me. “Oh,” he said, “I didn’t know you had cut yourself. I’m sorry.” I shrugged. After all, it was barely more than a scratch. “Oh, that’s all right.” “I’m sorry,” said the Swami.

“That’s all right,” I said again, but the Swami apologized a few more times. I was seeing another side of the Swami now. He had many things to take care of — writing books, teaching disciples, spreading Krishna consciousness — yet he was apologizing over a tiny cut. He was a real person. I got the same surprise another time in the Swami’s office, when I was half kneeling, half sitting on one heel. “Sit nicely,” the Swami said. “You cannot be comfortable like that.” As the summer moved on, the Swami began holding the morning class downstairs in the storefront, half an hour later, at seven o’clock. Alas! It was still too early for Bob, a thin guy with sharp but pleasing facial features. Poor Bob, he would sit there with a blanket hanging from the back of his head around his shoulders, face downcast, dark curly hair still mussed up from sleeping, as he struggled to keep his heavy eyelids from clanking shut. It was almost enough to break your heart, this picture of human misery.

The Swami started telling us about Krishna’s activities, or pastimes, as he preferred to call them. When Krishna was little, He was a naughty boy. He and His friends sneaked into the houses of the neighbors when no one was home and feasted on the butter and yogurt stored there. “But it gave the neighbors pleasure,” the Swami said.

Pleasure? How could this, of all things, give anyone pleasure? Can you feel pleasure if someone is stealing your butter? But the Swami said it again. “It gave them pleasure.” In Krishna’s time, five thousand years ago, wealth was measured in stocks of butter along with other tangible things like cows and grains, but not electronic bank statements. Butter will keep indefinitely in the form of ghee, the pure oil extracted by slow heating.

I have a love affair with ghee. I go mad for the taste it gives to anything cooked or flavored with it. And I have company. Not only was ghee the secret ingredient of the famous French chefs in the early 1900s, but as the scriptures tell us, on the higher planets of this universe, where people live for thousands of years in heavenly surroundings, all cooking is done with ghee. And I can well believe it.

Just a jar of ghee in my cupboard is enough to make me feel like a rich man no matter how broke I may be. I feel happy just looking at it, especially when it’s newly made and hasn’t cooled yet, when it’s like liquid gold but it’s transparent and I can look through it and see all the way to the bottom of the pot, and the buttery-caramely fragrance fills the whole house.

And God likes it too. Naughty little Krishna even steals it. But why would God, who can create tons of butter with a wave of His hand, pilfer it from the neighbors? Because it’s more fun that way. Krishna knew that the neighbors, unaware of His divine status, were hiding, waiting to laugh at the mischievous little boy sneaking into their houses as he glanced here and there, seemingly afraid of getting caught. The sight filled them with pleasureful love, worth the sacrifice of a little butter.

Butter and other milk products along with the cows that provide them figure prominently in Krishna’s childhood pastimes. And though the Swami spoke freely of these pastimes, some other pastimes had to wait.

My curiosity about these other pastimes started with the Swami’s correspondence. He kept his many letters and other papers well organized, not in cabinets or folders though, but in a stack of boxes and suitcases in the closet, a small room opening onto the office. It was just big enough to walk into and hang up some clothes or store a few things. Whatever document the Swami might need, he could tell someone just where to find it. “In the third suitcase from the bottom,” he might say, “in the middle is a letter to the American consulate in Delhi.” And the letter would be there.

One day I was in the prasada room retyping a letter for the Swami. The letter was in English, but it was for someone in India, and I came across the words “Sri Sri Radha Krishna.” Sri is a title of respect in India, but Sri Sri? Not only that, but one Sri was at the end of a line and the other was at the beginning of the next, a common mistake in the days of mechanical typewriters, when you had to reach over and push a lever to go to the next line. You might forget you had just typed a short word and then type it again. I got up and walked next door into the Swami’s office. The Swami was standing. “Sri Sri Radha Krishna?” I said.

“Yes.” “Oh,” I said, “I thought maybe it was a mistake.” The Swami looked at me sternly. “When I write in your language,” he said forcefully, “you can correct me, but when I write in my language you can not correct me!” Someone came into the room, and the Swami turned to him. “I told him he could correct me when I write in his language, and now he wants to correct me when I write in my language.” I slunk back to the next room and finished the letter.

The Swami was right to scold me. How could I think I could spot a mistake in Bengali, a language I knew nothing about? Nor was there any mistake. Sri could be repeated when speaking of more than one person, like Radha and Krishna.

But who was Radha? I knew only that She was a young girl often pictured standing next to Krishna. We had all heard about Krishna’s dancing with the girls of his village, as I had seen in my book of Indian art, and one day we asked the Swami about it, but he brushed our question aside: “When you first meet someone, you cannot ask about the confidential details of his life. You have to know the person first.” I had to think about this. I was not studying some mystical process from a meditation book. I was being introduced to a real person named Krishna, and of course, I could not walk up to someone I had just met and say, “Hi. Tell me about your girlfriends.” The Swami was taking me by the hand and introducing me to God: “Krishna, this is Wally. Wally, this is Krishna.” I had to see it that way. And I would have wait.

In the morning classes, the Swami was unyielding, rejecting everything but bhakti, worship of Krishna, often attacking the Buddhists and Ramakrishna, a famous religious figure who lived in Bengal in the late 1800s. And he attacked the impersonalists, though I had never heard of them. But why wasn’t the Swami liberal? Might not some of the other paths be even better than bhakti? Certainly one could attain perfection through bhakti — the Swami was proof — but it seemed like an indirect path, a concession for those who could not meditate on formlessness. Why not embrace all paths? Then one day during the morning class in the storefront, we came to chapter 6, verse 47: “Of all yogis, one who worships Me with great faith is the highest of all.” “You see?” said the Swami, “It says in Bhagavad-gita that the devotee of Krishna is the highest yogi.” I had seen that verse before, stated in plain language but impossible to understand till I heard it from the Swami. And it could not mean anything else, although some commentators said that Krishna did not really mean what He said. But then, if He didn’t mean it, why did He say it? The Swami had just swept away everything I thought I knew about yoga. I could not argue with the Bhagavad-gita.

At that time the Swami was still working on his Bhagavad-gita As It Is, so we would read other editions — the verses but not the commentaries — mostly by Dr. Radhakrishnan, a former president of India, whom the Swami called an impersonalist. But again, what was an impersonalist? I finally found out.

“Today,” said the Swami during a morning class, “I will prove that Dr. Radhakrishnan is an impersonalist.” He turned to Ray. “Read chapter 9, verse 34.” Ray read: “On Me fix thy mind; to Me be devoted; worship Me; revere Me; thus having disciplined thyself, with Me as thy goal, to Me shalt thou come.” “Now read the commentary,” said the Swami.

Ray read: “It is not the personal Krishna to whom we have to give ourselves up utterly, but the unborn, beginningless, eternal who speaks through Krishna.” “You see?” said the Swami. “That proves Dr. Radhakrishnan is an impersonalist.” But what did it prove? Dr. Radhakrishnan’s explanation — the inner unborn truth speaking through someone — sounded like all the other books I had read. I looked around. Everyone else seemed puzzled too, except Keith.

“He’s right,” said Keith emphatically. “It’s the unborn eternal within Krishna.” And as Keith babbled on and on about the unborn, the Swami’s face started turning red... and redder... and redder still. I had never seen anyone turn so red before.

Suddenly Keith stopped.

“Are you finished?” said the Swami impatiently.

“No,” said Keith as he started on another discourse. Finally he stopped.

“Are you finished?” the Swami asked.

“Yes.” “Do you understand what we have been saying?” said the Swami. “Do you understand that Krishna is the absolute truth?” “Yes,” said Keith.

The Swami slammed his hand on the little table in front of him and stood up with force. “Then why do you want to take it away from Krishna!” he shouted. “It’s Krishna! It’s Krishna! It’s not some unborn within Krishna! It’s Krishna! It’s Krishna!” The Swami looked magnificent, like a lion roaring. I had never seen so much power in a person, so much that I almost felt the room shake. (And I was glad I wasn’t the one who had said something.) The Swami stopped. The storefront became silent, so quiet I almost thought I could hear the sunlight coming in through the window. Keith looked bewildered.

“It’s like Jesus,” someone said to Keith.

“It’s like reading the Bible and saying ‘Not Jesus,’” someone added.

Later that day, Howard and I were talking at Mott Street.

“Swami yelled at Keith,” said Howard.

But this time I took the Swami’s side. We might like it or we might not, but this was what the Bhagavad-Gita said. “The Swami’s right,” I answered. “You’ve read the Gita before, but did you ever think of worshiping Krishna? Did it ever occur to any of us that Krishna Himself was the absolute truth?” Howard nodded.

The Swami had made his point. The supreme absolute truth was a person. And that person was Krishna. Look no further.

## Chapter 6 - A Preacher Has to Attack

“Howard, read your paper,” said the Swami.

It was late morning. Howard looked around the office at the guests sitting there, then cleared his throat and began in his deep booming voice: “There I am, whirling through eternity...” Although Howard’s paper, about his highs on LSD, had little to do with Krishna Consciousness, the Swami seemed pleased and had him read it when guests came. The guests listened, but I had heard it many times. I watched the Swami instead. I thought he looked like a happy father encouraging his talented little boy and showing him off while the mystical tale kept the people spellbound.

LSD was finished for us. It was still going strong on the Lower East Side though. Young searchers following the carrot of truth-in-a-pill were making it a way of life, as had Keith, Howard, and I. Even a theater marquee on Second Avenue was proclaiming the glories of LSD. Timothy Leary, another professor fired from Harvard for giving LSD to students, was holding a lecture series on psychedelic enlightenment.

“But he’s not hurting anyone,” someone said.

“He is killing them,” the Swami said gravely.

No LSD — that was part of the rule forbidding intoxicants, including drugs, alcohol, tobacco, coffee, and tea. What? Wait a minute. No tea? How could tea be sinful? Little old ladies drank it. I should have known that little old ladies, sweet and innocent as they may seem, holding out their pinkies as they lift the cup, are also sinful. They eat meat, and many enjoy a cigarette and a sip of brandy.

Not that I cared about tea — what was tea worth for a drug user? — but I objected on principle.

So I went upstairs to see the Swami. “What’s wrong with tea?” I asked. “How can tea be sinful?” After a bit of arguing back and forth, the Swami relented. “Then drink tea,” he said, “but chant Hare Krishna.” I did not drink any tea.

Around that time, Keith, Howard, and I heard that our friend Joanie, the young woman who had signed the guestbook with me, was pregnant somewhere in California. Her next step: abortion, the usual remedy. That evening we went upstairs to see the Swami and sat down around his desk. “A girl we know is pregnant,” I said, “and she is going to have an abortion.” “She must not have an abortion,” the Swami said.

“But what else can she do?” I said.

“She must not have an abortion,” the Swami said again.

“I could marry her,” said Keith. “Would that be all right?” I looked over at Keith: no money, no job, old baggy clothes — the last man in the world a nice Jewish girl from the suburbs would marry.

“She is pregnant by you?” the Swami asked.

“No,” said Keith, “but I could marry her so she won’t have an abortion.” “Let me think about it,” said the Swami. “I will tell you in the morning.

The next morning, I saw Keith. “What did the Swami say?” I asked. “Are you going to marry Joanie?” “No,” Keith replied. “The Swami said, ‘Are you going to marry every girl that gets pregnant?’” As it turned out, Joanie was not pregnant. It was only a rumor, which I later learned she knew nothing about.

But next time? Next person? the Swami’s little flock, as he well knew, had a devil-may-care attitude about sex. It was natural, right? Why worry about a mere social custom like marriage? But the Swami had a different idea of “natural.” Unrestricted sense gratification may be natural for the animals — even the dogs and hogs enjoy sense gratification — but sense control is natural for human beings. “Krishna consciousness and sense gratification go ill together,” he told us. Sex should be restricted to marriage, and the Swami himself, the father of five, had followed the principle. “I never knew any woman except my wife,” he said during an evening class in the storefront.

“You boys should all get married,” the Swami said one afternoon to a few of us sitting in his office.

“But Swami,” someone said, “no girl today will wait until marriage to have sex.” “Do not worry about the girl’s past,” the Swami said.

Seeing the Swami’s unfailing intelligence and his practical, down-to-earth advice, I began to wonder how far his knowledge went. “Can a devotee tell the future?” someone asked one afternoon in the office.

“It is like this,” the Swami said. “When winter comes and the sky turns gray, the father tells his little boy, ‘Soon it will snow.’ Then it snows, and the little boy thinks, ‘My father knows everything.’” One afternoon the Swami spoke about organizing an official society: ISKCON, the International Society for Krishna Consciousness. Someone had another idea. “Why not the International Society for God Consciousness?” “No,” said the Swami. “Krishna Consciousness. What do you think?” We all nodded in agreement, though I, for one, did not yet understand why the name Krishna was better than the name God. It took time to understand that the name God tells you only one thing: that He is God. The name Krishna reminds you that He is the child who steals butter and the handsome dark hero who plays a flute and dances in a garden.

Krishna also appears in other forms. He came as Nara Simha, in a manlike body with the head and claws of a lion, to save His great devotee, the little boy Prahlada Maharaja, from murder by the boy’s own father. Today, millions of years later, devotees still turn to Nara Simha for protection.

Krishna came as Rama, the perfect king, who fought a war on the island of Sri Lanka to rescue his kidnapped wife, Sita. Millions of devotees still worship Sita and Rama. Krishna and Rama are the same person, but those who worship Him as Krishna and those who worship Him as Rama have a friendly rivalry with joking words, as the Swami told us one day.

“You worship Krishna?” say the devotees of Rama. “He was a thief and a woman-hunter.” “You worship Rama?” say the devotees of Krishna. “He couldn’t even protect His own wife.” “You worship Krishna?” say the devotees of Rama. “When Radha went through the forest at night to be with Krishna, She prayed to Rama for protection.” “And so it goes all day,” said the Swami. “It’s Krishna! It’s Rama!” But while we all laughed at the fun, I had another thought. Rama’s rescue of Sita and Krishna’s stealing butter and dancing with the village girls were subjects of deep meditation by great sages.

Should we make light of what God does? Unless — and this would be a startling revelation — unless God has a sense of humor. And He does. He enjoys the fun. He enjoys being teased by His devotees and worshiped in joking words. But joking about God would have certainly brought frowns and furrowed brows from my Hebrew school teachers, nor had I ever come across a God who enjoys a laugh with His devotees, a God with a sense of fun. But here was Krishna, the right kind of God, a God I could learn to love.

The Swami told us about Krishna’s village, Vrindavan. “As soon as you get off the train in Vrindavan,” he said, “you become Krishna conscious.” Just by getting off the train? A place where you got spiritual knowledge just by setting foot there? I knew I had to go there, but I didn’t know yet how many years I would have to wait.

Once, as we gathered in the Swami’s office after the evening class, he asked us if we enjoyed hearing him quote Sanskrit verses when he lectured. I hadn’t thought about it, but yes, I liked the feeling of authenticity it gave to the class. I liked the rhythm of the language and the power in the Swami’s voice when he spoke it.

Chanting the Hare Krishna mantra loudly, as when singing the mantra with others, is called kirtan \[KEER-tahn\], and during the program one evening, the singing struck me as especially sweet and melodious. It filled my whole body with pleasure. I almost started laughing with joy. I would have sung and danced all night if I could have. I mentioned it when we gathered in the Swami’s office after the program. “The kirtan was so sweet,” I said. “I was hoping it would never end.” “It makes me very happy to hear you say that,” said the Swami. “It gives me great pleasure.” I felt complimented. I had done something right.

The next time we gathered after an evening program, I thought I might like another compliment, so I said the same thing: “The kirtan was so sweet. I was hoping it would never end.” But the words sounded like empty talk, even to me. The Swami looked at me with narrowed eyes as if to say, “Who are you trying to fool?” I had underestimated the Swami. Obviously he knew I was just looking for a compliment.

When the Swami told us we would eventually get spiritual bodies, I began to understand that I was that eternal soul Krishna talks about in the Bhagavad-gita, and like every other soul in this world, I had come through countless lives of pain and suffering. I had been a dog, I had been a cat, a tree, a worm, everything. Now I had a human body, but I could also become a dog or a worm again.

Even if my next body was human, it could be white or black or yellow or red. Too bad, O prejudiced white man, but that black boy you are spitting on was your dear sweet grandmother in his last life.

But Grandma got a human body. Not everyone is so lucky. “The householder is worshiping the picture of his dead father,” said the Swami, “but his father is now a cockroach in the toilet.” If I was intelligent, I would use this human life for worshiping God and escape the cycle of birth and death.

The Swami was vocal in his disdain for those so-called holy men of India who taught anything less, especially those who claimed to be God themselves. “He is not God,” said the Swami, “He is dog.” I was standing in the prasada room one afternoon, looking into the Swami’s office through a window between the two rooms when I saw two young men come in and sit down. “We are on our way to India,” said one of them. “We are going to see Meher Baba.” Meher Baba... I had seen that name in big letters on a poster in Weiser’s book store, in the mystical section, a favorite browsing spot for hippies. The poster showed a man’s face with a bushy mustache. At the bottom was a quotation: “I was Rama, I was Krishna, I was this one, I was that one, and now I am Meher Baba.” At the top, big letters proclaimed “Avatar Meher Baba.” I had heard the name before, but what was this “avatar” about? Did it mean something? Before I met the Swami, Indian spirituality was a jumble of gods and saints, past, recent, and present. No way to know who was who and what was what.

Now it was becoming unscrambled. At least I knew what an avatar was. When the Supreme Lord comes into this world, He is known as an avatar, like Krishna or like Lord Chaitanya standing with arms up in the picture on the Swami’s altar. My mind came back to the present as I looked through the window at the two young men talking with the Swami.

“Meher Baba is an avatar,” said one of them.

“If I say I am an avatar, will you believe me?” said the Swami.

“No,” said the young man.

“Then why do you believe Meher Baba?” said the Swami. “You have never even seen him.” Although the Swami’s answer was simple, he had gotten to the heart of the man’s illogical thinking in a split second with no time to mull it over. Later the Swami told us that Meher Baba had once been injured in a traffic accident in the United States. “God does not have auto accidents,” the Swami said.

Another day, looking through the window, I saw a young man talking with the Swami about Ramakrishna, a 19th-century Bengali, also popular among the hippies. “People say that Ramakrishna was not really enlightened,” said the young man, “because he worshiped at the temple of the goddess Kali.” The Swami nodded in agreement.

I understood the exchange. The Bhagavad-gita condemned worship of the gods of the material world — the Swami called them demigods — like the fire god, the moon god, or the goddess Kali. It was religion for the ignorant. But how did the young man know this? Were other people also saying it? Still, I felt uneasy, and I talked about it one afternoon with Howard on Mott Street as the cat merrily rolled in a little pile of catnip. (She, at least, could still get high even if I couldn’t.) Not only had Ramakrishna been one of my favorites, but the Swami had been attacking him in the morning classes. Although I admired the Swami’s sharp logic and quick thinking, I began to feel that his criticism of popular holy men like Ramakrishna and sometimes of Buddhists was unbefitting a man of God.

“Maybe we should talk about it with the Swami,” I said.

“This is not ‘Come let us reason together,’” said Howard.

I knew what he meant. The Swami could not be budged. So Howard and I stopped going to the temple. After a day or so, we were talking in the sitting room when Keith came in.

“I’ve decided to leave the Swami,” Keith said as he walked into the room. “I don’t like what’s going on.” “That’s just what we were talking about,” I said.

“You fool!” Keith shouted. “Do you think I would ever leave the Swami?” I looked at Howard. We had been tricked.

“We just don’t like all these things the Swami is saying about the Buddhists and Ramakrishna,” I said.

“Then you should talk about it with him,” said Keith.

“He won’t understand,” I said. “His English isn’t good enough.” “He’ll understand,” said Keith. “Let’s go.” As we walked in, the Swami was sitting on a cushion behind his floor-level desk. The late- morning light was coming in through the window along with the sound of children playing outside.

The clean smell of mustard oil faintly permeated the air. I sat down in front of the Swami, and Howard sat to one side. “You have not been coming,” the Swami said. “Is there some problem? You should not think you cannot talk about things with me.” I gathered up my courage. “We don’t like these things you are saying about the Buddhists and Sri Ramakrishna,” I said.

“I have never criticized Lord Buddha,” the Swami said. “I always call him Lord Buddha, but the Buddhists are atheists, and they do not believe in the soul. Can you show me anywhere in the Buddhist scriptures where they talk about the soul?” I thought about a vague concept called “buddha nature,” but I didn’t mention it.

“And the Buddhists eat meat,” the Swami continued. “Lord Buddha came to stop animal slaughter, and the Buddhists eat meat.” The Swami stopped for a second.

“As far as Ramakrishna goes,” he continued, “I have always said, and I will always say, that he was nothing but a crazy priest.” “What about all the wonderful things he said?” I asked.

“He didn’t say those things,” the Swami answered. “Those things are all in the Vedas. After he died, his followers took things from the Vedas and put them in books to make it look as if he had said them. They wanted this Gadadhar Chattopadhyay, Ramakrishna, to be accepted as the incarnation for this age, so they tried to make him look like Lord Chaitanya.” “And Ramakrishna worshiped the goddess Kali,” the Swami continued. “In the Bhagavad-gita, Krishna says that people who worship the demigods have no intelligence. Ramakrishna said that by worshiping Kali you get the same result as by worshiping Krishna, but the Bhagavad-gita says that if you worship the demigods you will go to the demigods and if you worship Krishna you will go to Krishna.” I nodded. The Swami was right. Ramakrishna did contradict the Gita.

“Ramakrishna is not respected by the holy men in India,” the Swami said. “Everyone knows that people in the Ramakrishna Mission eat meat. They get a letter from a doctor saying that if they don’t eat meat they will die. One time, during a large gathering of holy men, there was a dead fish floating on the water. ‘Quick! Call the Ramakrishna Mission!’ someone said, and everybody laughed.” People at the Ramakrishna Mission ate meat? But surely the Swami would know.

“His main disciple was Vivekananda. If you ever read the speech Vivekananda gave in Chicago, you will see it is all nonsense. There was even an American scholar who went up to Vivekananda after the speech and said, ‘You are from India and you are saying these things?’ “The first time Vivekananda went to see Ramakrishna, Ramakrishna touched him on the forehead, and Vivekananda felt electric shocks and fainted.” The Swami chuckled. “You have read Bhagavad-gita, the Universal Form. Where does it say anything about electric shocks? When Vivekananda woke up, Ramakrishna was crying.

“Vivekananda said, ‘Why are you crying?’ “Ramakrishna said, ‘I have given you all my power. I have none left.’” I had read that story. Ramakrishna generously gave away his power.

“So,” the Swami continued, “is spiritual knowledge like money? If I give it to you, I have none left?” My mind did a flip-flop. I hadn’t thought of the story in that light. “No,” I said. “The more you give, the more you have.” (It seemed like the right answer.) The Swami had convinced us. He paused. “A preacher has to attack,” he said.

I reflected for a moment. The Swami had yelled at me during the conversation, but no one yelled at little Wally and got away with it. Still, I dared not show any anger to the Swami, so I gritted my teeth and braced myself for the wave of resentment that was sure to come. But it didn’t come. Only the Swami could do this. Only he could yell at me and make me accept it.

It was lunchtime. The Swami looked at me. “Let us go take prasada.” (Though I knew that many people in Bengal accepted Ramakrishna as a great religious figure, I learned years later that many others there called him Pagal Thakur, “crazy holy-man,” or as the Swami said, “crazy priest.”)

## Chapter 7 - Want to Get Initiated?

It was lunchtime. The sunlight was shining in through the windows in the prasada room as I sat with the others, now about twenty five in number, while we chomped and slurped and gobbled down the rice, vegetables, dal soup, and whole wheat chapati breads. And eating everything with our fingers, except the soup. Besides being more fun, eating with the fingers made everything taste better.

According to Indian tradition, it is also healthier, as touching the food stimulates the digestion. The Swami was standing near the doorway, almost directly in front of me. “When I was on the boat coming to America,” he said, “I did not know whether the American boys and girls would like this food.” We all had a good laugh, even the Swami.

I always felt reassured when I saw the Swami laugh. It was a change from the pressure and the fear of a strong rebuke I often felt in my dealings with him. Things had to be done well and done efficiently. Though I knew, of course, that the Swami would never harm me or reject me, I began to feel that my life depended on pleasing him. So whenever he laughed, as he did that day in the prasada room, the world was all right.

And the Swami urged us on: “Take more. Take more.” But one morning during class in the storefront, he said something else: “You should eat as little as possible.” I felt a knot in my chest when I heard that, so when the Swami asked for questions, I raised my hand. “You have been telling us to eat a lot,” I said, “but now you are telling us to eat as little as possible. How can we know which instruction to follow?” (Surely this was a clever way of objecting while sounding sincere at the same time.) “When I told you to eat a lot,” the Swami said, “you did not have any questions.” The Swami had put me in my place... but again I admired the speed and the sharp edge of his reply.

At the end of lunch one day, I looked over at my friend Harvey, now a regular visitor. His Jewish face along with his dark hair and dark-rimmed glasses gave him a sort of official look, covering the sense of humor he always carried with him, along with a touch of childlike naughtiness: he had even lit up his marijuana pipe on the street but got arrested. As I looked at him, he suddenly stuck the fingers of his right hand into his mouth, one at a time, to lick them clean.

“Harvey,” said the Swami, “go wash your hands.” “I already did,” said Harvey, smiling as he motioned how he had licked his fingers.

“Go wash your hands,” the Swami said sternly. Harvey got up and went into the bathroom.

Touching the mouth, even to eat, was unclean. I had never thought about how often I touched my mouth till one afternoon in the Swami’s office, when every few minutes I saw my right hand go of its own will to my mouth. Each time it happened, I got up, went into the bathroom, and washed my hands.

It worked. I broke the habit.

I came into the temple one afternoon and found Steve sitting alone. Steve was thin and light- haired, a gentle, friendly guy with an Italian last name and an air of innocence and guilelessness that made you wonder whether he was really part of the local scene. He worked for the city, taking care of needy people. I walked up to him, but before I could sit down, he spoke. “Wally, I’m going to quit my job and spend all my time working at the temple. I’m going to go upstairs now and tell the Swami.” “I’ll go with you,” I said, and we chatted as we walked through the courtyard, into the back building, and up the stairs.

“No,” said the Swami. “Do not quit your job. Continue to work and give money to the temple.” As always, the Swami was following the Bhagavad-gita. Working for money, I later learned, is not inferior though it may seem so. It is as good as working in the temple if the money is used for serving Krishna.

One morning, I found Dan sleeping in the storefront. Dan always walked around looking as if he had just been hit over the head and didn’t know where he was going or even what world he was in.

This morning he was sleeping stark naked, lying on the floor on his left side in a semi-fetal position, the wrong end pointing to where the Swami would be sitting in a few minutes to give class. Some of the boys were standing around him. We tried calling out — “Dan! Dan! Get up!” — but he didn’t move.

We had to get some clothes on him. There was no question of anyone’s being naked in front of the Swami. Finally, at the last minute, he got up and dressed himself.

One afternoon Dan came in eating a ham sandwich. Another time he sat down, and a little bottle of marijuana fell out of his pocket. A third time, we found him in the temple sitting with a girl, embracing and petting.

“Dan,” said Keith, “that kind of hanky-panky shouldn’t go on in the temple.” The girl smiled. “I’m a goddess,” she said breathily. I saw a few people smirking at her answer.

“That may be,” said Keith, “but this isn’t your temple.” I liked Keith’s quick reply, but I wondered whether the girl even understood it. Her claim, stupid as it was, raised no one’s eyebrows. Wouldn’t you like to be a Greek god? A buddha? How about God Himself? Just take a little LSD, and you can still be God long after it wears off.

“What about LSD?” asked a young man at an evening program. “Doesn’t that give you spiritual enlightenment?” I knew he was setting a trap. Did he want the Swami to say he had never tried LSD? The man could reply, “Then how do you know it’s not spiritual?” Did he want the Swami to say he had tried it but it was not spiritual? The man could reply, “You say ‘no intoxicants,’ but you take intoxicants.” The Swami looked up from his sitting place. “I myself have never taken these things,” he said, “but my followers have all given them up.” The man didn’t say anything more.

One morning the Swami told us, “Do not ask to see Krishna.” But I had thought this was what we were supposed to do. “Who are you to tell God to come and stand before you?” the Swami continued. “Rather, you should work so that Krishna will see you. If you start work at a big company, you cannot immediately go to see the boss, but if you work very hard, someday the boss will come to see you.” Sometimes the Swami just looked up and smiled. “This Krishna consciousness,” he would say, “it is such a nice thing.” “Accha?” \[ah-CHAH\] said the Swami one afternoon when someone told him about a preaching success. “Accha,” as Keith and Howard told me after their trip to India, means “good” and is often used for surprise or agreement. I heard the Swami say it a few times that summer but not afterward, at least not while speaking English. Still, he always smiled or expressed pleasure when he heard that new people were coming.

But me, as usual, I wanted the wrong thing. I wanted the Swami to take just a few disciples, including me of course, to a mountain cave somewhere and give us the esoteric teachings, the innermost secret, meant just for the chosen few. Now that sounded exciting. “It won’t be so nice with a lot of people,” I said to the Swami one day. Another time, when someone talked about new people coming, the Swami looked over his desk at me with a smile. “But you don’t like that, do you?” he said.

“Keith is very enthusiastic,” said the Swami one day when Keith came and spoke of new people. Soon Keith decided to shave his head and wear robes. “The Swami told me to get seven yards of yellow cloth,” he told Howard and me.

“Seven yards?” I said. “What are you going to do with all that?” “I don’t know,” said Keith.

That evening, when Howard and I went to the temple, we found Keith wearing the cloth like an Indian dhoti. It wasn’t exactly a robe. With the Swami’s help, he first wrapped the cloth around his waist like a bath towel but with the long ends hanging loose in the front. He then pulled one long end between his legs to the back, then upward, and tucked it into the back of the waist, separating the legs like pants. He pulled the other long end all the way around his waist and fastened it in front in a knot.

(The Swami’s followers today pleat this end and tuck it in to make frontal folds.) He also wore a piece around his shoulders, and his head was shaved except for a ponytail.

He looked like a monk, clean and pure, golden and bright, coming out from beneath the grubby hippie veneer. It was nicer than I had expected. But no one, not Keith, nor Stanley and Steve who also started wearing dhotis — no one dared wear anything so exotic on the street until Keith decided to try.

“I’m going to go outside in my robes,” he told me.

“I’ll go with you,” I said. We walked out the door and turned left down to the corner and left again. Keith looked clean and shining, like a light walking down the street. I had thought New Yorkers were used to everything, but as I was about to learn, New York sophistication only went downward to the drab, not upward to the bright. Men whistled at Keith. People stared. We got jeers and catcalls every step of the way. Keith maintained a stoic look, and I could only guess what he must have been feeling. We walked around the block and finally, with a sigh of relief, came back to the shelter of the temple.

That evening I watched the Swami prepare for the temple program. After his shower, he wrapped a saffron-colored cloth around his waist with folds in front but not in back. Then he went into the prasada room and sat on a cushion before the small table-altar with its two pictures: one of his spiritual master and the other of five men with arms upraised. I knew now that the person in the middle was named Lord Chaitanya.

The Swami put a few drops of water in his left palm. With his right hand he picked up a ball of dry yellow clay called tilak, and rubbed it in his moistened left palm to make a soft paste. He picked up some paste on the tip of his right ring finger and pulled the fingertip up the center of his forehead, leaving a yellow U-shaped trail and then ran the fingertip down his nose, leaving a solid point. He repeated the process on his arms and upper torso.

Then the Swami took up his sacred thread, made of several parallel strands, worn over his left shoulder and hanging loose across his torso, wrapped a few turns around his right thumb, and said a prayer under his breath. Then he waved a few sticks of incense in a circle in front of the altar while he said a prayer and rang a bell. “The Swami is saying Bells,” we would say.

When some of us tried putting on tilak, we often used too much water and made a mess. I remember Howard sitting on the floor, looking at me so helplessly I thought he was joking till I saw the yellow water running through his fingers onto the floor. The Swami looked at him. “Do not waste it,” he said.

One day the Swami helped me put on my tilak. I saw him sitting in front of me, looking at me intently, and I felt the soft flesh and firm bone of his finger on my forehead. The Swami always smelled like mustard oil, no trace of an unpleasant mouth or body odor. I could breathe freely.

“Do you think you could wear tilak on the street?” the Swami asked me one afternoon.

“I would feel funny, but I’ll do it if you want.” “No, I am not asking you to do anything you don’t want to do.” But after a while, I noticed something else I did not want to do. Devotees had started bowing whenever the Swami came into the temple. But it was too exotic. Did we really think we could bring the Orient over to New York? Sometimes I imagined the sound of someone striking a large Chinese gong, as in the movies. Howard expressed the same feeling while we talked on Mott Street. “I don’t like all this bowing.” It was dawning on me, and this was the essence of my problem, that Krishna consciousness came bundled with its own lifestyle. One day a disciple declined to sit in a chair while Prabhupada was sitting on the floor. “One should not sit higher than the spiritual master,” the disciple said.

Prabhupada nodded.

It sounded like Oriental culture, but what did one culture or another, sitting higher or sitting lower, have to do with spiritual life? I liked New York and the hippie scene. Hippie life was comfortable. I moved in other circles too, but there was always some need in me that was not fulfilled or a bit of falseness I had to put on.

In the hippie scene, I could relax. I could be a rebel and still fit in. And the Swami had said that we could practice Krishna consciousness and keep our other activities. We just had to change our consciousness. That’s what I had been trying to do with drugs, and I thought I had found a natural way to do it in Krishna consciousness, but now I was being pulled into a world I did not want, a world of old-fashioned morality where I had to worry about things like bowing and sitting higher or lower, where culture dictated everything from what you ate to what you wore and how you wore it.

The idea struck me hard when I was walking down a street one night on an errand for the Swami. A car drove by, and a girl shouted, “Hey hippie!” It felt friendly. It felt right. I had found my place in the world at last, but now I was being dragged out of it. I talked about it with my friend Tony, a black man about ten years older than I who had converted to Islam some years back. “But you’re supposed to be able to do this and do your own thing too,” I said.

“They all say that,” he answered.

Bob also had a problem with it. “This will never succeed. It’s so totally Indian.” I went to see the Swami one day. “I don’t want to become Indian.” “I am not asking you to become Indian.” Another time the Swami mentioned that although Krishna consciousness had come to us from India, we should not think of it as Indian. “When the sun shines in India, the sun does not become Indian.” But in spite of the cultural problem, it would be nice to get what I wanted without drugs, though I had once thought I could never stop. Keith and I had talked about it when we first heard about spiritual realization.

“Why would you want drugs if you attain a state where your thoughts are so ecstatic?” said Keith.

“I can’t imagine never wanting to smoke a little pot.” Keith nodded. We passed the pipe around for another hit.

But getting high on thoughts instead of drugs would be freedom. I would not have to worry about the narcotics police throwing me in jail for my ecstatic thoughts. I would not have to traipse from one apartment to another all night looking for someone with thoughts to sell. Yes, wouldn’t that be freedom! But what did culture have to do with it? Why couldn’t I be a hippie too? Still, I knew that the Swami had what I wanted and all he asked for was to give it to me. It was mine for the taking, even if it didn’t fit right.

One thing I had yet to understand, and it would have pacified me, was that the Vedic culture also emphasized protection of the citizens. It was the duty of the king to see that all his subjects were employed according to their talents and tendencies. A poor working man like my father never had to worry about being fired or laid off or that the man with the key would come to take the water away from his family. The ordinary working people wore jewels and silk, and they ate sumptuously.

Wars were were fought on battlefields, away from the cities and farms, and no young boys like my neighbor Roger were drafted into the army and forced to fight. Nor did the king sit in his palace and send unwilling boys out, never to return home. The wars were fought by members of the military class. Everybody on the battlefield wanted to be there, and the king was at the head of the army, leading the soldiers into the fight.

An outstanding example of a Vedic king was Maharaja Sibi, who offered his own flesh to a hungry eagle in order to save the life of a pigeon. But the pigeon then revealed himself as Agni, the god of fire, and the eagle as Indra, the king of the higher planets in the material world. They had come to test the king. Such was the standard expected of a king in the Vedic culture, but all this knowledge still lay ahead of me.

Soon there was talk going around of an initiation, whatever that meant, and one day Ray walked up to me with pen and paper in hand as I stood on the sidewalk in front of the temple.

“Hey Wally,” he said, “want to get initiated?” “I don’t know,” I said. “I don’t think I’m ready for that.” “None of us are,” he said, “but we’re going to do it anyway.” “Okay. Me too.” Ray put my name on the list. Soon we would become real disciples. But first, Keith got himself in trouble.

## Chapter 8 - The Psycho Ward

If you walked out of the temple and started up Second Avenue, you soon came to Saint Mark’s Place, which wasn’t a place but a street. When you got there, you could stop in at Gem’s Spa, which wasn’t a spa but a store, where you bought magazines and newspapers and refreshed yourself with an egg cream, which contained neither egg nor cream but simply carbonated water and flavoring. If you walked another mile up Second Avenue to Twenty-Sixth Street and then a block east, you came to a large red-brick building called Bellevue, which means “beautiful view” in French, but the view from there was not beautiful unless you looked up into the sky.

Bellevue was the public hospital of Manhattan, famous for its psychiatric observation ward, better known as the psycho ward. Everyone in New York knew about this place. It was where the police took someone who tried to commit suicide or went crazy on drugs, and the doctors there decided whether to put the person in a mental hospital. This was called “getting locked up in Bellevue.” I had heard about the psycho ward like everyone else — a few of my friends had even been locked up once or twice — but it had nothing to do with my life, especially now that I was following the Swami. This, however, was about to change.

I was standing in the doorway of the temple one afternoon, basking in the summer sun, when Keith came out.

“Where are you going?” I asked.

“To Bellevue,” said Keith.

“Bellevue?” “I went to the Welfare Office,” said Keith.

“Welfare?” I asked. (The city’s Welfare Office gave financial aid to the needy.) “I went to the Welfare Office to see if I could get money and give it to the temple,” he replied, “but they said I need a certificate that I’m psychiatrically unable to work. I’m going to Bellevue to see if I can get it.” I looked at him as he walked up Second Avenue, the picture of Uncle Sam’s fair-skinned, fair- haired Protestant boy... almost. There was, of course, the ponytail, but why not? His hand in a beadbag would have been all right, but it was an Indian beadbag that kept the index finger sticking out. It looked strange. And he was murmuring the Hare Krsna mantra. Otherwise, he looked like any other hippie in his tee shirt and blue-jean cutoffs, barefoot but unmindful of the stones and dirt on the sidewalk. All in all, I thought he looked all right.

Keith wanted to give money to the temple, but many hippies cheated the Welfare Office as a kind of sport. Welfare was part of the government, the enemy that put people in jail for getting high.

The hippies also targeted the big corporations, enslavers of the little guy. Worse yet, many corporations were reaping huge profits from the war in Vietnam while the government drafted young men into the army and sent them there to kill and maybe to die. Dying for your country, said the hippies, was one thing; dying for the big corporations was quite another. Not only hippies but young men all over America were burning their draft cards in public, while both men and women were marching and protesting: “Hell No! Won’t Go!” “No More War! No More War!” But quick as the hippies were to decry corporate America and its government, they were just as quick to take advantage of them at every step: free clinics, welfare, food stamps (free food for the needy). Somebody had even found a way to get the coin back after using a public phone. The news spread fast, and though it was just a dime, getting it back was a symbolic victory over the telephone company. And today Keith had his own plan. Cheating Welfare? Probably won’t work. I stood in the doorway, watching him disappear up Second Avenue. Then I went inside and thought no more about it.

A couple of days later, Howard came up to me in the temple. “Have you noticed you haven’t seen Keith around?” he asked.

I hadn’t noticed, but I didn’t want to say so. “Where is he?” I said.

“They locked him up in Bellevue,” said Howard. “I’m going to bring him some prasada. Want to come?” A harried-looking nurse met us at the door of the ward. “Yes?” she said gruffly. I would not have imagined that someone could sound so hostile just by saying “Yes.” “We would like to see Keith Ham.” “Who?” “Keith Ham.” “Have a seat.” We sat down at one of the tables in the visiting room. The tables were rectangular, big enough to seat three people on each side and one at each end. I looked around. It was a dreary place, with drab colors and unhappy voices. The patients (men and women in separate wards) wore hospital pajamas, adding a feeling of sickness to the atmosphere. A few of the faces had that blood-chilling look that insane people get. I had never seen it before, but I knew what it was. Anybody would. Some of the patients were sitting around tables, some were milling about, some were talking with visitors, some were talking with the empty air, some were weeping, some were doing nothing.

In a few minutes, Keith joined us at the table. “You’ve got to get me out of here,” he said. “This place is awful. I can’t stand it. Get me out of here.” I had never seen Keith desperate like this. He was always so poised, so sure of himself. A nurse walked up behind Keith and looked at the ponytail. She frowned. “Hmpf,” she said. “Still there, huh?” Other nurses were scampering about, tapping people on the shoulder, barking orders at patients and visitors. The patients were calm, and the nurses ran around yelling. Made me wonder who the crazy ones were. Then I saw a doctor in a business suit come strutting by, twirling a ring of keys around his finger, acting like Superman.

The next day, Howard and I found Keith’s parents there. Gordon Ham was a Baptist minister and, from what Keith had told me, a powerful preacher. He looked a lot like Keith (or vice-versa) except for his solid build.

I had met them once before, and Mr. Ham’s wife, Marjorie, struck me as a woman of strong character and firm dedication. I knew that she was well liked even by those who rejected her Christian views. Her twang-twang accent reminded me of my childhood in the Midwest.

Keith, the youngest of their five children, had been the perfect Christian boy, their little darling, a preacher in the making till he became a hippie and shattered their dream. But Mr. and Mrs. Ham never gave up on their son, and today they had come to help. They were sitting at a table with Keith and a psychiatrist who worked for the city of New York but independently of Bellevue, a pudgy man with a thin, high voice who kept tapping his fingers nervously on the table. He had come to examine Keith, and his evaluation could carry some weight in securing Keith’s release.

We all greeted one another, and the psychiatrist turned to Keith. “Do you want to get out of here?” he asked.

Keith looked up with a mystical air, his hands raised to his shoulders, palms upward. “Oh, I don’t care,” he said. “Whatever God wants — that’s OK with me.” I caught my breath. What in the world was Keith trying to do? Who was he trying to impress? But it wouldn’t work. That was for sure. The psychiatrist showed no reaction. Then Howard handed him an invitation to the temple.

“Uh oh! Wrong move!” said the psychiatrist, indirectly threatening to lock us up too. “Why are you so insecure that you have to believe in a personal God?” he continued. I wondered why the psychiatrist himself was so insecure and nervous that he couldn’t stop tapping his fingers on the table.

The psychiatrist left, and Mrs. Ham turned to Keith. “Would you like a prayer wheel?” she asked.

“Oh,” I said, “I didn’t know Christians had prayer wheels too.” “She’s joking,” Keith said softly.

I had never seen Keith so subdued, even to the point where one might think he was being respectful. I turned to Keith’s parents. “You know,” I said,” these psychiatrists are all atheists. If you tell them you believe in God, they think you’re crazy.” “Well we know a psychiatrist who believes in God,” said Mrs. Ham. “We’ll send him over.” I took another deep breath. A friend of theirs? He’ll never understand what Keith is doing.

That evening I walked over to the Mott Street apartment, now taken over by my friend Wayne and his wife, Pat. They might have some advice, especially Pat, once a mental patient herself. I told them what had happened and how Keith had said he didn’t care. “That’s the worst thing he could have said,” Pat replied. “If you’re normal, you want to get out.” Howard and I went to Bellevue every day. Mrs. Ham’s psychiatrist friend went once, but without even looking at Keith, he took the word of the staff that Keith needed help.

Howard contacted the writer Allen Ginsberg, who gave him the name of Dr. Edward Harnick of the Einstein Psychiatric Institute, but when Dr. Harnick went to Bellevue to sign Keith out in his care as a psychiatrist, the hospital doctors refused. New York City had a law: If Bellevue released someone who later went insane, the family could sue the city. “We will only sign him out to a relative.” Then Howard called Keith’s sister Roberta, the eldest of his siblings, a liberal, an interior decorator who lived on Twelfth Street, just above Greenwich Village. She had also turned away from Christianity, but unlike Keith, she kept a close relationship with her parents. Everybody liked Roberta. A pleasant, good-natured person, she was close to Keith and always smiled at whatever he did. She would not be shocked by Krishna Consciousness. She would understand. But the doctors told her that even though Keith seemed all right at the moment, he would go incurably insane if he wasn’t treated. So Roberta, our last hope, was afraid to sign him out.

About this time, someone told me what the Swami had said: “Keith says he believes in God, but he goes to them for help. Of course they think he is crazy.” I did not know whether the Swami had really said it, but it made sense. The doctors had not come to the temple to drag Keith out kicking and screaming, nor had they run after him on the street with a big butterfly net. He could have walked past the front door of Bellevue, even gone inside. No one would have bothered him. Keith Ham, of his own free will, walked into an insane asylum, talking about God and asking to be certified crazy. No wonder they locked him up. But why did they keep him? Some of the patients were a danger to themselves or others, but Keith was not.

The misunderstanding was cultural. In America at that time, anyone who spoke of God outside of the standard Christian or Jewish context was seen as crazy, at least a bit cracked. Buddhist temples were all right in Chinatown, of course, but the people there were strange anyway. And the ponytail was shocking because Keith was white. On a brown-skinned foreigner, it would have been an exotic tradition; on an American black, some outlandish fad. But please, not on a white boy. Whites were supposed to be above such things. But whatever the cause, whatever the misunderstanding, Keith was now locked up, and we did not know how we would ever get him out.

My poor friend! I had visions of him sitting for years in some dark, musty institution, forced to hear the rantings of crazy people, be they patients or doctors.

## Chapter 9 - The Initiation

It was the day before the initiation. Morning class had just finished in the storefront. “Now,” said the Swami, “I will tell you what initiation is. Initiation means the spiritual master accepts the student and takes charge of him, and the student accepts the spiritual master and agrees to worship him as God.” The Swami stood up and walked out the side door.

Worship him as God? Did he really say that? I looked around. No one said anything. Everyone looked stunned. The hydrogen bomb had just exploded right in that room. But I knew, and this had been clear from the beginning, that the Swami thought himself God’s servant, not God. And I knew that God’s servant could be worshiped. The question was, Would God be uncompromising like the Swami? True, the Swami represented God by his teaching of the Bhagavad-gita, but did he also represent God by his uncompromising stance? That afternoon, as usual, I went to Bellevue, but for some reason I went alone. I looked at my friend Keith sitting across the table from me. He would understand my problem. We had walked along this mystical road together, with Howard.

“I don’t think I can get initiated,” I said. “The Swami says we have to worship him as God.” “Well,” said Keith, “you’re already doing that by accepting whatever he says.” “Hmm,” I said, “I guess that’s right.” The next evening, sporting a newly grown beard and a bushy head of hair, wearing a tee shirt and blue jeans cut to make shorts, I went to the Swami’s apartment for the initiation. There were eleven of us — nine bachelors plus Mike and his girlfriend, Jan. On the floor of the prasada room was an arrangement for safely building a small fire. Eleven paper plates held barley and other grains, and another eleven each held a string of small yellow plastic beads along with a string of a hundred and eight large red wooden beads for chanting.

“Jan strung the beads,” said the Swami. “Women are good at this kind of work.” I wondered whether the Swami meant that women have more patience than men for work like stringing beads, or whether women should stick to traditional women’s work like stringing beads and sewing, which certainly went against the current trend in society. Then we tied the small yellow beads around one another’s neck. I felt someone tying my beads in a permanent knot.

“How do you get these off?” I said.

“You don’t,” someone answered.

Now I would have to wear yellow beads around my neck, one more intrusion into my life, one more tug into that other world. But I had to go forward with this. We eleven and the Swami sat in a circle around the fire pit. A plate of grains was in front of each of us, and the plates with the big red beads were in front of the Swami. When my turn came, the Swami held up my beads.

“Whose are these?” he said.

“Mine,” I said.

The Swami motioned for me to approach him and then to bow. And I had to bow. I had to go ahead with it, so I pushed my doubts out of my mind as I touched my forehead to the floor and kept it there. The Swami started reciting some Sanskrit, a word at a time. I concentrated all my force on hearing the Swami’s words till there was nothing in the universe but the Swami’s voice and my being.

With each word I gathered more force, feeling happy to recognize a few words like “Bhaktivedanta” and repeating the words as faithfully as I could. Though I did not know what I was saying, I repeated as if my life depended on it. The Swami stopped, and I sat up. I felt cleansed. I had done something good, something right, something I had never done before.

“Your name is Umapati \[oo-MAH-pa-tee\],” the Swami said and handed me my beads. Then I thought he said, “Umapati is the husband of the goddess of fortune.” I must have heard wrong.

Umapati, I later learned, is the god Shiva, husband not of the goddess of fortune but of the goddess of the material nature. I sat down next to Howard, who had not yet had his turn. “Getting your beads is wonderful,” I said in his ear.

One by one, we all got our beads and our new names. Jan became Janaki, Mike became Mukunda, Ray became Rayarama, Howard became Hayagriva, Bob became Ravindra Svarupa (the same name was later given to someone else), Jim became Jagannatha, Stanley became Stradish (stra- DEESH), another Stanley became Satyavrata, Carl became Karalapati, and Janis (a Latvian man living in Montreal) became Janardan.

“Now,” said the Swami, “everyone should go up to the altar and touch his beads to the lotus feet of Lord Chaitanya.” I wondered why we would touch our beads to Lord Chaitanya and not Krishna. After we sat down again, the Swami told us to say one round on our beads, that is, to run the hundred and eight beads one at a time between the right thumb and middle finger, saying the Hare Krishna mantra once as each bead passed. That seemed easy enough. Still, I felt compelled to say a short Hebrew prayer, the Sh’ma, on one bead even though I had not practiced Judaism since high school. The Swami also told us to restring the beads later with a knot between every two beads.

Then the Swami lit the fire and began reciting Sanskrit prayers, word by word, which we tried to repeat. Every so often he stopped and said, “Svaha,” three times, each time spooning some ghee onto the fire and motioning to us to throw in a pinch of grains from our plates. As the smoke filled the room and billowed out the window, I was afraid the neighbors might call the fire department. I imagined the firemen barging in wearing raincoats and hats and carrying axes. I pictured them staring with unbelieving eyes at what they saw: the Swami and his followers sitting around a fire in a religious ceremony from another world. But no firemen came.

After the fire died down, the Swami mixed some of the ash with a bit of ghee to make a paste, and with his fingertip put a large dot of it on everyone’s forehead. Then we left. I did not see anyone stay to help the Swami clean up. I walked out into the summer night and crossed over to First Avenue, thinking about the beauty of the ceremony I had just seen, a beauty I could not define or slip into a category.

I’m not Wally, I kept saying in my mind, I’m Umapati. I’m not Wally, I’m Umapati.

I ran into Louis Abolafia, a casual friend well known in the neighborhood for his streetside office with a big sign over the door: “Louis Abolafia for Governor.” “Hi,” I said.

“Hi,” he said. “You have something on your forehead.” (It was the dot of ash.) “I’m coming from the temple.” “Oh, that’s right,” he said. “It’s Friday night.” “Not the synagogue. I’m coming from the Krishna temple. I’ve — ” My voice choked as a rush of emotion overcame me. I took a quick breath, tensed my body, and pushed the words out — “I’ve just been initiated!” I couldn’t say anything more. I started up First Avenue again.

I’m not Wally, I’m Umapati. I’m not Wally, I’m Umapati.

The warm night and the black sky seemed intoxicating. Every breath felt good. I made my way to Fifth Street and up the stairs to John’s place, where I was staying.

I’m not Wally, I’m Umapati. I’m not Wally, I’m Umapati. I’m not Wally...

## Chapter 10 - Keith Escapes

The initiation took place on a Friday night. A week from Sunday, the Swami would perform the marriage of Mukunda and his girlfriend, Janaki. But I missed the wedding. I was still staying at John’s place, not having heard from him for some weeks, when his girlfriend, Ellie, called to tell me he was in a mental hospital. The police had found him running naked on a beach in New England. Could I go with her to visit him on Saturday? We would take a train to the hospital and stay overnight.

The Swami shrugged at the idea. “What can you do for him?” he asked me. I went anyway. How could I not go see my friend when he was in difficulty? I could preach to him about Krishna, and give him a paperback Bhagavad-gita I had bought for him. But at the hospital, I lost hope as soon as John walked into the room. I felt it physically, like being punched in the chest. My friend John — we used to get high together and joke together and talk about mystical things — had gone insane. His handsome face now had that awful look. He could not hold a normal conversation or even appreciate the trouble Ellie and I had taken to come see him. Once again the Swami had been right. What had I accomplished by going? But Keith was another matter, not insane but still imprisoned in Bellevue. Howard (now Hayagriva) and I were going every day, wondering whom to turn to for help. Then one day Keith was standing next to me in the storefront.

“How did you get out?” I asked.

“I came to get my clothes,” he said. “I told my parents if they signed me out, I would go home and be a good Christian. I’ll stay at home for a couple of days, and then I’ll come back.” He took his things and walked out the door. One minute later he came back.

“What happened?” I asked.

“I told them I couldn’t go with them.” “What did they do?” “They drove off.” I felt sorry for his parents. I could feel their disappointment. But if it were not for the narrow confines of their Christianity they could have been joyful at seeing Keith dedicate his life to God. But I was the joyful one. Krishna had defeated the establishment and brought my friend back home. That night I celebrated the victory by shaving off my beard. The next morning, my smooth face got a big smile from Rayarama and a compliment from the Swami: “Wally looks very nice.” Wally? I was surprised to hear the Swami call me that after he had given me a new name. I wondered about it for a few minutes, then let it go.

Soon there were more initiations. Steve, who worked for the Welfare Department, became Satsvarupa, and Keith became Kirtanananda. We understood the meaning of Keith’s new name — “kirtan” was the chanting of the holy name and “ananda” was bliss — so the name drew a tiny round of applause in the Swami’s office.

More initiations were to come. One evening I started talking with a strongly built young man with close-cropped hair who had come to the temple for the first time. His name was Bruce, he told me, and he was a schoolteacher. When I said something about giving one’s life to religion, his eyes opened wide. “You know,” he said, “I’ve been interested in religion, but I never thought of giving my life to it.” As the temple was closing I invited Bruce to John’s place, where I was staying, and we talked for a few more hours. When he left he mentioned that he lived around the corner, so every morning I walked over and picked him up on the way to class. After just a few days he wrote a letter to his younger brother, Gregory, who had just started college in Colorado, and Gregory sold his typewriter to buy a plane ticket to New York. He moved in with Bruce, and sometimes on my way to class I had to climb the stairs to their apartment and rouse one or the other from bed. In contrast to Bruce, Gregory was thin with shoulder-length hair. My first impression was that he looked like a businessman, and I was right. He was always involved in some project for making money.

Soon Bruce was initiated. His mother, a Jewish woman active at her synagogue, came to the ceremony in the prasada room. I wondered what she thought when the Swami, during his talk, mentioned the four great religions of the world: Hinduism Buddhism, Islam, and Christianity. Then the Swami gave Bruce the name Brahmananda.

Now,” said the Swami, “bow to your mother.” Brahmananda bowed to his mother.

Foreign as the gesture was, I liked it although I could not imagine bowing to my own mother and father. Later I saw a letter from the Swami to Brahmananda and his brother telling them not to think bad of their materialistic parents. “We are trying to raise devotee children,” wrote the Swami, “but your parents have raised two devotee children without even trying.” One evening on St. Mark’s Place, I ran into Charlie, one of the regulars at the temple. He was eighteen, thin and limber with a head full of reddish brown hair. His razor-edged wit and quick sense of humor often sent everyone in the room into fits of laughter. But he had not been coming lately.

“Hey Charlie,” I said, “how come you stopped coming to the temple?” “I’m going to start going again.” “Want to get initiated? Everybody’s doing it.” “Okay.” A short time later, Charlie became Acyutananda, and Gregory became Gargamuni.

But not everybody got initiation. A young man named Dave with an Irish last name and bright red hair started coming to the temple. The Swami seemed to know him. “He is making advancement, don’t you think?” the Swami asked one of the men in an eager tone. Later, I heard that the Swami had stayed for a while in Dave’s apartment on the Bowery (Third Avenue). One day, Dave went crazy on drugs and ran after the Swami with a knife. The Swami ran out the door and escaped.

Now if I had been the victim, I’d want to kick the guy into jail and let him stay there. But the Swami only wanted the best for poor Dave. “He is making advancement, don’t you think?” I had heard about such selfless, magnanimous people and read about them in scriptures, but I had never thought I would meet one. Not only did I see here a quality in the Swami that I would like to imbibe, but I might have to rethink my assessment of the Swami as an unyielding person. After a few days I saw Dave sitting on the floor, laughing wildly all by himself. I never saw him again.

At the end of the class one morning, the Swami turned to Jagannath. “How is the painting coming?” “Almost finished,” said Jagannatha. I wondered what he was painting.

A few days later as I walked into the storefront, Achyutananda called out to me: “Look!” I gasped with delight. “Wow!” It was Jagannatha’s painting, the most beautiful painting I had ever seen — Radha and Krishna, copied from the Srimad Bhagavatam the Swami had brought from India. It was round, about four feet across, standing on the floor, leaning against the wall. As people came in, one after another, we said “Look!” and everyone said, “Wow!” The painting was the first step in brightening the shabby storefront. The time to decorate had come. Kirtanananda, Hayagriva, and I went to Mott Street and took the curtains off the windows to wash them at a self-service laundry. The water in the washing machine turned dark brown as years of tobacco and marijuana smoke were washed away. When the wash was finished, we put in some more coins to start another cycle. Kirtanananda quickly threw in some powdered purple dye, taking care that no one noticed, as you are not supposed to use dye in the self-service washing machines. He jokingly put on an exaggerated look of guilt, and we all giggled like naughty children.

We took the purple curtains to the temple and went back to Mott Street to gather some of the paintings from India along with a hand-held gong and the brass bowls from the two hookahs. Struck with something hard, they rang like bells.

We took it all to the temple, and along with the others we went to work. We made a seat for the Swami at the back, facing the front door. In back of the seat we hung the purple curtains with an orange one in between and Jagannatha’s painting over the orange curtain. We hung up a string of small bells given by my friends Barbara and Larry, the ones who had driven Hayagriva and me to the beach.

Someone else donated a gong about a foot and a half wide. We hung the paintings from India along the walls. Two of them seemed strange — one of a golden-skinned man with six arms and one of an animal with a furry, long-tailed, humanlike body, clothed around the hips, with an upper lip that made me think of a cat. In one hand he seemed to hold a mountain, though I could not tell how a mountain could fit in his hand. The Swami might not like these two paintings, so we placed them near the door, far from where he would sit. People brought a picture of Lord Buddha and one of Saint Joseph holding the infant Jesus, and we hung them up too. Finally the job was done. The temple looked much better, everyone agreed. But nobody had said anything to the Swami.

That evening when the Swami walked in and saw what we had done, the burst of joyous radiance on his face made me think of the Bhagavad-gita: “If thousands of suns rose into the sky at once...” The next day the Swami came to look at the pictures and tell us which ones were good. Most of them were not Krishna conscious, but the Swami agreed to some pictures of musicians playing and people dancing. Then we came to the front of the temple and to the humanlike animal.

“This is very nice,” said the Swami.

“Who is he?” someone asked.

“This is Hanumanji,” said the Swami.

“Is he a cat?” I asked.

“No,” said the Swami, “he is a monkey.” Then I remembered once reading about a species of Indian monkeys known as hanuman monkeys, named after the great monkey hero Hanuman in the scripture Ramayana.

Then we came to the golden man with six arms.

“Oh!” said the Swami. “This is very nice!” “Who is this?” someone asked.

“This is Lord Chaitanya.” “Why does He have six arms?” “Because He showed himself to be both Rama and Krishna.” The Swami pointed to a pair of green arms raised upward, a bow in one hand and an arrow in the other. “These are the arms of Rama.” Then he pointed to a pair of blue arms and hands holding a flute to the Lord’s mouth. “These are the arms of Krishna.” Then we looked at a pair of golden arms with a long stick in the right hand and a waterpot in the left. “What are the other arms?” “Those are the arms of a sannyasi.” I knew what a sannyasi was: a man who had abandoned all worldly attachments including his wife and children to devote himself to spiritual practices and to wander homeless and poor. The Swami himself was a sannyasi.

A day or two later, I came into the temple, and the pictures of Jesus and Buddha were gone.

“What happened to them?” I asked Kirtanananda.

“The Swami said we can have those pictures if we have pictures of all the other avatars too,” he answered.

As the days passed, I began to feel bothered by the Swami’s comment about women and stringing beads. Surely women could do more than that. The Swami had also said that women were generally less intelligent than men. So finally I went to see him.

“The Americans will not accept these things you are saying about women,” I said.

“I am not going to change the truth for the Americans,” said the Swami.

Then I started hearing people say that blacks were less intelligent, though I never heard the Swami say it. Was I joining a movement that was going to spread prejudice around the world? I knew what it was like to be on the receiving end of prejudice, and I was not going to impose it on anyone else. And I knew black people. I had worked in recording studios. I knew black musicians, and I had black friends. I failed to see how they were different from white people except for the color.

My fears were put to rest whan a young black woman with a strong, handsome face started coming. I had seen her in the neighborhood. One day, the Swami passed around some pieces of large, thin wafers to a few of us sitting in his office. “This is called papad,” he said. The way he pronounced papad sounded like popper, a drug used in the neighborhood. The young woman and I looked at each other and shared a smile at the little hippie joke.

Then one afternoon, I walked into the Swami’s office as the Swami and the woman were talking. The conversation was friendly, but the woman did not accept the Swami’s conclusions.

“If what you are saying is right,” she said, “then there is no freedom.” And again: “Then there is no freedom. I may as well kill myself.” A little while later she left. I felt disappointed. She seemed like an interesting person, but she wasn’t going to stay. The Swami turned to the rest of us. “She is very intelligent,” he said.

## Chapter 11 - That’s Where I Want to Go

The Swami looked over at Janaki as she walked up to his lecture seat to talk to him one morning. “That is not a nice way for a woman to dress,” he said.

Janaki’s pants — plain corduroys, not tight or sexy — may have looked all right for the Lower East Side but not for the world the Swami was taking us into. Meditation in Krishna Consciousness was outward as well as inward, seeing the outer world in terms of the inner vision. It made sense.

One could stay focused on the inward while living in the outward, but it demanded a complete surrender of the mind. I would have to accept the Swami’s preference for an old-fashioned society where there was a standard for almost everything including women’s clothes: a long skirt or a sari or a long tunic over pants. I preferred the modern do-your-own-thing society. Nobody cared what you wore.

I caught the value of the Swami’s idea, though, when I saw Janaki in a sari. One day Dr. Mishra — it was at his yoga class that I first saw the Swami — invited the Swami and the disciples to his country ashram. We went the next day. That evening, as the disciples gathered with Dr. Mishra’s followers in the large living room of the main house, Janaki walked in wearing a red sari. She looked elegant. I had never seen a Western woman in a sari before, and I had to admit it looked a lot more feminine than corduroy pants. It made a woman look like a woman.

That evening the Swami told us a true story about a poor young cobbler who worked at his trade at the same spot every day, in the street in front of a big beautiful house. Every day the poor cobbler watched the rich man leave the house in the morning and come back in the evening. “What a lucky man,” the cobbler thought. “He lives in that beautiful house, and he has so much money. Me, I am just a poor cobbler in the street.” Then one day, the cobbler was struck by a thought: “Maybe I could ask that rich man for a rupee. Surely he would not miss one little rupee.” That evening, as the rich man was returning home, the cobbler stopped him. “Oh, sir,” said the cobbler, “could you spare one rupee for this poor young boy.” The rich man’s eyes turned red with anger. “You wretch!” he shouted. “Get away from me!” And he slapped the cobbler. The cobbler began to cry loudly as the rich man continued to yell at him. Soon a crowd gathered, watching the argument.

A wise man came by, and someone told him why they were arguing. The sage looked at the rich man’s palm and the cobbler’s palm. Then he started to laugh. “This poor little cobbler,” he told the crowd, “in his last life he was the rich man’s father. He worked hard to leave all this wealth for his son, and now when he asks his son for a rupee, the son slaps him.” After the gathering I went outside with a few of the others to enjoy the nighttime air. The stars shimmered and twinkled in the clear black sky with the Milky Way running through. As we bathed our eyes in the celestial nectar — a rare treat for a bunch of Manhattan boys — I turned to someone next to me. “But why isn’t the Swami out here enjoying the beautiful sky?” I said. “He must be seeing something even better. But what?” I slept in a dormitory that night and woke up after midnight to find something lying on my chest.

It was a cat. I felt flattered that it had chosen me above the others. Maybe I had this special mystic mellowness that animals could sense. I arose before dawn and went outside to wait for the sunrise. I stood looking east, the cat by my side and the house in back of me. It would be special today, like the stars last night. I had watched the sun come up over the rooftops in New York, San Francisco, and Paris, but today I would see it come up over the mountains. The black sky started turning red. Soon I would see the tip of the sun. Soon... Time went slowly, but I waited. Soon... Any second now...

Then I heard a knocking sound behind me. I turned around. The Swami was knocking on the large window of the house, summoning me to class. So I missed the sunrise.

After class, as we had breakfast with the community, the Swami pushed one of his plates away.

“I cannot eat this,” he said. “It has onion.” Now I saw how the Swami obeyed his own spiritual master. What he wanted from us, he himself gave, and more.

Afterwards, as we were standing around waiting to go back to the city I saw the cat by my side.

I picked it up, cradled it in my arms, and began rubbing its belly. I looked over at the Swami. He smiled. Did the Swami think it was nice that I was petting the cat? That’s what I would have expected. Then I had a second thought. Maybe he was smiling because he knew I wanted him to. I had never seen him pay attention to a cat or a dog, not to mention touching one.

Back in New York one afternoon someone sent me up to the Swami’s office. The Swami was not there but the amplifier had stopped working. I looked it over, but everything seemed in order till I noticed that it needed a new fuse, an expenditure of thirty-five cents, a tiny sum but more than I had. I went downstairs and happened to meet the Swami coming in the building.

“I need thirty-five cents to buy a fuse for your amplifier,” I said.

“The student should not ask the spiritual master for money,” the Swami said.

“But it’s for your amplifier.” “The student should not ask the spiritual master for money.” “But I don’t have any money,” I said, “and it’s for your amplifier.” “The student should not ask the spiritual master for money,” the Swami said.

Finally the Swami gave me the thirty-five cents. But I had been foolish. In that neighborhood I could have easily begged thirty-five cents on the street.

On another occasion though, I managed to give money to the Swami. One afternoon I came out of the temple and saw the Swami standing at a bus stop across Second Avenue, wearing bright tilak on his forehead and a fresh set of clothes. I walked over to him.

“Where are you going?” I asked.

“To Immigration,” he said. “I have to fill out a form and answer some questions.” I wanted to go with him but felt shy about asking. I went back into the storefront. A few days later, just before the evening program, Rayarama called us together. “All the devotees should stay after the program,” he said. “We have something to talk about.” After the program, the Swami spoke to us. “Immigration has refused to extend my visa,” he said. “I do not know why. I have answered all of their questions. Mr. Allen Ginsberg knows a lawyer who can help, but it will cost five hundred dollars.” We calculated. Thirty-five dollars from each of us would cover the bill. We all agreed immediately.

“I am very moved,” said the Swami, “that you all want me to stay so much that you will do this for me.” How could the Swami be surprised? Thirty-five dollars was the least we could offer after what he was doing for us. How could he be so humble, and genuinely so? The next day I called Tony, the friend who had converted to Islam. He was one of the first friends I had made in New York, when we worked together as recording engineers. I could always count on Tony.

“We had the same problem with the imam at the mosque,” he told me. “I’ll give you the money.” We raised the money, and the Swami stayed.

I began to understand. The Swami might be unyielding in matters of philosophy, but he was humble, and he was flexible and considerate in dealing with people. I saw it again one morning after class when we went up the stairs with the Swami but we could not open the door to his apartment.

The lock was jammed. We all went back downstairs to the courtyard and sat around a table. Stradish \[stra-DEESH\] and I walked around the block to get a locksmith on First Avenue, but he could not come till after an hour. It was a pleasant summer morning with a hint of a breeze and a blue sky over the cement and bricks of the courtyard. I was happy to sit there with the others, talking with the Swami.

Suddenly Stradish got up, and I followed him upstairs. He knelt down in front of the Swami’s door, took a screwdriver out of his pocket, and tried to open the lock with it. “Stradish,” I said, “you’ll never open the lock with a screwdriver.” No sooner had I said it than the lock opened. We called the others. The morning was getting on, so we decided to go without breakfast and attend to our duties. We would meet again at lunchtime.

Then someone came in. “Bob is sitting on the stairs,” he said, “and he won’t work unless he gets breakfast.” The Swami turned to Kirtanananda. “Make him some breakfast,” he said.

One day we were invited again to Dr. Mishra’s ashram. I was chosen, I don’t know by whom, to stay and guard the storefront and the apartment. Well, it sounded like fun. And I might even discover something interesting. As the others were leaving, Mukunda and Janaki walked into the storefront carrying a cage with two cats. “These are our cats,” Janaki told me. “Can you feed them while we’re gone.” She put the cage, along with some cat food, on the floor next to the Swami’s elevated lecture seat. Then she and Mukunda left with the others.

It was indeed fun being alone. During the afternoon, I happened to go behind the Swami’s seat in the storefront, and I saw three pieces of crumpled paper on the floor. I opened them. It was a picture of Lord Narayan, Krishna’s four-handed form, a page from the calendar that Kirtanananda and Hayagriva had brought from India. I smoothed out the pieces and taped them together. It felt good to see the picture again, like finding an old friend. I took it upstairs and taped it on the wall in the Swami’s office.

Then I picked up a Bhagavad-gita and took it into the prasada room. It was one of the two Gitas the Swami used in class, but he only read the verses, which he said were translated well. The commentaries were useless. As I was leafing through it, I stopped at one commentary: “Now Krishna is telling Arjuna to look inside to find God.” “Look inside to find God.” I had read this and heard it many times, but I could only look outwards. How could I turn my eyes around to look inside? What did it really mean? Then I noticed some writing in the margin, not the Swami’s handwriting, but only the Swami could have told this to whoever wrote it. “God is standing in front of Arjuna,” it said, “and the foolish commentator wants Arjuna to turn around and look inside.” Right! Wasn’t this what the Swami had been driving at? Krishna is God, so if you are already seeing Krishna, why turn around and look inside? I felt relieved. I would not have to learn how to look inside. More important, I knew the Swami had just taught me something that would change my life: Don’t worry about inside or outside. Just look at Krishna wherever you see him. It was exactly what the Bhagavad-gita was saying, yet I had never heard or seen it said so clearly before. What luck! I was glad I had stayed behind.

When everybody came back the next afternoon, a few of us gathered in the Swami’s office. We were looking at the salvaged picture, and the Swami was smiling broadly. I told how I had found it.

“When I put it together,” I said, “I felt as if a light had gone on.” The Swami smiled again.

The next morning, when the Swami sat down in his seat in the storefront, he looked over and saw the cage.

“What is this?” he asked.

“Mike and Jan left their cats here so I could feed them,” I said.

“This is not a home for cats and dogs,” said the Swami.

When Janaki and Mukunda came in, I told them what the Swami had said. “Now I’m afraid to go upstairs and see the Swami,” Janaki said. But I never heard any more about it.

When I left the storefront that morning I stopped for a minute, as I often did, and looked in the display window at two pictures the Swami had brought from India: Lord Chaitanya and His devotees dancing; and little boy Krishna sitting on Mother Yasoda’s lap as she smiles and feeds him.

Next to them was a set of books with a name I could not pronounce: Srimad Bhagavatam, an ancient scripture in three volumes, translated and explained by the Swami. Printed in India, the books had a mysterious, oldish look about them. What secrets might they hold? But I had no money to buy them.

Hayagriva had bought a set, though, and on Mott Street one day, he and I were looking at the dust cover, a painting of the spiritual sky. We could understand a little. Krishna’s planet was the center, shaped like a lotus flower, with some people dancing and some sitting. In the rays of light coming out from Krishna’s planet were many other planets, smaller and represented by circles, or bubbles, with pictures inside. One bubble in particular caught our eye. We could not make out what was happening inside, but it looked mystical.

“Hey,” I said, “I like this one.” “Me too,” said Hayagriva.

“Far out, man!” “That’s where I want to go.” “Yeah. That’s the place for me.” We sat looking at the mystical bubble for some minutes. Then I stood up.

“I have some things to do,” I said.

“I’m going to the temple,” said Hayagriva.

“Ask the Swami what that bubble is,” I said.

I left, and when I came back, Hayagriva was there. “Did you ask the Swami what that bubble is?” I said.

“Yeah.” “What did he say?” “He said it’s the material world.”

## Chapter 12 - Srimati Radharani Is So Kind

One day I walked with the Swami and a few others around the Lower East Side looking in store windows at mimeographs, a low-cost printing machine popular with small organizations. We were going publish a magazine. The Swami, I learned, had started the magazine, Back to Godhead, in India in 1944 to fulfill Lord Chaitanya’s prophecy: The Hare Krishna mantra would spread to every town and village in the world. Now we disciples would take up the mission. Our magazine would be a small start, and a mimeograph would be just the thing, but even the cheapest were beyond our means.

As we walked that day I became aware of a certain humility in the Swami, like the air of innocence he carried, and again, it was a quality apart from any special thing he said or did. It was a quiet, soft humility, always present and inseparable from the Swami himself. He might defeat me in an argument or even show anger, but I always felt this humility in him. He only wanted me to understand what he was teaching.

Free as he was from the illusory energy, the Swami showed the eternal qualities of the soul, though I did not understand this at the time. The qualities that we fallen beings show are acquired through contact with matter, like the self-conscious humility that I would put on to impress people, but the qualities of the soul are a part of the soul himself just as Krishna’s qualities are a part of Krishna.

The Swami’s other qualities, like his peacefulness, seemed related to his humility. It seemed obvious — and contagious. I felt peaceful just by sensing the Swami’s peacefulness.

Over the years, the Hare Krishna movement grew, and the Swami became a world traveler worshiped by people everywhere and served like a king with the best food and quarters and cars. Yet he never changed. I always saw the same humble, gentle Swami that I chatted with and strolled with along the Manhattan streets from corner to corner, from store window to store window that day.

A few days later I went to the temple and found two hand-cranked mimeographs. “Where did we get these?” I asked Kirtanananda, but he wouldn’t tell me. It was some kind of secret. I felt a flash of heat, annoyed at being treated like an outsider, and by someone who was supposed to be my good friend. We were all in this together, weren’t we? And what was the big secret anyway? I thought about what to do for a moment, whether to tell him what I thought or to let it pass. I finally decided to drop the subject so we could start on the magazine. Hayagriva was already upstairs typing stencils for the mimeographs.

A newly initiated young man of eighteen was hard at work cranking out the pages on one machine, and he showed me how to work the other one. Soon the storefront was swinging, and devotees stacked the pages on wooden benches to be assembled into magazines. Back to Godhead had come to New York.

The Swami had some free tickets for a performance that night at Town Hall (not really a town hall but a concert hall). Burton Greene, a young pianist who sometimes visited the temple, would be playing. A few others and I left the storefront with the Swami for the concert, while the rest stayed back to assemble the magazines. As we set out, a young devotee brandishing a stapler called to us.

“You’re going out for sense gratification,” he said, “but we’re here serving Krishna.” I had to smile at his childlike innocence. Didn’t he see the Swami with us? As Burton played, he sometimes reached inside the piano and plucked the strings.

Coincidentally, a few days later, some of us found the stringboard of a piano, with strings and all, discarded on a sidewalk. “Hey!” someone said. “This will be great for Burton Greene!” The stringboard was so heavy we four young men could barely lift it off the ground, but someone got a dolly, so we wheeled the stringboard over to the temple and set it inside near the street door, next to a string bass someone had put there. One more instrument was always welcome. Someone could play it at the programs on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday nights along with the string bass, the gongs, the string of bells, and the brass bowls from the hookahs while everyone sang and danced and jumped and shouted.

The storefront would be packed, the street door open to let in the summer air. Sometimes a wino from the neighborhood staggered in, drunk, filthy, and stinking, making us laugh as he fell all over himself trying to dance. At end of every program the Swami picked up an apple that had been by his side and cut it into slices to be passed around. Someone else came by with a bowl for donations.

“That’s what I don’t like about these religions,” muttered a young man one night, but people gave what they wanted. There was no pressure.

The preaching and publicity were going strong, but we also had to work on another side of Krishna Consciousness: our own purification. One morning after class, the Swami gave us a new instruction for using our beads. “You should all chant sixty-four rounds a day,” he said. I gasped.

Sixty-four rounds? Chanting just one round meant saying the Hare Krishna mantra a hundred and eight times, once for every bead on the string. It takes most people about seven minutes although some can go faster, so sixty-four rounds would take about seven and a half hours.

“What!” someone said. “Sixty-four rounds?” “We can’t!” said another.

“Impossible!” “Can’t do it!” “No! No!” “All right,” said the Swami, “Thirty-two rounds.” “No!” “We can’t!” “Impossible!” “No! No!” “All right,” said the Swami, “Sixteen rounds. No less.” And sixteen rounds became the standard for the Hare Krishna movement.

“Tomorrow is Krishna’s birthday,” the Swami said one morning. “We will fast until midnight.” The next morning we started our fast. Janaki, however, had another project. “Swami,” she said after the morning class, “I have to go now. I have to take our cats to the doctor. They’re sick.” Having once had my own cat, not the cat living on Mott Street but a jet-black female named Blue, I understood Janaki’s concern. I had also felt an urgent need to take care of the cat when she got sick.

“Stay here,” the Swami told Janaki. “Do not leave.” “But I have to take them to the doctor. They’re sick.” “Stay here.” “But they’re sick.” “Stay here.” Janaki stayed.

The morning went fine, but by afternoon, some people were lying on the floor, exhausted from the fasting. I looked at my watch. It was 3:00 PM. Only three o’clock? Oh, would this day of torture never end? My whole life passed before my eyes a few times. I tried to think about different things to get my mind off the hunger. Nothing worked. I lay down. I sat up again. The seconds dragged on into hours. I looked at my watch again. Only twelve minutes had passed.

Finally, finally, after a day of suffering and agony, midnight came. We raced up the stairs to the Swami’s apartment and sat down in the prasada room. Now we were going to get our feast. I rubbed my palms together in anticipation. My mouth started watering. Yum yum! Here it comes! Then the Swami came in carrying a platter of sliced fruit. He gave everyone a few slices and walked out. I waited for him to come back with more... and I waited... and I waited...

Was this all I was going to get? I’d been fasting all day, dying of hunger, and all I got was a few slices of fruit? I looked around. The others were all staring hopelessly at their almost-empty plates.

Then I ate my fruit, and I felt full and satisfied. I looked around again. Everyone was smiling. It was a miracle. We had had our feast.

As the days went on, I began to wonder about another side of self-purification: What about my sexual future? I knew there would be no sex outside of marriage, but what about married life? Some others had the same thought. “What are the rules for sex in marriage?” someone asked in an evening class.

“I will tell you some other time,” said the Swami.

“We want to know now,” said someone else.

“All right,” said the Swami. “Not more than once a month and on the woman’s fertile day. No contraception and not less than six months after childbirth.” I looked around. Everyone seemed as shocked as I was. “Once a month!” someone said. “That’s worse than not at all.” “There was a king,” said the Swami. “This king said that now that he knows Krishna, whenever he thinks of sex, he turns his head in disgust and wants to spit.” “How old was that king?” shouted Mr. Gold, a young lawyer who had been coming regularly. I never saw Mr. Gold again.

Two weeks after Krishna’s birthday, another special day arrived: the appearance day of Krishna’s consort, Radha, also known as Radharani (Radha-rani). “Today is Srimati Radharani’s Appearance Day,” the Swami said in the morning, “but we will only fast until noon because Srimati Radharani is so kind She does not want us to go hungry.” I felt grateful to Srimati Radharani.

One morning the Swami told two little stories about the value of Indian culture even in the modern world. The first was about a snake that escaped from a science laboratory. The scientists had to find a snake charmer to get the snake to come back.

The second was about some Englishmen driving on a road through a forest when they found the road blocked by a mother elephant standing with her baby. Nearby, the Englishmen saw a sage who lived naked in the forest, and they asked him for help. The sage walked up to the mother elephant, his palms joined in a gesture of respect. “My dear mother,” he said, “these gentlemen would like to pass.

Could you please take your baby and move off the road.” The elephant took her baby, and they moved off the road.

I liked that story. Not only did it indirectly show the value of what I was now studying, but also it showed respect toward all forms of life. And if the Swami was telling the story, he must have agreed that even an elephant could be treated with respect.

Summer had moved on into early fall, but the weather was still warm. Soon we had a permit from the city to hold kirtan, to chant and dance the following Sunday in nearby Tompkins Square Park.

Tompkins Square was the only green space on the Lower East Side. Three square blocks of grass with trees, benches, and pathways, it was an ideal place to spend an afternoon in good weather, especially if you were high. It was a favorite strolling place not only for the hippies but also for the local Poles, Ukrainians, and Puerto Ricans. Small though it was, Tompkins Square offered a bit of nature, an escape from the fury of the big city. On Thursday before our Sunday kirtan, some hippies held a rally there to protest a new anti-LSD law in California. A few of us went and joined the gathering, and we chanted until evening.

Then on Sunday, we all went there with the Swami. Our little procession, with the Swami and some of his followers in robes, carrying musical instruments and a painting of Lord Chaitanya with His chanting party, walked up second Avenue and then turned right, continuing past First Avenue to Avenue A. We entered Tompkins Square and sat down on a plot of grass. (There were no fences then.) The Swami led the chanting while the rest of us played instruments or danced. The Swami called me over. “Hold up the painting of Lord Chaitanya and dance with it,” he said. What an honor the Swami had given me! I held up the painting and started to dance. Lord Chaitanya’s chanting party had come across the world to New York. I did not see the importance of the moment, though, and I soon put the painting down.

The Swami called me over. “Why did you put the painting down?” “I got tired,” I said.

The Swami did not say anything more.

Of all the stupid things I have done in my life, and there have been a good few, this one gets a special prize. Oh Lord! I wish I could bring that moment back and dance with the painting to the Swami’s satisfaction. What wouldn’t I give! The chanting ended, and the Swami gave a short talk. Then we went back to the storefront, not knowing that a reporter from a local hippie newspaper, The East Village Other, had been in the park.

The next edition of the Other carried an article titled “Save Earth Now!” with two photos — one of the Swami sitting and laughing and one of the Swami standing under a tree with onlookers on both sides.

You can still see these photos and the article if you go to the ISKCON temple in Mayapur, India. Just look on the other side of the wall in back of the Swami’s sitting place.

The chanting became a regular Sunday feature at the park. Soon Allen Ginsberg and his companion, Peter Orlovsky, were chanting with us every Sunday. They had been to India, and Ginsberg also sang the Hare Krishna mantra on television appearances. One time, Brahmananda and Acyutananda stood up and danced, as graceful as two angels. The next day their picture appeared in the New York Times. “Two youths are stirred to dance...” began the caption. The article also had a statement by Hayagriva: “‘I myself took fifty doses of LSD,’ said Howard Wheeler, ‘and now, nothing.’” Hayagriva gasped when he saw it. “Oh, my God! They put in the New York Times that I took LSD! Now I know how it feels to wake up and find yourself exposed in the papers.” Around this time, Mukunda and Janaki left for India along with a young man named Maurice and Janaki’s sister Joan, who had come from Portland for Janaki and Mukunda’s wedding.

(Joan was later initiated as Yamuna Devi Dasi. Her cookbook, Lord Krishna’s Cuisine: The Art of Indian Vegetarian Cooking, written some years later, received the Cookbook of the Year award from the International Association of Culinary Professionals.) They would first go to the West Coast and from there to India.

“I went to say goodbye to the Swami,” Mukunda told me, “and the last thing he said as I walked out the door was, ‘Try to open a center on the West Coast.’” Soon we got word that they had opened a temple in San Francisco. Krishna Consciousness had now gone to California, another step toward every town and village.

## Chapter 13 - We’ve Lost Somebody

Everyone should have been happy. Our little group was growing, and the public chanting was getting good press, but the devotees were arguing over the smallest things, everyone trying to show his superiority. I had already gone through this. In our LSD days, Kirtanananda and I sometimes argued about who was the greater mystic. It was stupid. I didn’t want to live through it again, so I went to see the Swami. “The devotees are fighting all the time and doing so much nonsense.” “If you don’t like what the others are doing,” said the Swami, “then you set the example.” It wasn’t the answer I had hoped for, but it made sense. Still, I went another time to complain.

“What do you want from them?” said the Swami. “They are trying to serve Krishna.” I resigned myself to living with it. What could the Swami do anyway? But Stanley, now named Satyavrata, never argued with anyone. He was a bit short with dark neatly cut hair, dark-rimmed glasses, and a serious expression, and though he did not talk much, when he did say something it was always intelligent. His clothes looked new and well-maintained, so I assumed he had a job. I liked his mature, sober demeanor, and though I had never spoken with him, I thought he would be an interesting person to know. When we finally had a conversation though, it was not in the way I expected.

Another person who never argued was the other Stanley, now named Stradish \[stra-DEESH\]. A gentle young man about nineteen, he was always peaceful. One day he and I were standing in the temple talking about why we had come to Krishna consciousness.

“I was looking for a way to stay high,” I said.

“I came because I was distressed,” said Stradish.

Distressed? How could someone who seemed so serene be motivated by distress? But Stradish had will power. One day he told me he had brought his sleep down to just a couple of hours a night.

“How can you do it?” I asked.

“When you do it,” he said, “you begin to crave being awake the way you now crave sleep.” One day, as I rode in a car with Stradish, we started singing Hare Krishna, so I reached into my pocket and pulled out my new set of finger cymbals, tiny cymbals you strap onto your fingertips to play those great ching-ching-a-ling rhythms the way belly dancers and Gypsy musicians do. When I would ching them at the start of kirtan in the temple, it seemed to bring a smile to the Swami’s face. I started playing them in Stradish’s ear as we sang. The crisp ringing made my gentle friend smile.

The next morning, as we chanted in the temple, I sat next to Stradish and again played the finger cymbals in his ear. He leaned over to listen. Then I looked up and saw the Swami motion to me not to do that. I understood. Stradish should hear the holy name, not the finger cymbals, so I moved my hands away. But Stradish had not seen the Swami. He kept leaning farther and farther toward me to hear the cymbals, leaning so far I was almost afraid he would fall over, but I had to obey the Swami.

In the Swami’s office one afternoon, someone put on a tape recording of devotees chanting.

“Stradish,” said the Swami, “why don’t you dance?” Stradish got up and started doing a pop-music dance.

“Stradish,” said the Swami, “that is not how I taught you to dance. Why don’t you dance the way I taught you?” “I don’t remember how, Swami,” said Stradish.

The Swami stood up and started to dance. He held his arms up and put one foot in front of the other and pulled it back. Then he put the other foot in front and pulled it back. In this way — one foot and then the other — the Swami danced. We all stood up and followed the Swami, dancing as we moved forward in a circle, one behind the other.

But the neighbors didn’t dance to the sweet sound of our chanting, especially at seven in the morning when they wanted to sleep. We tried our best to keep it soft. It didn’t help. Grumpy as bears awakened in winter, they pounded on the walls or opened the side door to the temple and stood there frowning, their hands on their hips.

As the morning moved on, we started our work. One gray, sunless day the Swami and I were together in his office, each absorbed in his own work when the clouds parted, instantly flooding the room with sunlight. The Swami looked up. “When the spiritual sun comes out,” he said, “it is like that.” Sudden enlightenment. It was the promise of sudden enlightenment in the Buddhist scriptures that had started me on my religious search. Now the Swami was confirming it. What I had yet to learn, and the Bhagavad-gita stresses this point, was that the highest enlightenment, the only one worth working for, is about devotion to Krishna.

“What about liberation?” I asked the Swami one day in his office.

“Do you want a million dollars?” asked the Swami.

“No.” “Do you want a woman?” “No.” “Then what do you want to be liberated from?” Another time in the Swami’s office, as I started typing a list of people’s initiated names, the Swami told me that a devotee’s name should be followed by the word prabhu (“master”), so I mentioned this principle in the list. Then the Swami told me to leave some space at the bottom of the page when I came to the end.

Finally, I looked up. “I have finished,” I said.

“Did you leave some space at the end?” the Swami asked.

“Yes.” “Then type this,” the Swami said and began dictating: Namah Om Vishnupadaya /Krishna presthaya bhutale /Srimate Bhaktivedanta /Swamin iti namine. “I offer my respectful obeisances unto Bhaktivedanta Swami, who is very dear to Lord Krishna on this earth, having taken shelter of His lotus feet.” Though I knew it was the duty of the disciple to offer respects to the spiritual master, it was beyond my grasp how someone could dictate a prayer offering respects to himself. But I knew the Swami had no need for it. He had never shown any sign of pride. Nor did he seem embarrassed. He was as casual as if he were saying, “A pound of carrots, two pounds of potatoes...” It was something we had to learn, and if the Swami didn’t teach us, who would? But did I really want to learn it? Could I ever surrender to somebody? I had tried to avoid facing the idea, but now with the arrival of this prayer I would have to deal with it. I would have to replace my own views and thoughts with someone else’s. The Swami had said as much in class: The disciple’s mind should be like a blank slate the spiritual master can write on.

But wouldn’t that be a kind of death? Was I not the aggregate of the thoughts and opinions and experiences I had built up over the years? Could I replace my own self, whom I held so dear, with a self that someone else gave me? But I had to go on. The Swami had something I wanted, though I wasn’t sure what. I would have to let my doubts hang suspended and hope that some day everything would become clear. Now that I knew this prayer, though, I discovered that every morning before class when the Swami led the chanting, he started by singing this prayer of obeisances to his own spiritual master. If surrendering was such a bad thing, how could the Swami do it and be so comfortable about it? If I looked at things from the Swami’s point of view, everything he said and taught was logical, and perfectly so. I had never come across any system of thought that had such airtight reasoning. And wouldn’t I love to speak with undefeatable logic! Nor did we have to go into deep meditation. The Swami could show us Krishna consciousness in the everyday world. One day he was telling a few of us in his office that women, by their nature, want to be enjoyed by men. “Just like you see on the bus,” he said, as he stuck out his lips and mimed a girl putting on lipstick, a common sight on the buses in New York. “She is saying, ‘Please enjoy me, please enjoy me.’” Some people may take exception to the Swami’s statement about women as there are wide variations in human sexuality, but the classical roles of men and women are often used as an analogy for another conception of male and female where the principle always holds. Krishna is the supreme male, the supreme enjoyer. All others, regardless of bodily gender, are His females, objects of His enjoyment. It took me years to understand that through all these lifetimes, I had gotten it backwards.

Wasn’t I myself the supreme enjoyer? Didn’t this world and all its inhabitants exist for my pleasure? And I was not alone. Every living being in this world, down to the smallest germ, thinks he is God, the supreme enjoyer, though he may not say it in those words.

Then I took up Krishna consciousness. Now I thought the instrument of my enjoyment was Krishna: “Krishna, make me high. Krishna, give me ecstasy.” The same goes for these so-called inspirational preachers who tell me to ask God for money or success, things that are already available in the material world.

But if I give pleasure to Krishna — in religious ways like worshiping Him, chanting His name, preaching His word — then I, the object of enjoyment, will also taste pleasure. The servant of the king lives in the same palace as the king. And here was something new: The pleasure felt by Krishna’s devotee is so great that even Krishna wants to taste it. Lord Chaitanya, standing in the picture on the Swami’s little table-altar, is Krishna Himself, coming into this world to enjoy the life of a devotee.

The Swami once said in a class that a man is considered learned if he speaks in a way that no one can understand. We laughed as he started speaking gibberish and then said, “Like that.” But the Swami himself could explain philosophy, from the highest principles to the deepest, in simple terms, like lipstick. And he even used humor. His mime of a girl putting on lipstick only lasted a second or two, but it was funny, a delightful caricature.

Humor came up again one day when a few of us sitting in the office with the Swami were joking about setting up a Krishna-conscious factory. The noon lunch whistle would be a conch shell. As the Swami chuckled along with the rest of us, I thought about his smile. Though I never noticed any smell from the Swami’s mouth, his smile gave me the same feeling as the clean warm fragrance of mustard oil that pervaded the apartment. And humor came up again with the subject of drugs. Most of the Swami’s followers had used drugs, and no matter how pleasurable the high, how intense the visions, it always ended with the disappointment of coming down, back to normal consciousness.

“You know,” someone said, “with Krishna consciousness you just keep getting higher and higher, and you don’t come down.” “Yes,” said the Swami.

“No more coming down!” someone exclaimed.

The rest of us started to clap and laugh. “No more coming down! No more coming down!” The Swami was smiling.

Later that day on Mott Street, Hayagriva and I composed a leaflet. “Stay high forever,” it began.

“No more coming down with Krishna Consciousness.” We went to the temple and mimeographed copies to hand out. Some devotees thought it catered to the hippie mentality, but the Swami approved of it.

Yet with all of the Swami’s knowledge of the material world, he could still be surprised at learning something new. One time someone mentioned a Mr. Moscowitz.

“Moscowitz,” I said. “That’s a Jewish name.” “Oh?” said the Swami. “You too can tell by the names.” One afternoon, the Swami gave me special protection. I was sitting with a few others in the prasada room, chatting about this and that, when I glanced through the open window between the rooms and saw the Swami in his office, at work on his manuscripts. I wondered whether he heard us wasting our time in idle talk.

In the middle of our conversation, a young man whom I did not know came into the room and began telling us the latest news about Millbrook, a town in upstate New York where Timothy Leary, the self-proclaimed LSD guru, had moved into an estate. I was curious.

“A friend of mine just came back from Millbrook,” the young man said, “and he told me they had a terrific orgy there.” Now I was all ears. Wow! Tell me about the orgy! Suddenly the Swami looked through the window. “Umapati,” he said, “you are not sitting properly.” I was sitting with my back toward the little altar, so I turned around, my back to the conversation. And try as I might, and I sure did try, I could not make out what was being said. My ears were blocked. The Swami had protected me.

A few days later I was sitting in the storefront typing when I decided to take a little break and stand up for a few minutes. I looked out the window and listened to the traffic noise and the people talking on Second Avenue. I thought about the Swami’s having protected me. But how much could the Swami protect us if we didn’t want it? Just then Kirtanananda came in and walked up to me. “We’ve lost somebody. Satyavrata has left.” Satyavrata, the quiet guy with the glasses. Though I hardly knew him, I felt that I myself had lost someone. I had thought nobody would go away from Krishna consciousness. We were all in this together searching for the absolute truth, weren’t we? How could someone go away? I thought about it for a few minutes. Then I sat down and went back to my typing. I could not have imagined that I would be the next to leave.

## Chapter 14 - Leaving

One day, the Swami said something that bothered me: “Family members should live like strangers in the same hotel.” It didn’t bother anyone else, but I began to wonder whether I was doing the right thing. Did I want to give my life to a movement against family love? I thought about my own childhood. Live like strangers? How lonely I would have felt! Could I take away from other children what I myself had? Many of the hippies and other marginalized young people had felt unloved as children, the most vocal about it being children of Jewish immigrants. If their parents had indeed loved them, they had shown it in negative ways like endlessly haranguing the children or throwing in that famous Jewish guilt: “So how do you expect me to feel? Did you ever think of that? Your poor mother?” There were the jokes and the stories and the groans about the horrors of being forced to overeat. Many Jewish mothers, terrified at the thought that their healthy, active, rosy-cheeked children might not not be eating enough, resorted to desperate measures. “I used to force the food down Joey’s throat,” a friend’s mother told me. “I knew he would throw up, but at least he would retain some of it.” And the Gentile children had their issues too. “Goddamn you! Goddamn you!” Tommy O’Brian’s sister was screaming at her father. The lava that had been boiling inside for years had finally exploded — the childhood spankings, the unfair treatment, the favoring of one child over another. Poor Mr. O’Brian. He meant well. He had simply tried to raise the children the way he had been raised in rural Ireland. But it didn’t work in America.

Now these children were grown up, and they wanted to give their own children the love and understanding they themselves had missed. How was I going to tell them to treat their children like strangers? But surely I had misunderstood something. Later I would read in the Swami’s books about affection between family members and in his letters about children’s need for love.

Maybe I needed to step back a bit, or so I thought, become more independent of the temple and take a fresh look at what the Swami was teaching. The first thing to do was get a job.

I walked into the Swami’s office. The Swami was sitting behind his floor-level desk talking with a few people. I offered obeisances, getting down on all fours and touching my head to the floor, softly reciting the mantra of obeisances the Swami had dictated to me: “Nama Om Vishnupadaya...” Offering obeisances was now the normal procedure whenever one saw the Swami, in his office or anywhere else. I waited till the Swami paused in his conversation.

“Swami,” I said. The Swami turned to me. “I think I should get a job,” I said.

“Why?” “I have to live.” “What are you doing here? You are dying?” “I have to support myself.” “That is your illusion, that you think you have to support yourself.” Live without supporting myself? Inconceivable! Maybe I could coast for a while, but how could I live any length of time without making money? So I took a job as a messenger, delivering documents from one office to another in midtown Manhattan. The weather was still warm, so I did not mind walking around all day. Only one problem: the cheap ill-fitting cloth shoes I had bought to avoid leather were hurting my feet, but I could put up with it. And it was fun being outside all day. I called the Swami from an outdoor telephone booth one afternoon. “I haven’t been coming because I’m working.” “You have Krishna work!” the Swami shouted into the telephone. “You should do your Krishna work!” Then late one afternoon, walking up Broadway, I ran into Satyavrata. We greeted each other.

“I heard you left,” I said.

“Yes.” “I’m thinking of leaving too,” I said.

“You are?” “Yeah,” I said. “I don’t know. I mean, I was just looking for a way to stay high all the time.” “If you just want to stay high,” he said, “there’s an easier way to do it.” So we went to his apartment and did it the easier way. (Some time later Satyavrata told me that if I hadn’t said I was leaving, he would not have offered me the “easier way.”) But now I had to decide. I could not live in both worlds. I had to tell the Swami. I went to his office, offered obeisances, and sat down. The Swami turned to me.

“I’m leaving,” I said.

“Why?” “Because you said that family members should live like strangers in the same hotel.” The Swami opened the Bhagavad-gita to a verse about detachment and showed it to me.

“This won’t do,” I said. “I’m leaving.” The Swami shrugged. “Then what can I do?” he said in resignation.

After work one day, I tugged hard on my neck beads to break the string and threw the beads away. I moved back to the Mott Street apartment, now the home of Harvey, the young man the Swami had scolded for putting his fingers into his mouth. Then I found a job as a recording engineer, a chance to succeed in a profession I had abandoned. On my way to work the first day, I bought a pair of comfortable high-ankled soft-leather shoes, a popular style called desert boots. I found a job for Satyavrata where I worked, and sometimes he and I went to the storeroom in the basement and smoked marijuana. And getting high at recording sessions was now a normal thing.

“What’s that smell?” a visitor asked the boss in the hallway.

“It’s that stuff they smoke,” the boss answered.

Gradually, I began breaking the other rules. I did not want to start smoking cigarettes again, but people offered them to me at recording sessions.

“I’m smoking again,” I said to the producer at a session, “even though I don’t want to.” “Come on,” he said. “You sound like a Catholic.” I saw then that people did not understand what I was feeling. I had changed. I wasn’t like other people now. One of the secretaries, a gentle girl named Cynthia, could see it. “These people don’t understand where your head is at,” she said. Clients took me to dinner at restaurants, so meat crept back into my life.

I could always get a vegetarian meal, though, something called the macrobiotic diet, at The Paradox, on East Seventh Street. The macrobiotic diet, a variation on the Chinese-Taoist concept of yin and yang, two opposing forces in the universe, was supposed to keep me healthy and mystically aware, but I had my doubts. I got whole grains at The Paradox along with vegetables and tried to drink something awful called mu tea, but no fruit, no dairy, and no sweets. Fish was served, but I never ordered it. I wasn’t tempted to try living on the diet, as the people who worked at the restaurant looked pale and sickly, constantly sniffling and snorting back their catarrh. But I liked the place. The atmosphere was relaxed, and it was a popular spot, a good place to run into friends. On warm summer nights I hung out with the others on the patio as long as I wanted. No cold, curt employee ever asked me to leave. Thus, in a matter of weeks, I saw drugs, tobacco, and even meat come back into my life. Illicit sex took a while longer.

But the Swami was still just a walk away, around the corner. Devotees came knocking on my door from time to time, asking me to fix some problem with the Swami’s tape recorder or record player. One day, after fixing something, I was in the prasada room when I saw a new altar with three unusual deities. “This is Krishna with His brother and sister,” Kirtanananda explained. Acyutananda had already told me about Krishna’s brother: “He looks just like Krishna, but He’s white.” The deities’ heads were almost as large as their bodies, and they had big smiles. The one on the left, Krishna’s brother, had big rounded-oval eyes on a white face, and His arms seemed to come out from just under His head. The one in the middle, Krishna’s sister, also had big rounded-oval eyes but on a yellow face. You could not see her arms. Krishna was on the right with big round eyes on a black face and arms like His brother’s, seeming to come out from just under his head.

I remembered having seen them on one of the paintings Kirtanananda and Hayagriva had brought from India. Strange they were, but I didn’t have to accept strange things any more. I was no longer committed to Krishna consciousness.

And if I couldn’t imagine how God had a brother and a sister, it was because I did not understand that God has relationships. Krishna’s brother, Baladeva, is an expansion of Krishna Himself. The brothers are God appearing in two forms; yet they are individuals, sometimes even disagreeing. They think of each other as older brother Baladeva, and younger brother Krishna.

Their sister Subhadra is one of Krishna’s many potencies, such as Maya, the deluding potency who keeps the fallen souls in bewilderment. Subhadra is another kind of Maya, known as Yogamaya, but she only bewilders great devotees like Krishna’s mother Yasoda and keeps them from seeing Krishna’s supreme position. Thus Yasoda is overwhelmed with motherly love for Krishna, thinking Him her son. Krishna, though all-knowing, thinks of Yasoda as His mother and even enjoys being scolded by her when He steals butter.

Deities like these, I later learned, are worshiped all over India. They are copies of larger-than- life deities in a famous temple at Puri, in the Indian state of Orissa. Every summer the three siblings ride on large chariots pulled through the streets in a festival crowded with millions of people.

Krishna in this form is called Jagannatha (Lord of the Universe). The name is the origin of the English word juggernaut.

As I stood looking at Jagannatha, Baladeva, and Subhadra, Kirtanananda opened a large kitchen pot that was sitting on the floor and took out a round, brownish object dripping with syrup. He handed it to me.

“Try this,” he said.

I popped it into my mouth. It was deliciously sweet. And when I bit into it, the thick syrup exploded out and filled my mouth. It took a few minutes to chew it and swallow it, but the sweetness stayed the whole time and lingered afterwards. “We call these ISKCON bullets,” Kirtanananda said, “because if you get shot with one of these, you’ll never recover.” Then the Swami came into the room and walked right up to me. “Even if you don’t want to be one of us,” he said, “you must associate with us. You cannot get away from us.” I felt embarrassed, not knowing what to say, like a little boy who might put his hands in his pockets and look at the floor. Even though I had walked out on the Swami, he was still concerned and wanted to bring me back.

Another time, after I had repaired something, a few of us were in the Swami’s office, and the subject of India came up. I thought I would say something to please the Swami. “I would like to go to India,” I said.

“For what?” said the Swami. “Sightseeing?” I could not answer.

Another time, after I had repaired the Swami’s record player, someone put on a record of classical Indian sitar music. The Swami smiled. I thought he must like the music. It was from India.

“Do you like this music, Swamiji?” someone asked.

“This is sense-gratification music,” the Swami said.

“But it’s a raga,” Hayagriva said. “They play it in temples.” “It is sense-gratification music,” the Swami said.

“But, but it’s a raga,” Hayagriva said, a little agitated. “They play it in temples.” “It is sense-gratification music,” the Swami said. “The musician is a businessman.” “What if he wants to become a devotee?” someone asked.

“Then he can come,” the Swami said.

“But Swami,” someone said, “weren’t you once a businessman?” “Because I went naked then, I should go naked now?” said the Swami.

“But, but it’s a raga, but it’s a raga!” Hayagriva kept insisting. “They play it in temples! It’s a raga! It’s a raga!” The Swami and I looked at each other and shared a little laugh.

One day on Mott Street, I got a telephone call from Harvey’s girlfriend, Jean. “You know,” she said, “Harvey and I went to a party last night, and I caught him fooling around with another woman, so I threw his beads in a garbage can.” I didn’t know Harvey had beads. I found out more about the beads later.

On New Year’s Eve, I was invited to the house of a friend, a young man named Yon, for some LSD. Yon was in the Marine Corps but had run away. He was later found and thrown in a military jail, but only for a few weeks, and then kicked out of the Marines, to his great relief. I heard there was going to be a midnight feast in the storefront, so I went there first. I ate till I was stuffed.

“Umapati,” said the Swami, “eat more.” “I’m full,” I said.

“Eat more.” So I had another helping. This time I was bursting.

“Umapati, eat more.” So I had still another helping. This time, I could not move.

“Umapati,” said the Swami, “eat more.” “I can’t,” I said.

“Eat more,” the Swami said. But I finally had to refuse. I was so stuffed I could barely walk, but I made it to Yon’s house for the New Year’s high. I was getting bored with LSD, though. I had once thought it would take me to infinity, but now I saw it was just the same thing over and over.

The next month I moved out of Mott Street into a second-floor apartment on East Fourth Street.

The place was clean. It only had one room, but it was as big as two rooms with a kitchen against one wall and a bathroom. The window had trees outside and let in soft daylight. I decorated the place with posters from hippie stores. I thought I had made a good deal, but my friends were shocked when they heard I had paid a fifty-dollar cleaning fee and the rent was sky-high. “Ninety dollars a month!” they exclaimed. “Are you out of your mind?” I had stopped going to the temple, but one day I got news that Stradish, the boy who had almost stopped sleeping, had had a mental breakdown. First he went to ask the Swami for fifty dollars.

“What for?” said the Swami.

“To buy gasoline,” said Stradish. “I want to pour gasoline on myself and set myself on fire.” I thought about 1963, when events in the news stirred my dormant curiosity about religion.

Seven Buddhist monks in Vietnam, one at a time over a few weeks, poured gasoline on themselves and set themselves on fire in the street, protesting against government repression of Buddhism. I saw photos of them in the newspapers, covered in flames as they sat in meditation. Was it their religion that gave them the power to do that? Me, just burning my fingertip on a hot frying pan was enough to make me jump and yell. How did these Buddhist monks sit calmly as the flames burned them to death? Was religion that powerful? The devotees called Stradish’s mother. When she came and put him in a mental hospital, we learned that he had a history of mental breakdowns. Then I understood that his going without sleep was the onset of a problem. And I understood why he was distressed.

Winter came and went. In the spring I heard that the Swami had had a heart attack and later that he had gone to a country place near San Francisco to recover. Kirtanananda had gone with him as his servant.

It was now the summer of 1967. A devotee came to my door to take me to the temple. The Swami was there, on his way to India to complete his recovery, and Kirtanananda would go with him.

But I felt awkward in front of the Swami. I had once been part of the group; now I was just an onlooker in the crowd. I wondered whether the Swami even noticed me there.

That summer, a hundred or so hippies gathered in Tompkins Square every Sunday for free rock concerts with bands playing on a stage. Joints (marijuana cigarettes) were passed around openly. One day someone threw a bunch of bananas into the crowd, and people rushed to pick them up.

The rock bands, the marijuana, the free bananas — these were the blaring trumpets heralding a great new cosmic age, the Age of Aquarius, when everyone would become enlightened about the truth of life by smoking marijuana and taking LSD, when people would throw away their inhibitions and let nudity and free sex become the norm, when there would be no more war and everyone would get along. It was already starting: “You know,” my partner, Andy, told me at the recording studio, “down south the black kids and the white kids are smoking pot together.” But I didn’t go along with it any more. The Age of Aquarius — it was entertainment? OK. It was social evolution? OK. Enlightenment? No. I knew better now.

One Sunday afternoon I ran into Stradish in Tompkins Square. He had just come out of the mental hospital and seemed to be his old self. We talked a bit, and then he left. I never saw him again.

Although I knew him for only a short time, I have often wondered what became of this gentle friend who disappeared from my life that afternoon like a kite with a broken string.

Suddenly I heard people laughing about the bananas. They had been spiked with LSD. No surprise. “We know what you’re smoking out there!” a musician shouted into the microphone. “We can smell it. Keep it up!”

## Chapter 15 - Krishna Calls Us Back

Getting high the easier way with Satyavrata soon became a regular event. Although we had both left the movement, pictures of Krishna still decorated the walls of Satyavrata’s apartment, and we often called each other by our spiritual names. Satyavrata was intelligent, able to converse on just about any subject, and especially well versed in the different kinds of yoga as well as music, drugs, and social issues. One evening, as we strolled in Manhattan’s Central Park, I spoke some words often spoken in New York: “The air is so polluted.” Satyavrata moved his head from one side to the other, taking in the vew. “And we’re in the park,” he said. “This is the best air in the city.” “That’s really bad.” “Yes,” he said. “That’s what happens when you have a philosophy that says, ‘No sense gratification.’ Why should the city be beautiful?” In my mind I saw a world of dark, grimy, iron-and-concrete cities with pungent air. Was life meant for this? I knew I would never find a higher teaching than the Bhagavad-gita, but could I devote my life to a philosophy that led to ugly cities with barely breathable air? We were both a million miles from the truth. Neither of us had read the Swami’s reflections on Krishna’s capital, Dvaraka, in the Srimad Bhagavatam: “... Fruits, flowers, beautiful gardens, parks and reservoirs of water with ducks and swans playing in the midst of lotus flowers, and cows giving sufficient milk and butter are essential for developing the finer tissues of the human body. “ \[1.11.12\] As the summer moved on, I began to miss Krishna. “I want to decorate my place with pictures of Krishna,” I said to Andy at the recording studio. And my wish came true. Hayagriva came over one day and tore the hippie posters down from the wall, replacing them with posters and smaller pictures of Krishna. But I was still not going to the temple. I was enjoying the company of my friends and a hint of success as a recording engineer. Then one day, Krishna came back into our lives.

Most hippies liked to think they were mystical, as long as they did not have to give up any sense gratification. Hippie stores (we called them “head shops”) sold religious posters from India, and with the temple nearby on Second Avenue, Krishna was becoming known around the neighborhood. I walked into a head shop one evening and found a couple I knew from the neighborhood. “My wife’s pregnant,” the husband told me, “and we’re going to name the baby Krishna.” Suddenly a beautiful poster on the wall caught my eye. It was Krishna’s head and upper torso surrounded by clouds. He was youthful and gently smiling with a flute in his hands and a crown like a jeweled tiara. Below Him was some Sanskrit writing.

“I want that poster,” I said. I bought the poster, took it home, and put it on my wall.

One afternoon in late August, Satyavrata was visiting me, and for some reason we did not “do it the easier way.” I was showing him the new poster, when suddenly the poster seemed to come alive.

Nothing miraculous, the poster didn’t move or anything, but it seemed to become a window. It had depth. The blue sky was in the background, and Krishna was in the front. In my mind I began hearing Krishna speak.

“I contain all things,” said Krishna, and I saw in Krishna all the experiences I had had with LSD, the mystical visions, the communings with nature, the age regressions. I saw myself trying to attain the unattainable, and I saw visions of Greek temples and Zen books and Taoist books and the timeless wisdom of the ancient East. Krishna could easily give all these to me, but I didn’t need them anymore. Now I was seeing Krishna Himself.

“I contain all things,” said Krishna, and I saw in Krishna shining cities and mystical truth and many things I had never known I could see, all in a setting of magnificence such as I had never known I could perceive. I felt a gentleness in Krishna, assurance that Krishna would eternally give me the understanding and compassion that I had never known I wanted or never admitted I needed.

“I contain all things,” said Krishna, and I saw in Krishna’s form the beauty I craved, like the beauty of exotic lands we dream about, and I saw happiness flavored with that beauty, a happiness I had never known existed, and it was all there for me, just for the taking.

“I contain all things,” said Krishna, and I knew I had only to look for Krishna, and I would find everything I wanted and everything I did not even know I wanted. And best of all, I would find Krishna.

I looked at Satyavrata. I could tell he was seeing something too. “Krishna really is God,” he said.

“Yes,” I said, “Krishna really is God.” We looked at the picture a while longer, until the experience subsided and the window became a poster again and Krishna became a picture again.

Afternoon was turning to evening, and it was time to go out and eat. We strolled over to The Paradox and sat at a table, talking about the beautiful picture and our wonderful experience with Krishna that afternoon. Our dinner was a long time coming, as usual, but we hardly noticed it.

“Krishna really is God.” “Everything is there in Krishna.” “The Swami was telling the truth.” “Should we go to the temple?” “Yes. Why not?” “Maybe they’ll give us a hard time.” “Could be. Let’s not go.” “But maybe we ought to go.” “That must be what Krishna wants.” “I have an idea,” I said. “Let’s go, and if they give us a hard time, we’ll leave.” We walked out into the late-summer night — no talk of air pollution this time, only Krishna — over to Second Avenue and down to the temple. We looked in the window and saw the room filled with people chanting and dancing. Brahmananda opened the door and smiled.

“It’s so nice you could come,” he said. “Today is Krishna’s birthday.”

## Chapter 16 - Life Moves On

I wanted to write to the Swami and tell him I had come back, so I bought an aerogramme, a piece of thin, light-blue writing paper that folded into a self-contained, self-stamped envelope to be sent airmail anywhere in the world for one price. But I did not mail it. I gave it to Acyutananda to deliver to the Swami. Acyutananda was nineteen now, time for him to go to college, but he was going to India instead. “We were going to pay for his college education,” his mother told me,” but if this is the education he wants, then we’ll let him have it.” The Swami was now known as Swamiji, a more respectful form of the name Swami. And I noticed on a temple brochure that he was also being called His Divine Grace. But wasn’t that a bit much? “His Divine Grace” was indeed the right title for a spiritual master in India, but how would it fit in America? Then I thought no more about it. I couldn’t always worry about what the outside world thought.

I needed my beads, so Hayagriva and I went to the Mott Street apartment. As we searched, looking everywhere without success, Harvey came in.

“I can’t find my beads,” I said.

“You know what happened to your beads,” said Harvey.

“No, I don’t.” “You know.” “No, I don’t,” I said. “Where are they?” “Jean threw them away.” “Ohh. Those were my beads. I thought they were yours.” The next day I walked over to the temple. There was a table near the door with devotional items for sale, and I bought a set of dark beads made of rosewood to start chanting on. (And I am still chanting on them today.) I also bought a set of the Srimad Bhagavatam. As I started reading about the sages in the forest of Naimisa, I was struck by the beauty of the book. Here it was! Here was the beauty I had seen in the picture of Krishna, this long-ago otherworld beauty I hungered for, that beauty I had hoped to find in the absolute truth but thought it could only be seen in a vision. Here it was, right in my hands. All I had to do was open a book, and I walked into into Krishna’s beautiful world.

The scripture itself was written five thousand years ago but made understandable for us today by Swamiji’s explanations. The English had a foreign sound, as Swamiji was not a native speaker and had written the book with no help from anyone. I thought of Swamiji innocently sitting alone at an old typewriter in a plain room in India typing in a language not his own, determined to carry out the order of his spiritual master and preach to the Western world. I might have felt sorry for him, had I known him then, thinking he was chasing a daydream, trying to do the impossible in his old age. But Swamiji knew better. He had that single-minded determination that can move mountains. He knew he had Krishna on his side.

Soon news came from India that Kirtanananda had taken vows of sannyasa, the order of renunciation, the same as Swamiji. I tried to imagine Keith as a traveling monk in saffron robes, a far cry from the days at Mott Street.

Then a letter came from Swamiji dated September 5, 1967: “My Dear Woomapati, \[That’s how we spelled it then.\] “I am so glad to receive your letter dated 28 August, through Acyutananda. You will be glad to know that he has safely arrived here via Delhi. He is a very intelligent boy, and has safely come without any difficulty.

“Your letter is very much encouraging to me, that you are appreciating my humble service in spreading Krishna Consciousness in your great country. It was my ambition to begin my movement from New York; and by Krishna’s Grace, I got good cooperation from some boys like you; and I felt too much aggrieved when some of you left me, but I was confident that all of you would come again, because Krishna Consciousness is not a material thing, and cannot be broken: it cannot be burned or wet or dried or stopped at any stage. When I received your letter, it appeared to me that I had received a letter from a lost child; so please continue your present attitude.

“I have not heard anything from Harvey. Hope he is doing well. So far my health is concerned, I have improved considerably, and I hope to return by October next.

“Kirtanananda is now a completely Krishna Conscious person as he has accepted sannyasa on the birthday of Lord Krishna with great success. He is the first sannyasa in my spiritual family, and I hope he will return back soon to begin preaching work with greater vigor and success. Please try to cooperate with the temple managers, because in my absence they might feel some difficulty. You are one of the old founders of N.Y. temple, and I hope you shall take some active part in improving the original center.

“I shall be glad to hear from you at your convenience. Hope you are well.

“With all my blessings, “A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami” Soon someone came to my apartment and told me that Kirtanananda Swami, as he was now known, was at the temple. I rushed over, breathlessly eager to see my old friend. And there he was, looking radiant in his robes, holding a danda (four thin bamboo poles wrapped together in saffron cloth). What else could I do but give him a big hug? Out of the corner of my eye I saw Brahmananda smiling broadly.

But on the street, the welcome was not as hearty. People could scarcely believe their eyes. Was this really a young white American dressed in pink robes and a raggedy pink sweater, holding a pink pole? People stared. The cars on Second Avenue stopped. In the stores, people looked at him with furrowed brows. “Why are you dressed like that?” Kirtanananda didn’t know what to say. “It’s, it’s God,” he would say softly. “You know God, don’t you?” Hayagriva had just come to New York from Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania, where he was teaching at a college. Mott Street had been taken over by Harvey, so Kirtanananda and Hayagriva, along with the Mott Street cat, moved in with me in my ninety-dollar apartment on East Fourth Street.

I sent an aerogramme to Swamiji saying how inspired we all were by Kirtanananda Swami.

Kirtanananda finally got tired of people frowning at him. Maybe the black suit and white collar of a Christian clergyman was more fitting in America. I went with him and helped pick out the clothes. But soon I began to worry about him. One weekend, Kirtanananda, Hayagriva, and I went to Hayagriva’s place in Pennsylvania along with George, the friend who had gone with me to Dr.

Mishra’s the first night I saw Swamiji. While we sat around in Hayagriva’s living room, I mentioned that I would like some halava, an Indian sweet (not the same as the Turkish variety), so Kirtanananda cooked up a batch for all of us. He served it immediately.

“Did you offer it to Krishna?” I said.

“I offered it while I was cooking it,” he said.

That sounded strange, but what would be the point in arguing? Back in New York, I came home one morning from an all-night recording session and found a book from Mott Street on the table: The Zen Teachings of Huang Po. Now I knew something was wrong. I hurried over to the temple and got there as Kirtanananda was giving class.

“Cease all conceptual thought,” he said again and again. “Go to Krishna in a flash.” It didn’t sound much like Swamiji, but it sure sounded like Huang Po. I looked around.

Everybody was sitting there soaking it up, words of wisdom from our new swami. I had to think of something, but again, arguing would be of no use. Then I got an idea. As Kirtanananda and I walked back to the apartment, I spoke to him.

“This is too advanced for them,” I said. “They can’t understand this. Better just to speak of Krishna the cowherd boy. That’s more on their level.” “Maybe you’re right,” he said.

Some of the men had shaved their heads except for a ponytail (we called it a flag), standard for devotees in India. But Kirtanananda told them they should shave off the flag in America.

Then Brahmananda got a letter from Swamiji. “I understand from Woomapati’s letter that Kirtanananda Swami is in New York,” Swamiji said, and went on to say that he had given Kirtanananda money to open a center in London. Why had he gone to New York? Another letter came from Swamiji saying the devotees should keep the flag. “When I see the devotees with tilaka and flags,” the letter said, “to me they look like angels from Vaikuntha \[the spiritual world\].” Then someone came to my apartment one morning when I was alone and told me that Kirtanananda Swami and Hayagriva had gone to the temple and taken back all the paintings and other things they had given. “This is mine,” they said, “and this is mine, and this is mine.” A young man there became so angry he spat on Kirtanananda. But they continued: “And this is mine, and this is mine.” That was the end of the road for me. They had to go. I wanted them out of my apartment. I knew Hayagriva had a dentist appointment near Washington Square Park, so I waylaid him as he came through.

“I want you guys out of my apartment,” I said. “Now.” “They spat on Keith,” he said angrily.

“I don’t care,” I said. “I want you guys out. And give me back the key. Now!” He argued for a while, but finally gave me the key.

By the end of the day they were gone, but the cat was still there. A week or so later, a young man from the temple moved in with me, and one day the cat urinated on his sleeping bag. I called Kirtanananda in Pennsylvania.

“I want to get rid of this goddamn cat.” “Have you got a key to Mott Street?” “Yes.” “Take her there.” Harvey already had a cat, so one more was not a problem. I picked up the cat, carried her over to Mott Street, and dumped her there. Then I walked back to my place. As soon as I opened the door, I felt the difference. The house felt clean, like breathing fresh air again after a long time. I made up my mind I would never have another house pet.

Life had moved on. The cat was gone. Kirtanananda was gone. Hayagriva was gone. The Zen book was gone. Mott Street was ancient history. I sat down and picked up my new Srimad Bhagavatam: “Once, deep in the forest, the sages were trying to light the sacrificial fire...”

## Chapter 17 - If You’re Going to San Francisco

One day at the recording studio Satyavrata and I were walking down the hallway laughing about something. “Have you guys been in the basement?” said Andy, wondering whether we had been sneaking a little marijuana. “No,” I said. “We’re in Krishna consciousness again. No more basement.” During this time, the temple received a letter from Swamiji saying that the man who had spat on Kirtanananda Swami should write a letter of apology. I was surprised. I had thought if anyone should apologize it should be Kirtanananda and Hayagriva. Not satisfied with taking their things back and leaving, they had also written rude letters to Swamiji. When Swamiji initiated Kirtanananda into the sannyas order, he gave the new monk a certificate of sannyas. Now Swamiji was asking him to return it. “We will return the certificate,” they wrote. “We don’t equate sannyas with a piece of paper.

Maybe the surabhi cows \[in the spiritual world\] are IBM’d \[computerized\].” But Swamiji still considered Kirtanananda a devotee and a sannyasi, not to be insulted. But then again, Swamiji had still considered me a devotee after I left, and there was even the young man who had gone after him with a knife.

And I had another surprise coming: Satyavrata told me he was leaving Krishna consciousness again. “But I want to give a donation to the temple,” he said as he took out his checkbook and wrote a check for a thousand dollars. In the morning, Brahmananda’s eyes opened wide when I gave it to him.

The treasurer hurried over to the bank to deposit the check before Satyavrata changed his mind and stopped the transaction. But I knew Satyavrata wouldn’t do that. He was honest.

At the studio, the trips to the basement started again, but Satyavrata went alone now. When he came back upstairs, I smelled the marijuana on his breath even from across the room. It stunk. Andy had said he didn’t smell it on us, and nobody else seemed to notice it, but it smelled like burning rags.

Smokers say marijuana is harmless, but putting something so vile-smelling in your body has to be bad for you.

Soon more news came, good news this time: Swamiji was on his way back to America. And then he arrived in San Francisco. At home, I called Swamiji. We chatted about this and that, even the time difference between San Francisco and New York.

“What time is it there?” asked Swamiji.

“Three o’clock,” I said.

“Here it is twelve o’clock,” said Swamiji.

It felt good to hear Swamiji’s accent again.

One day I saw a new man in the temple, dressed in white with a blondish ponytail. “That’s Subal,” someone told me. Subal and his wife, Krishna Devi, had opened a temple in Santa Fe, in the mountains of New Mexico. Krishna Devi was in Santa Fe now, taking care of things. One day a young brahmachari picked up the telephone to make a call but heard Subal and Krishna Devi talking on an extension. He put down the phone and smiled. “I miss you, honey,” he said, imitating what he had just heard. That was nice, I thought, an affectionate couple.

Rayarama, now the editor of Back to Godhead magazine, had visited Swamiji in San Francisco and was telling us how the hippies had taken over Haight Street, near the temple. “They ask you for money,” he said, “and if you don’t give it to them, they beat you up, and they’re all wearing these buttons that say ‘love’ and ‘peace.’” In the spring of 1968 I quit my job and went to San Francisco to see Swamiji. The temple there was a storefront with living quarters in back, on Frederick Street just off Golden Gate Park, where Swamiji walked in the morning. The park ran three miles west from Stanyan Street to the Pacific Ocean and was half a mile wide, containing lakes, a bandshell for musical performances, a botanical garden, a rhododendron dell, a flower conservatory, and a hill known as Hippie Hill for its popularity with pot smokers. A narrow strip about a block wide, called the Panhandle, ran east of Stanyan for about three fourths of a mile.

When Mukunda, Janaki, and Joan left New York for India, they made it as far as San Francisco.

Joan married a man she met there, a photographer, and they were now initiated as Yamuna Devi Dasi and Guru Das. The temple president, a brahmachari named Jayananda, was an extraordinarily fine man: gentle, soft-spoken, polite, intelligent, always ready to help. He made friends with people instantly. Everybody liked him and looked up to him.

I moved into the brahmachari ashram, an apartment on Willard Street, a few blocks west and uphill. (In San Francisco, everything is either uphill or downhill.) The householders had apartments on Ashbury Street, east of the temple. (The intersection of Haight and Ashbury streets gave the neighborhood its name: Haight-Ashbury.) One day I strolled over to Stanyan Street, just around the corner from the temple and then over to Haight Street, where the hippies were. I thought I had walked into a scene of devastation, maybe a war zone. Stores were empty and shuttered. Young people in old clothes were milling around everywhere, some lying down or sleeping in doorways. A young woman in a revealing blouse was walking down the sidewalk hawking her wares of LSD and hashish: “Acid! Hash! Acid! Hash!” The singer Scott MacKenzie had romanticized the area in a hit record, “If You’re Going to San Francisco Be Sure To Wear Flowers in Your Hair.” But the devotees waxed a bit less poetic about it. They called it “the pigpen.” Haight Street runs eighteen blocks from Market Street downtown to Stanyan Street and Golden Gate Park. The pigpen took up the few blocks before Stanyan. During the day a steady stream of cars drove up Haight Street with curious people gawking at the hippies. At the end, on the corner of Stanyan, was Gargamuni from New York selling strings of beads to the tourists: “Hippie love beads! Get your hippie love beads!” The temple was a haven of peace, off the main street. On the altar were deities like the ones I had seen in New York: Jagannath, Baladeva, and Subhadra with their big smiles and big loving eyes.

The deities were a few feet tall, carved by Shyamasundar, a tall light-haired young man with a ready smile. He and his wife, Malati, a vivacious young woman, were among the first devotees to join in San Francisco. The deities were copied from small ones that Malati had found in an import store.

Below them on the altar was another form of Krishna, known as Kartamaji (kar-TAH-ma-jee), standing with His right hand holding a flute and His left hand on His hip. Swamiji called Him the Boss, sometimes the Naughty Boy.

But Swamiji had gone to Los Angeles, so I left most of my things in the Willard Street apartment and went there to see him at the temple on West Pico Boulevard, also a storefront with living quarters in back. Aniruddha, a smiling jolly brahmachari, lived alone in the temple. A householder couple, Dayananda and his wife, Nandarani, lived in an apartment nearby with their baby daughter, Chandramukhi, and a shaggy dog named Jaya.

I arrived at the temple in the afternoon and waited for Swamiji to come to the evening program.

When Swamiji came into the temple and saw me, he smiled broadly. I walked up to him, and he hugged me. I touched his shoulder in reciprocation, but he immediately drew back.

That night during his lecture, Swamiji spoke about our eyes. “The scriptures say that the sun is an eye of Krishna,” he said, “so unless Krishna sees first, we cannot see. We may be proud of our so- called eyes, but in the absence of the sun, we cannot see. We have to construct an artificial sun.” I visited Swamiji every day at his apartment, over the rear garage of a private home a few miles away. Swamiji was being served by a householder couple, Gaurasundar das and Govinda dasi, both of them artists — Gaurasundar was in the process of carving a wooden deity of Krishna — and both of them serious in their mood and their expression. Subal from Santa Fe had also come. His wife, Krishna devi, had run off with a young man who had been coming to the temple.

One of the first questions I asked Swamiji was about my name. “Is Umapati another name for Lord Shiva?” I asked.

“Yes,” said Swamiji. “That means you are the servant of the servant.” I asked about putting on tilak, as I had seen devotees in California putting it on in the bathroom.

“Tilak should be put on in front of Krishna,” said Swamiji. “I taught you that in New York. But don’t worry about the rules and regulations here. Just get people to chant Hare Krishna.” Another question was about a thought I had had. I had been trying to see the world in relation to the scriptures, but I did not know how.

“Is the world a reflection of scripture?” I asked.

“Why are you thinking like that?” said Swamiji.

“Is it wrong?” I said.

“Why are you thinking like that?” said Swamiji again.

“Is it wrong?” “It is not wrong,” said Swamiji. “The world is a reflection of scripture, but scripture is not a reflection of the world. You should not think like this as an excuse for sense gratification.” “Oh no,” I said. “When I see somebody doing something wrong, like eating meat, I think, ‘My spiritual master said this is bad.’” “Ahh,” said Swamiji. “When you think like that, your life at once becomes sublime.” One afternoon, after Subal and I had just shaved Swamiji’s head with an electric hair clipper, Swamiji talked about present-day conveniences. “We should live moderately,” he said, “Not too much austerity, not too much sense gratification.” Soon I decided to have my own head shaved for the first time. Subal went over my head with the electric clippers, and while I was in the bathroom finishing the job with a safety razor, Swamiji came in. “Oh!” he said with a big smile and went back to his room. After I finished, I went into Swamiji’s room to show off my new head. “You look very nice,” said Swamiji from behind his floor- level desk, “just like brahmachari.” One day Swamiji told Govinda dasi that he wanted some “ladyfinger” for lunch.

“Ladyfinger?” said Govinda dasi. “What’s that?” The only ladyfingers we knew of were finger- size little cakes made with eggs, the kind my mother and everybody else’s mother used to like with their coffee.

“It’s green,” said Swamiji, “and it has seeds inside.” “That almost sounds like okra,” I said.

“Yes!” said Swamiji. “Okra! That’s it.” I had always thought of okra as part of the American south. How did Swamiji know about it? I had yet to learn it was a popular vegetable in India.

One time, after lunch prasada, I noticed that the dirty dishes were stacked on the floor just outside the kitchen. Prabhupada had told the disciples to bring them in the kitchen for washing just a few at a time.

Another time, after lunch prasada, we all went into different rooms to lie down. I was the first to get up, and as I was walking around the apartment, Swamiji came out of his room. “You are not resting?” he said.

One time after Swamiji had lain down to rest, we heard the neighbor mowing his lawn with a power mower. Govinda dasi leaned out the window. “Could you do that another time?” she said. “My grandfather is trying to sleep.” Her grandfather? Would anyone ever believe Govinda Dasi was Swamiji’s granddaughter, with her blond hair and pink skin and her American girl-next-door face? “Does it hurt the grass when you cut it?” Govinda dasi asked.

“It is like cutting hair,” said Swamiji.

Thus I passed my days, going to see Swamiji every afternoon. Then Swamiji and the rest of us flew to San Francisco.

## Chapter 18 - Off to Santa Fe

When we arrived in San Francisco that evening, I learned that the brahmachari ashram on Willard Street was now Swamiji’s apartment and the young men had moved into the basement of the temple. I would also move there, but first I went with Swamiji and his servants to the apartment to pick up my belongings. While I was in my former room, I looked out the doorway and noticed Swamiji walking with a few turns of his sacred thread wound around his right ear. I walked up to him. “Why do you have your sacred thread over your ear?” “Because I have been to the bathroom,” Swamiji said. “When you go to the bathroom, you wrap your sacred thread over your ear.” The devotees at the temple, of course, had been hoping to see Swamiji that night. There would be kirtan, a lecture (by Swamiji, they hoped), and more kirtan. Those of us at the apartment also expected to go with Swamiji to the temple, but Swamiji told us, “I will not go to the temple tonight.” One of Swamiji’s servants called the temple. The devotees would surely feel disappointed.

But after about an hour, Swamiji changed his mind. “I would like to go to the temple,” he said.

Swamiji’s servants and I strolled the few blocks downhill with Swamiji, but when we walked into the temple, the devotees did not see us. The first kirtan had just ended, and they were bowing, touching their heads to the floor while someone recited the prayer. When they sat up, they gasped with surprise. Swamiji was sitting on his lecture seat. Swamiji spoke, and then he led the second kirtan.

Kirtan can be lively, or it can be mellow and sweet. This kirtan was as sweet as sweet can be. The devotees’ disappointment had turned into unexpected pleasure.

The next day, Swamiji and a few disciples were walking in Golden Gate Park.

“Were you chanting last night?” Swamiji asked a young man.

“Oh yes,” the disciple said. “It was wonderful.” “Were you dancing?” Swamiji asked another young man.

“I wanted to, but the temple was crowded and there was no room to dance.” Swamiji turned to Gargamuni. “Were you chanting?” “No,” said Gargamuni. “I was afraid I would cry.” “When you are with ordinary people you should not cry,” said Swamiji, “but when you are with devotees you can cry because they will know you are crying for Krishna.” Another day in the park, Subal from Santa Fe, who was now without a wife, asked a question.

“Should I take a formal vow of vanaprastha \[retired life\]?” he said.

“We have had enough of people taking formal vows,” Swamiji answered, alluding to Kirtanananda Swami.

Another day in the park, Swamiji spoke of the Sanskrit words put (pronounced poot) and mut (moot). Put means semen, Swamiji explained, and mut is urine. Semen and urine come from the same place, so a good son is said to be the son of semen and a bad son is the son of urine. I was surprised to hear Swamiji speak of semen and where it came from when some young teenage girls were with us, but I saw over the years that Swamiji often spoke frankly about such topics. (I remembered this conversation years later reading the Ramayana. When Lord Rama faced the demon Ravana on the battlefield, the Lord called him the son of urine.) As I walked next to Swamiji in the park one day, I tried out a sort of mind exercise I had invented. Although I was not using any drugs, I still had a desire for the experience, and I had discovered that by meditating in a certain way and twisting my mind, I induced a sort of quick high.

As I walked next to Swamiji, I tried out my trick a few times, but every time I did, Swamiji turned his head and looked at me. It seemed he knew I was up to something. I gave it up then and there.

One afternoon Janaki and I were at the Willard Street apartment standing and talking with Swamiji while he sat at a low table. Someone brought a plate of fruit slices, and Swamiji immediately gave a slice to Janaki and to me. I felt like a child, holding out my hand to get a piece of fruit. Only Swamiji could do this — humble me to the status of a child and make me happy about it — but I still felt a little embarrassed.

One night at Willard Street, Guru Das was showing photo slides on a screen while Swamiji sat at his table. A slide of the altar with Kartamaji, the Naughty Boy, came up. “I think this statue has changed very much since we have had Him,” said Swamiji. I was surprised to hear Swamiji call Him a statue. Swamiji had told us that the worshipable forms of Krishna are not dead statues but manifestations of Krishna Himself, coming in forms of wood or stone so people without spiritual vision could see Him and serve Him. Swamiji called Them “deities.” Then Guru Das showed a slide of Kartamaji taken a year earlier. The difference was astounding. Kartamaji had put on weight, His color had deepened, His expression was happier. There was no doubt about it.

Afterwards I asked Guru Das about the change. “Have you painted Him since you’ve had Him?” “No,” said Guru Das, “we haven’t done anything.” After some weeks Swamiji went to New York.

The time for a parade had come. Swamiji had named the temple New Jagannath Puri and asked the devotees there to take Jagannath, Baladeva, and Subhadra on a ride through the street every year in a festival like the festival at Jagannath Puri in India. The previous year, the deities had ridden on the back of a flatbed truck. This year Shyamasundar and some others would build a large wooden chariot about twelve feet high with long ropes to be pulled by festivalgoers up Haight Street and all the way through Golden Gate Park to the Pacific Ocean. Eventually, our yearly festival made it onto the city’s official calendar.

During the course of construction, Shyamasundar got a letter from Kirtanananda Swami. He and Hayagriva had come back to Krishna consciousness. They had acquired a piece of land in the hills of West Virginia and were writing to devotees around the country asking for donations of musical instruments and whatever else was needed to start an ashram. Swamiji himself had named the land New Vrindavan, in honor of Vrindavan, the place of Krishna’s childhood pastimes. Shyamasundar laughed at the letter. It had to be some kind of scheme. But a few days later, I got a personal letter from Kirtanananda saying they had indeed come back. I welcomed the news. I was back with my old friends again, if not physically at least in consciousness.

On the festival day, hundreds of people came to pull the long ropes and guide the chariot through the park under a beautiful sunny sky. Afterwards at the oceanside, while devotees and guests were chanting and feasting on prasada, I climbed to the top of the chariot, where I would be alone with Jagannath, Baladeva, and Subhadra. Their big eyes and smiling faces greeted me as I sat down next to Baladeva and started fanning them. There was the sound of kirtan, and the air was warm and pleasing as the deities looked out at the bright blue sky over the beach. I felt glad to be there but glad in a different way: not just for myself but glad to see Jagannatha, Baladeva, and Subhadra enjoying the festival and looking out over the beautiful panorama. It was a new feeling. Their pleasure was shining out from them, and I also felt it as they shared their vision of the festival with me. It was what Swamiji had been saying: Krishna is the primary enjoyer, and we are secondary enjoyers. Krishna sees first, and then we also see.

In the evening we took Jagannatha, Baladeva, and Subhadra back to the temple. For the next few days I noticed that the deities had the glow of a fresh suntan. The others were talking about it too.

Jagannatha, Baladeva, and Subhadra were telling us they had had a good time at the beach and gotten a suntan besides.

Some devotees suggested that I go back to Los Angeles. The brahmachari, Aniruddha, needed some help. Mukunda and Janaki were also going and would take over the apartment, as Dayananda and Nandarani were moving out. The three of us drove down together. I moved into the temple, and Mukunda and Janaki moved into the apartment. Dayananda and Nandarani put baby Chandramukhi and Jaya, the shaggy dog, in the back seat of a car and started for Florida, where Dayananda would study computers.

After a time, I went for a few days to San Francisco, where I ran into my old friend Yon, the runaway marine who had invited me for LSD on New Year’s Eve (and his name was really Stanley).

The clean-cut bright-faced boy was now dirty and disheveled, living on Haight Street and in the park.

“I don’t even know what drugs I’ve been taking,” he told me. “People just come by and put a pill in your hand, and you take it.” “Come on with me to the temple,” I said, and we walked over.

As soon as we turned the corner on Frederick Street, Janaki’s sister, Yamuna Devi Dasi, happened to come out of the temple and saw me. She looked as happy to see me as I was to see her, and we started to bow to each other. Suddenly I knew this was one of those magic moments in life when my brain opens up and lets these few seconds come in with hurricane force. It doesn’t have to be something unusual — devotees bow to each other all day — but there is some kind of brief, intense awakening that makes the moment stay with me. I could tell that the moment had struck Stanley too. It would change his heart.

Stanley went with me to Los Angeles, and we stayed in the storefront with Aniruddha. At that time, I wrote to Swamiji about a thought I had. “We don’t always need to be happy,” I wrote. “I think it is as good to be sad as to be happy as long as you are Krishna Conscious.” I had been thinking about this for weeks, maybe months. It might be hard to imagine that there was something better than happiness, but happiness came and happiness went. The pleasure of Krishna consciousness could be with me all the time. Could I force myself to place more importance in Krishna consciousness than in a particular emotion, like happiness, which could change at any time? If my thought was right, then I had discovered something, but I wanted to confirm it with Swamiji. After some days, I had still not gotten an answer, but Swamiji was busy taking care of many devotees, and I should not think I was so important.

Stanley sobered up, and his body began eliminating the poison it had taken in from all the drugs.

He smelled awful. Sometimes you could hardly go near him. Here was more proof of what drugs really were. Then I started wondering what we hippies must have smelled like in 1966 when we first started coming to see Swamiji. We had all been using drugs and eating meat, which also increases one’s body smell. But Swamiji never said anything about it.

After a couple of weeks I got a letter from Swamiji. “I was in Boston,” the letter said, “and I came back to New York to find a beautiful letter from you.” My thought was confirmed. Now I could continue cultivating this idea. Krishna consciousness was better than happiness. Although it started out as a small thought, it changed my life over the years and enabled me to carry on through difficult times. Krishna even tells Arjuna, early in the Bhagavad-Gita, not to be disturbed by the changes of happiness and distress. It is easy to be enthusiastic about spiritual practices when everything is going your way, but things change and you have to look to a higher principle than happiness.

Certainly, over the years I have gone through periods of happiness more intense than anything I had ever gotten from material pleasure, especially in the most difficult times, when I had no choice but to depend on Krishna. And it was a better kind of happiness, shining down on me like sunlight.

But the best part was knowing that it came from Krishna. I would remember the words of Krishna’s aunt Kunti Devi: “Let these calamities come again and again for then we see You, and seeing You means we will not see repeated birth and death.” By this time, Subal had returned to Santa Fe, and a letter came from Swamiji asking me to go there and help him. “Subal is my good friend,” I wrote to Swamiji, “and it will give me great pleasure to work with him in your divine service.” Then I left for Santa Fe.

## Chapter 19 - Santa Fe and Onward

Santa Fe with its Spanish colonial buildings was a popular spot for tourists and intellectuals, but my first and lasting impression was of a sleepy mountain town where nothing ever happened.

American Indians sold handcrafted items around the central square, but I did not see many customers.

Still, the quietness of Santa Fe had its good points. I had time to think about how refreshing the mountain air felt in my lungs and how large the sky seemed, especially to a New York boy. Whenever a storm was brewing, the rumble of thunder and the neighing of horses in the distance created a mysterious, ominous feeling like a scene in a suspense movie. I was living next to nature, something I had never experienced before.

About a year earlier, a few devotees from San Francisco had gone to Santa Fe and moved into Seton Castle, once the home of E.T. Seton, founder of the Boy Scouts of America. Surely they would find potential devotees among the young people who had come from all over the country to live in the surrounding mountains, looking for truth in closeness to nature. But all the devotees except Subal had left by now, telling tales of a haunted castle. The ghosts, so said a local friend, were attracted by the Indian religious symbols Seton had used as decorations.

I laughed when I heard that. Of course, I had never met the late Mr. Seton and could not judge him, but would he, or any other white man, decorate his walls with the symbols of Christianity? Of course not. Those symbols were sacred, but who cared about the symbols of someone else’s religion? They were mere decorations to be plastered on the walls. Anyway, I did not care about ghosts. I had never seen one and didn’t want to, but I was soon to learn that they did not need castles or Indian symbols.

Subal, on his own now, had started a temple near the edge of town in a storefront with living quarters. As I think back on those days, I have to admire his courage and determination as he struggled to raise money making little plastic deities of Jagannatha, Baladeva, and Subhadra, but the process was slow and the demand sparse. Maybe I could help by taking a job. I looked around, but with nothing available except in restaurants, I had to give up. There was no question of working with meat, fish, or eggs. I wrote to Swamiji, and he confirmed my thought.

I was living with Subal in the storefront when one dark night as I turned off the lamp and snuggled into my sleeping bag, I saw a glowing brown spot floating in the air. I tried to tell myself that it was only the lamplight persisting in my eyes. I started to chant Hare Krishna, but a strange, eerie feeling came over me. I picked up my sleeping bag and walked into Subal’s room. “I’m going to sleep in here,” I said. “It’s weird out there.” “Oh, no!” said Subal. “Don’t tell me they’re here too.” Subal went into my room. “Yeah,” he said, his eyes open wide, his mouth in one of those non-smiling smiles. “There’s one here. He’s strong too.” “One night,” he continued, “I was in the back here, but in my mind’s eye I saw a strange creature looking in the front window.” I shuddered. Strange creatures looking in the window? Glowing brown spots in the dark? Not exactly what I had been hoping for. I lay down on the floor in Subal’s room, and he put a copy of the Srimad Bhagavatam next to my head. “This usually keeps them away,” he said. As I lay there, hoping to fall asleep, I felt a cold hand grab my feet. I sat up with a start. But that was the end of it. I slept through the rest of the night, and I never saw anything strange after that. “Sometimes they go away when they’re found out,” Subal told me.

Life was quiet and slow. Only a few people came to the temple. Subal was a gentle person, and we got along, but I soon began to feel bored. Maybe I was wasting my time in this place where nothing ever happened. Maybe I belonged in a big city like New York. I tried meditating on an Indian poster that I liked — Krishna sitting on a rock with His arm around a calf — and it worked. As long as I kept the poster in my mind, I felt satisfied. I mentioned it to Subal. “Now you understand Santa Fe,” he said.

But I wasn’t determined enough, not even enough to follow my own philosophy about happiness and Krishna consciousness. I let my mind wander. The more I thought about the fun of living in New York, the less I thought about my promise to Swamiji, that I would help Subal. I secretly called Andy at the studio in New York. If he sent me some money, I would go to New York and work with him again. Andy sent the money, and one day when Subal was away, I sneaked out of the temple like a dog with his head down and his tail between his legs and got on a long-distance bus to Denver and from there to New York. I felt free. I could wander at will again, like an old hippie.

At Mott Street I found my friend George. We drove up to Connecticut and spent a relaxing evening with friends, chatting and listening to music. George and I stayed the night. In the morning I went outside into the back yard to enjoy the beautiful summer day. The sun was shining, the sky was blue, birds were chirping, the air was clean and warm. All around were geen plants and flowers. My senses were rolling in pleasure, but...

Suddenly I realized that my senses had become prison walls, surrounding me, closing in on me.

I could not see beyond them anymore. I couldn’t get out. I was trapped. I had lost the beautiful vision of Krishna consciousness. I had gone back to where I was before I met Swamiji. Oh no! What stupid thing had I done this time? My bus ride to freedom? What a joke! It had been a ride into jail.

I took a train back to New York and went to the temple. The devotees already knew I had left, and a godsister was the first to say something. “Prabhupada said we don’t realize how high we are until we fall down.” Did she say, “Prabhupada”? Were the devotees in New York using the name “Prabhupada” now instead of “the Swami” or “Swamiji”? I had heard the name before, but used for Swamiji’s spiritual master. Now our Swamiji had also become Prabhupada, the master at whose feet other masters sit.

A householder couple, Hansadutta Das and Himavati Devi dasi, were moving to Montreal.

They were both artists. Himavati, a tall stately natural blond, was a practical-minded person and always knew the right solution to a problem. Prabhupada once said about her character that she was as beautiful inside as outside. Hansadutta was well known among the devotees for his beautiful singing and his brilliant classes. I took over their apartment on East Seventh Street.

Soon afterwards, I walked over to Mott Street and found Hayagriva standing in the kitchen. He and Kirtanananda Swami were on their way back to West Virginia after seeing Prabhupada in Montreal. “I have never seen anyone as magnanimous as Prabhupada,” said Hayagriva. “After all the things we did, he didn’t say a word about it. He just took off his garland and put it on Kirtanananda.” I was happy to hear this, but not surprised. It sounded like Prabhupada, sweeping away past mistakes, dynamic, always going forward.

Montreal was just an hour’s flying time from New York, and on the weekend, I went there to see Prabhupada. I also found three families from California there: Gurudas with his wife, Yamuna; Mukunda with his wife, Janaki; Shyamasundar with his wife, Malati, and their baby daughter, Saraswati. They were on their way to open a center in London. Gurudas took me to the house where Prabhupada was staying.

“I’m embarrassed to go in,” I told Gurudas. “I’ve done something stupid.” “Don’t worry,” he said. “Prabhupada knows you’re a big-city boy.” As soon as I walked into the room, Prabhupada smiled. “Santa Fe didn’t have enough society for you?” I felt at ease.

“There are ghosts in the Santa Fe temple,” I said during the conversation.

“Oh?” said Prabhupada. “There is chanting of Hare Krishna and there are ghosts?” Prabhupada was going to give second initiation to the three couples that evening, but surely not to me, not after what I had done. As the ceremony was about to start, Prabhupada turned to me.

“Are you ready?” he asked.

“Yes,” I said. What else could I say? “Where is your dhoti?” asked Prabhupada.

I had not brought a dhoti, but Himavati found a piece of cloth I could wear like one. Thus the ceremony started, and I was part of it.

Now, years later, whenever I give second initiation to my own disciples, my mind goes back to that evening. I see Prabhupada sitting next to me, only Prabhupada and me, nothing else in the world, as he shows me how to count to ten on the pads of my fingers and tells me to try. I feel nervous, wondering how many times I will get it wrong, but Prabhupada reassures me just by his peaceful presence. I hear him giving me the mantra word by word, his powerful voice softened almost to a murmur, his perfectly manicured golden-brown finger moving across the printed sheet, pointing to each word as he says it. I feel the hush of the moment and the gentleness of Prabhupada, and in my mind I pray that I can give the same moment to the young disciple sitting next to me now, waiting to learn the mantra.

One afternoon when several of us were with Prabhupada in his room I again noticed the softness of his heart. Malati was sitting in the back holding baby Saraswati. Suddenly Prabhupada stopped speaking. “Oho!” he exclaimed. “Your baby has tilak!” I was not surprised to see the tilak, nor, I am sure, were any of the others. It seemed normal, but Prabhupada complimented the little extra effort his disciple had made.

I went to see Prabhupada several times that summer. I visited him at his house as much as I could, usually along with a few others, and we talked about this and that.

We talked about eating in restaurants.

“You must pay for the food,” Prabhupada said.

“Sometimes other people take me to restaurants and pay for the food,” I said.

“That is all right,” Prabhupada said, “as long as someone pays.” We talked about women.

“Women are not here just for our enjoyment,” said Brahmananda.

“Women are not here for our enjoyment at all,” said Prabhupada.

Someone brought up Jesus’ advice: “Cast not your pearls before swine.” “There is a Bengali proverb,” said Prabhupada. “‘Do not give pearls to a monkey.’ What will a monkey do if you give him pearls?” Then he mimed a monkey chewing on a string of pearls. It only lasted a second or two, but it was a perfect mime, and funny.

When we talked about the deity, someone mentioned the Catholic Church. “The Catholics say the deity of Jesus is not really Jesus,” he said.

“Then Jesus \[would not be\] not absolute,” said Prabhupada.

“But many Catholics have attained perfection by worshiping the deity of Jesus,” someone else said.

“If you touch fire,” said Prabhupada, “it will act like fire even if you don’t know it is fire.” A girl whose boyfriend had just been initiated came to see Prabhupada.

“You are living with him?” Prabhupada asked.

“Not any more,” said the girl. “We didn’t think it was very nice.” “You should get married,” said Prabhupada. “We do not tell couples to separate. We tell them to get married.” Another day, someone brought in a teenage girl. “This is Wendy,” he said. “She wrote you a letter from San Francisco.” Some minutes later, while Prabhupada was talking with the disciples, Wendy suddenly spoke up. “Prabhupada,” she said, “I want to be a devotee.” “From your letter,” said Prabhupada, “I think you are already a devotee.” So Prabhupada initiated Wendy soon afterwards and named her Rukmini.

At the Montreal temple one day, I heard that an import store was selling small deities, a little over a foot tall, of Jagannatha, Baladeva, and Subhadra. I bought a set and took them to New York.

I had a room I could use as a temple with a narrow table for an altar. Above, I hung the picture of Krishna looking out through clouds, the picture that had talked to me on Krishna’s birthday. I bought some cloth and did my best to sew clothes for the deities. I found posters of Krishna in neighborhood shops and put them up in the apartment, with a large poster in the living room showing Krishna speaking the Bhagavad-gita to Arjuna. The sunlight coming through the window onto the poster created a beautiful scene.

My apartment had been transformed into a world of spiritual beauty. And I had learned something. Previously, I had thought of deities and pictures as artifacts, things you buy in a store, things you can lose. What if next year Indian art was not in fashion anymore? How could I depend on deities and pictures if I could not find any? Now I knew. Deities and pictures were direct manifestations of Krishna. If I didn’t find any, then I had to make the effort to get them, even make them myself. That’s what bhakti-yoga was: using the material energy to serve Krishna. Wasn’t this what Prabhupada had been saying? I felt clean inside. I knew I had taken a big step forward on the path to Krishna consciousness.

At the New York temple Krishna put himself into my hands again by the mercy of Brahmananda.

“Here, take this,” Brahmananda said and handed me a small pile of paper from his desk.

Prabhupada’s Bhagavad-gita As It Is was about to be published, and here was the whole book, sent for approval from the publisher, Macmillan, printed on one very, very long sheet of paper, one page below the other. Hansadutta and Himavati had made the living room of the apartment into an art studio with a large worktable. I put the book on the table, and whenever I had some time, even a minute, I looked at it. The Gita was leading me by the hand into the same beautiful world I had found when I started reading the Srimad Bhagavatam.

On Janmastami, Krishna’s Birthday, I left work at noon to go to Montreal. Tomorrow was Prabhupada’s birthday, and I had bought a bottle of rose oil for him. Still, I thought it would be nice to give something else too, so I went to a few upscale stores in midtown Manhattan but did not find anything. Disappointed, I got into a taxi and started home to pick up my things. Then I saw a store I had never seen before. It was worth a try. I paid the driver, went into the store, and looked around till I found a golden bowl, about six inches across.

“Is this solid gold?” I asked the saleswoman.

“It’s gold plate on top of sterling silver,” she said. “It’s a fashion started by Jackie Kennedy.” I arrived at the Montreal temple about ten o’clock that night. The temple and surrounding rooms took up an entire floor in a multi-story building. The place had once been a bowling alley, and the bowling lanes were still there in a large room. There was plenty of space for people to live there, and some people had even put up small tents.

The Janmastami program was still going on, and I walked up to Prabhupada and gave him the bowl and the rose oil wrapped in a package. Next on the program was a dance by an Indian woman about “a milkmaid named Radha on her way to sell milk at the market when Krishna stops her.” Prabhupada spoke. “Even fifty years ago,” he said, “people gave milk away free in Vrndavan.” The next morning Prabhupada’s servant asked me whether the bowl was solid gold. Later, in Prabhupada’s apartment, Prabhupada held up the bowl for everyone to see. The devotees made little ooh’s and aah’s of approval. “I am going to use it to offer sweet rice to Krishna,” said Prabhupada. I felt like a little child who had just been patted on the head.

In the temple the next day, during the celebration of Vyasa Puja, the spiritual master’s birthday, Prabhupada had every disciple stand and offer a testimonial to him. Another bold move. Prabhupada had put aside the usual standard of modesty and told the disciples to glorify him because that is what a disciple is supposed to do.

Then Prabhupada himself spoke. He told how his spiritual master had appeared to him in a dream saying, “Follow me.” “I knew he wanted me to take sannyasa,” said Prabhupada. “I thought, ‘How horrible! I will have to leave my children.’” The dream came several times. Still, Prabhupada hesitated. “But now,” Prabhupada continued, “I have so many nice children. So you never lose by serving Krishna.” Finally, I went to Montreal and found that Prabhupada was going to fly to Santa Fe the next day.

“Can you come with me to Santa Fe tomorrow?” he asked me.

“Tomorrow?” I said. “I did not bring any money or clothes or anything.” Prabhupada asked me a few times that day to go with him, but I was afraid that if I simply walked out of my job, no one would ever hire me again. (As it turned out, after that job I never worked in recording again anyway.) That evening, I went to see Prabhupada at his house and told him I could not go. I also gave him the beads I had bought at the New York temple and asked him to chant on them, even though I had already been using them.

A few weeks later, Subal came to New York. He had closed the temple in Santa Fe. “Why did you leave?” he asked me, but I had no answer. If I had stayed or if I had gone back with Prabhupada, maybe Subal could have kept the temple open. I had let everyone down.

## Chapter 20 - Back to Los Angeles

I could never make up my mind whether I wanted the excitement of New York City or the palm trees and sunshine of California, so in the fall of 1968 I decided to quit my job once again and move to Los Angeles.

But there was also a deeper reason: Prabhupada was in Los Angeles. When I was with Prabhupada, no matter where it was, I was in the best place in the world. Just the thought of being with Prabhupada, of seeing him smile and hearing him speak, was enough to make me go anywhere. In Los Angeles I could live in the temple — it was bigger than the one in New York — and see Prabhupada every day. What was I accomplishing in New York anyway? Although Prabhupada had said many times that serving the spiritual master, even from a distance, was more important than being in his presence, I was neither serving Prabhupada nor living near him. I was simply recording pop music. True, I was giving some money to the New York temple but not much.

Around this time, Brahmananda told me they had found a bigger place up the street at 61 Second Avenue. When I walked over to have a look, I remembered having noticed the place a couple of years earlier, laughing with Hayagriva at the dusty old tuxedos in the second-floor display window. “Those tuxedos look like they’re from 1930,” I said. “Who would ever wear one? How does the place stay in business?” So now the old tuxedo store had become Krishna’s new temple. Funny how life could put things together, wasn’t it? When I went back in the evening, the devotees were starting to clean up the place, a large store with living quarters, closed for many years, the tuxedos long gone, except the ones in the window. But old cardboard boxes were everywhere. The devotees took them out and piled them on the sidewalk.

The next afternoon, I stopped in at 26 Second Avenue and talked with Brahmananda. “How’s everything going with the new temple?” I said.

“There’s a problem,” said Brahmananda.

“What’s that?” “The garbage collectors won’t take the boxes. They said I have to get a private collection service, but that’ll cost a lot of money. Then the City said if we don’t get rid of the boxes we’ll have to pay a fine for every day they’re there.” “What’re you gonna do?” “I don’t know.” The next day I went over and talked with Brahmananda again. “What’s happening with the boxes?” I said.

“No more problem.” “How’s that?” “Some bum came by and set them on fire. The whole pile burned down to nothing.” Soon the temple was in its new home, but another problem came up at the devotees’ weekly discussion. “This used to be a clothing store,” someone said, “and there are mirrors everywhere.

When we’re having kirtan, instead of seeing the altar you keep seeing yourself.” “Why don’t we take the mirrors down?” someone said.

“We should keep them,” said someone else. “So what if you see yourself? The mirrors make the place look nice.” “I’m going to see Prabhupada soon,” I said. “I’ll ask him.” In the spring of 1969 I quit my job, this time for good, and went to Los Angeles, where the devotees had moved into a former church on South La Cienega (la-see-ENN-a-ga) Boulevard. The building, a well-built one-story wooden structure, had four large rooms, one serving as the temple itself, one as the men’s quarters, one as the women’s quarters, and one as the gathering place for taking prasada and holding meetings. The temple president, Tamal Krishna Das, had joined only a year earlier, but the temple was running smoothly under his management. He and I became good friends, and in later years, when we both had disciples, he would tell them that he and I were one soul in two bodies.

Prabhupada was living in an apartment a few miles away. “Can you buy me a house?” he asked one day as we rode in the back seat of a car.

A perfect idea! Surely I could find a good job based on my recording experience in New York and continue my career. I could render service and be with Prabhupada. What more could I ask for? But enough money to buy a house? Well, it was a tall order, but I would have to try.

Sometimes on my way back to the temple after a day of job hunting, I would stop in at Prabhupada’s apartment. “Go see the deities,” he always said, and I would go into another room and bow to his small brass deities of Sri Sri Radha and Krishna.

One day we talked about the recent marriage of Hayagriva to Shyama devi dasi, arranged by Prabhupada. “Your most intimate friend has just gotten married,” Prabhupada said.

The new couple had already gone back to New Vrindavan, so I did not get to see them. I had met Shyama Dasi once, when she and her parents were coming back from a trip to Mexico and stayed for lunch at the old Los Angeles temple on Pico Boulevard. But I never saw her again even though she was married to my friend. They divorced after several years and two sons, even though Prabhupada disapproved of divorce.

“The situation in your country is lamentable,” Prabhupada continued. “The girls are being exploited, and the boys are also being ruined. If a woman remains simple, she can become Krishna conscious more easily than a man, but if she becomes sensuous she is like an unbridled horse.” I asked him about the mirrors in the temple on Second Avenue.

“Can you arrange the mirrors so the deities see themselves?” he asked.

“No,” I said. “Not possible.” Prabhupada thought for a moment. “The mirrors can stay.” One afternoon in Prabhupada’s room I noticed that I was sitting along with some of the disciples from the first days in New York. “This is just like the old days,” I said.

“For us there is no new or old,” Prabhupada said. “We are eternal.” Another time, in a similar situation, Prabhupada looked at me. “The old days,” he said with a smile.

“Do you remember the first time I went to the park?” Prabhupada asked me one day.

I had often heard people say that Prabhupada went alone and chanted in the park soon after he arrived in New York. “I didn’t know you then,” I replied.

“No,” said Prabhupada. “You were there. Kirtanananda was there.” I understood then that the first time Prabhupada went to the park was for the kirtan in Tompkins Square.

At the temple there was a new worship service, called arati, at seven o’clock every morning and evening. The ceremony would begin with the mellow trumpeting sound of a conch blown by the worshiper, or pujari, and then the ring-ling-ling of a handbell in the pujari’s left hand, rung throughout the ceremony. With his right hand the pujari picked up items one at a time and waved them in circles in front of Jagannath, Baladeva, and Subhadra as they looked on with their big eyes and big smiles.

First he offered a few sticks of burning incense, then a small brass lamp holding a lighted wick of cotton soaked with ghee and sprinkled with camphor, then a lamp with a ghee wick but no camphor.

Then he offered some water in a conch, followed by a decorated silk handkerchief, then a flower, then a whisk made from the hairs of a yak tail, and finally a fan made of peacock feathers.

After offering each item to the deities, the pujari showed it to the audience, the idea being that something once offered to Krishna, like the food at mealtime, became prasada for the devotees to partake of. Someone walked through the audience and held the camphor lamp up to each person, followed by someone with the ghee lamp. I passed my fingertips over the little flames and then touched my warm fingertips to my forehead, a gesture of respect to the offered fire, while the toasty- sweet fragrance of burning ghee wafted into my nostrils along with the pungent smoke. Then I sniffed the fragrance of the offered flower passed around by someone else. I felt cool drops of offered water sprinkled on my head, and finally heard the trumpeting of the conch announcing the end of the ceremony.

The arati ceremony was a way of welcoming Krishna into our lives, a few times a day.

Although a worshiper, like me, might not have attained a high state of love for Krishna, he showed his desire for that state by following the rituals and rules. And there was a special song for the audience to sing about arati offered to Lord Chaitanya on the bank of the Ganges, the original Bengali words transliterated into English phonetics.

After the evening arati Prabhupada spoke, and there was kirtan, sometimes led by Vishnujan Das, a young brahmachari who had joined with his friend Tamal Krishna. He was musically talented and sometimes sang the kirtans with new melodies he had composed.

Every evening before the program, Prabhupada gathered with the disciples in a smaller room, like an alcove but larger, off to the side of the temple itself. Sometimes he told us about his early childhood, like the day he found some matches in the street. We watched as he mimed a little boy trying to light a match. Again, a perfect mime. Then, he said, one of the matches lit and set his clothes on fire. “I saw people come running from everywhere,” he said. And he still had scars on his knees from the incident.

Another time he talked about sitting on his sister’s lap while she was knitting. “I was looking up,” he said looking upwards, “and she was knitting.” One evening, Prabhupada praised our godbrother Dayananda Das, who had come back to Los Angeles after taking a course in computers. Earlier that afternoon, as Prabhupada was walking toward the temple, Dayananda had had his three-year-old daughter, Chandramukhi, prostrate herself before him. “Dayananda is a perfect father,” said Prabhupada as we sat in the little room, “because he is teaching his young daughter to prostrate herself before a saintly person.” Prabhupada told us about the holy places in India. One time Vishnujan spoke up. “You must feel separation from these places,” he said.

“Yes,” said Prabhupada, “but my service is here. If I were to go to these places now, it would be sense gratification.” Some people may wonder about this statement, as the scriptures praise the spiritual benefits of living in holy places. But Prabhupada writes, “Pure devotees of the Lord are not enthusiastic to put aside their service to the Lord in order to go visit holy places of pilgrimage. A great devotee of the Lord in modern times, Sri Narottama dasa Thakura, has sung like this: ‘To visit holy places of pilgrimage is another bewilderment of the mind because devotional service to the Lord at any place is the last word in spiritual perfection.’” One evening Birbhadra, a twelve-year-old boy, asked about the great devotee Prahlad Maharaja, a five-year-old prince of ancient times. A few days earlier some devotees had put on a puppet show about Prahlad Maharaja, the story of how his demonic father drew his sword to kill the boy and how Krishna appeared as Nara Simha Deva, half-man half-lion, and killed the father instead.

Birbhadra had worked the puppet of Prahlad Maharaja, and now he wanted to know what became of him after the incident.

“He grew up,” said Prabhupada. “He became king and got married, became a householder.” Birbhadra sat up in shock. “A householder?” he asked, his voice quivering with dismay.

Birbhadra, like many other young devotee boys, had visions of growing up to become a lifelong monk. There was a mystique about it, like growing up to become a great baseball player. The householders, in their eyes, were the ones who did not make the grade.

“That attitude is not good,” said Prabhupada.

Then Prabhupada quoted a verse: “Whether one is a brahmana, a sannyasi or a sudra — regardless of what he is — he can become a spiritual master if he knows the science of Krishna.” Prabhupada looked at me. “Teach him that this attitude is not good.” On the morning of a festival day, Prabhupada walked into the kitchen while some devotees were cooking, and he took over and cooked the whole feast. That feast had a taste like nothing else I have ever eaten. In fact, the devotees who tasted Prabhupada’s cooking that day all agreed it was special. Surely, Krishna had eaten it first.

But I especially remember one evening after a ceremony, when Prabhupada was sitting on a floor cushion in the middle of the temple as the disciples chanted and danced around him. Prabhupada suddenly stood up, his glass of water in one hand. With the thumb and a finger of the other hand, he began flicking the water. The drops were flying everywhere. Prabhupada smiled as we danced and jumped and yelled with pleasure at the rain of his water prasada on our heads. Tamal Krishna remembered it too. Over the years, when I saw him, I sometimes made a motion of flicking water, and we both would smile.

Another evening during Prabhupada’s lecture, Upendra Das raised his hand. Upendra, always gentle and polite with a sweet singing voice and a chuckling sense of humor, was one of the early San Francisco devotees, and he later traveled with Prabhupada. This evening, Upendra had a question: “Were the cowherd boys of Vrindavan eternally liberated souls who had come down with Krishna, or were they conditioned souls who had attained liberation?” “Both,” said Prabhupada.

“Does that mean a conditioned soul can become an eternal associate of Krishna?” “Certainly,” said Prabhupada. “Otherwise, what is the use of Krishna Consciousness?” When a guest asked Prabhupada, “Are you liberated?” Prabhupada answered, “Yes. And so are my disciples, but how can you tell?” Another time, Prabhupada mentioned that if you try to catch a small bug, it will run away because it has free will. “This free will,” said Prabhupada, “is the perfection of God’s creation.” Someone asked whether the spiritual master comes back to rescue disciples who have had to take birth again. “Do not make the spiritual master come back,” said Prabhupada. “Finish it in this life.” Someone brought up the analogy of the electric fan: When one’s karma comes to an end, it does not stop immediately but only after gradually slowing down, like an electric fan. “Does that mean that when karma stops it cannot be started again?” “No,” said Prabhupada. “You can start it again. You know where the switch is. On. Off. On.

Off.” Around this time, there was to be an installation of small brass deities of Sri Sri Radha Krishna on the altar.

The next day, Prabhupada spoke at the ceremony. “Do not be proud,” he concluded.

“Remember, you are dealing with Krishna.” Later, Prabhupada told the temple managers that there should be no other altars in the building.

When a young woman taking her initiation vows, mentioned “no sex,” Prabhupada interrupted her: “No illicit sex, not ‘no sex.’” The marriage ceremony of Sudama Das and Chintamani Devi Dasi had a special significance.

Some devotees would defend their own racist views by accusing Prabhupada of being racist, saying he opposed interracial marriage, but here Prabhupada performed the marriage ceremony for a black man and a white woman.

Some men also defended their supposed male superiority by falsely presenting Prabhupada as a misogynist. One morning in the temple the disciples were all standing, waiting for Prabhupada to arrive. “Prabhupada is a sannyasi,” a man announced. “He is a renounced monk, so when he comes in, the ladies should stand back.” Just then Prabhupada came in, walked right up to one of the women and started talking to her.

Someone gave Prabhupada The Aquarian Gospel, a book claiming mystical knowledge of the eighteen years of Jesus’ youth unaccounted for in the Bible. Jesus, so said the book, spent those years in India. Prabhupada seemed interested in the idea and talked about it for a few days, then suddenly stopped. “But where does this all come from?” he asked.

Around this time, the United States government claimed that it had sent two astronauts to the moon. As I stayed outside late one night, finishing my day’s chanting, I kept looking at the large yellow full moon in a clear sky and thinking about the two American men up there walking on it. I tried to imagine what it must be like. But Prabhupada in his lectures had said that the moon was a heavenly planet, where life was better than on earth, and ordinary people could not go there. If one could not even set foot in another country on earth without proper documentation, what could be said about flying onto another planet? Besides, according to the Vedas, the moon was farther away than what the scientists said.

I admired Prabhupada’s boldness, speaking out against the news that the whole country was raving about. And later, other people also doubted the government’s version. There was even a book called They Didn’t Go to The Moon, and some people suggested that the filming of the hit movie 2001: A Space Odyssey was a cover-up for filming the false moon landing though no one came up with any evidence. Still, I could not stop enjoying the thought of the two earth-men walking on the moon.

But with my mind back on earth, I soon found out that recording experience in New York counted for nothing in Los Angeles. I turned down the one job offered to me because the salary was low, but I said nothing about it to Prabhupada. A few days later, I came back to the temple just in time for the evening program so I hurried to finish my shower. I was standing in the men’s dressing room still wearing only a towel when Prabhupada came in.

“I haven’t found anything yet,” I told him.

“You are asking for too much money,” he said.

How, I wondered, did Prabhupada know I had turned down a job because the pay was low? One evening at Prabhupada’s house, Prabhupada put on a tape recording of himself singing a song — Nitai Padakamalam — and explaining it. The few of us were all sitting on the floor, including Prabhupada, who had his back toward me. When in the recording he said atheists were “simply horrible,” the sentence struck me funny, and I laughed heartily within my mind but not outwardly.

Prabhupada turned around and looked at me with a big smile, obviously enjoying the laugh with me.

How did he know I was laughing? One evening at the devotees’ weekly meeting, a young mother, Krishna Devi Dasi, told a story.

The birth of her little girl had been so difficult it looked as if Krishna Devi might leave this world.

“Suddenly I knew I was not this body,” she said. “I just knew it. And then I saw Prabhupada in front of me.” Prabhupada was not there, of course, and the birth was successful. “Some time later,” she continued, “a few devotees and I were with Prabhupada, and he said, ‘We are not this body.’ Then he looked at me, and he said, ‘You have experience.’” Prabhupada also seemed to know when someone was trying to pull a compliment out of him. A devotee artist, Muralidhar Das, painted a large picture of Radha and Krishna and the eight principal gopis, or cowherd girls. On Sunday, as Prabhupada sat down to lecture, he looked at the painting.

“First,” he said, “I want to thank Mr. Muralidhar for making such a nice painting.” He did not mention Muralidhar again, but all the disciples said later that the whole lecture was really about Muralidhar’s painting.

Shortly thereafter, some of us decided that the temple was drab and needed decoration, and Bhavananda Das was the right man for the job. Tall, dashing, and sophisticated, he was also an interior decorator. He did a great job. The ceiling was the color of Krishna’s lotus feet. The columns went from pink to violet to light blue. As soon as we started painting the outside of the building, a flock of butterflies came, attracted by the bright color. We did it all between Prabhupada’s visits so it would be a surprise. “Wait till Prabhupada sees this,” we were saying. “If he liked Muralidhar’s painting, what’s he going to say about this? He’ll really be happy.” On Sunday, Prabhupada came to the temple, walked in, sat down, and did not say one word about it.

One evening after kirtan, Upendra Das recited the closing prayers. “You said it very nicely,” Prabhupada said. At the next kirtan, I decided that I would recite the prayers and get a compliment too, so I sat up straight and spoke in my best voice with my best diction, but Prabhupada said nothing.

Soon the time came for the festival of Jagannath in San Francisco.

## Chapter 21 - On to London

Prabhupada and some disciples had already left for San Francisco, where Jagannatha, Baladeva, and Subhadra would once again ride on a large car pulled by the crowd through Golden Gate Park to the Pacific Ocean. I was to go the next day with some others. Muralidhar, the artist, would stay back and clean Prabhupada’s apartment. I was outside hanging up clothes when I saw Muralidhar walking toward me. I knew why.

“You want to go to the festival,” I said.

“Yes.” “OK,” I said. “You can go. I’ll clean Prabhupada’s house.” Prabhupada’s servant, Sridham Das, drove me to Prabhupada’s apartment, where I was to stay for a couple of days. The next day I began the cleanup starting with the altar. As I kneeled, dust cloth in hand, I saw a photo of Prabhupada with some of the disciples in San Francisco. I paused. Did Prabhupada love his disciples that much? Even to the point of putting them on his altar? I tried to imagine Prabhupada placing the picture there. Was he smiling? What was he thinking? I tried to imagine his feelings. Did he see his disciples as lovable little children? Did he see them as a manifestation of his mission, of his service to his own spiritual master? What a deep love that would be! But I knew I could not answer the questions. It is impossible for a fallen soul to fathom the clear, pure mind of a great devotee. I went on and cleaned the whole apartment, but I have often thought about that picture.

It still bewilders me, but then in Krishna Consciousness you often come across something that makes the mind stagger, that goes beyond the understanding, where you have to stop trying to grasp it and simply bask in the sweetness of it. And the more you try to penetrate that sweetness and get to the essence, the deeper it goes. You cannot find the beginning, you cannot find the end, and you begin to understand the nature of infinity, not something theoretical, not an equation on a blackboard, but something wonderfully alive and shining, a quality of Krishna the unfathomable.

The next day, when Sridham came back from San Francisco, my housekeeping was rewarded.

“The house looks great,” said Sridham. “Come on. Let’s go to the airport and pick up Prabhupada.” On the drive back from the airport, Prabhupada and I talked about real estate values and whether the astronauts had really gone to the moon. From the beginning, Prabhupada had insisted that no one had gone there. I knew Prabhupada was right, but still I could not give up the awe I had felt that night when I looked at the full moon and imagined the Americans walking there. I could not give up the pleasure of being swept along in the current of popular enthusiasm about the so-called space age.

“Even if they did go,” I said, trying to find a middle ground, “what would be the use?” But I knew Prabhupada was right. There was no “even if.” I had to get a grip on my feelings.

After some weeks I went to San Francisco to try for better luck in job-hunting. I stayed at the temple, and when I mentioned that Prabhupada had once said not to sleep in front of holy pictures, one devotee started taking all the pictures down, as the devotees slept everywhere. “This cannot be,” I said. “Don’t take them down. I’ll write to Prabhupada.” I wrote a letter to Prabhupada’s secretary and received a quick reply. Since we have so many pictures of Krishna, the letter said, we cannot avoid sleeping in front of them, but we must not be naked or speak nonsense in front of pictures.

I found no job in San Francisco either, but I had almost expected as much. San Francisco was not a major recording center. Then I got a letter from Prabhupada. “I think there is a great plan of Krishna,” the letter said, “that you are meant for preaching a hundred percent. So if you do not find a job, you can go to Paris where there is contemplation of opening a center.” I had mentioned Paris in a letter to Prabhupada about a year earlier. I knew the language and I knew the city, having once served on an American army base in France. I thought about the city and how much I had enjoyed it. Some of my old friends might even help me get started there, so as soon as I got the letter from Prabhupada I went to Los Angeles and met with him. “We must have a big, beautiful temple in Paris,” he told me, “because it is one of the world’s most important cities.” I was going to Paris with Henri, a young French-Canadian man who had just joined. He had been in Mexico, looking for truth in psychedelic mushrooms, when someone gave him a copy of Back to Godhead magazine with a cover painting of Lord Caitanya and His devotees. “Dancing in Ecstasy,” said the title. Henri wanted to learn more about dancing in ecstasy. He hitchhiked up to California, and his last ride dropped him off at the front door of the Los Angeles temple. He was soon initiated and given the name Hanuman Das.

Prabhupada lived in a bungalow in Hollywood, in the north of Los Angeles. All the neighboring houses had neatly trimmed lawns, but at Prabhupada’s house the grass was growing tall. I asked Prabhupada’s servant about it. “You have given the animals a home,” Prabhupada had told him.

“Do not take it away.” Although Prabhupada was focused on a worldwide institution for the benefit of all humanity, he was personal and caring, even down to the smallest intimacy, as with the picture of the disciples on his altar and in this case, the tiny insects in his front lawn. But did anyone really care what tragedy, what heartbreak these little creatures would feel if their nests and dens and lairs were suddenly cut down by the blades of a lawn mower? Who cared what a little bug felt anyway? But now I saw that Prabhupada cared. He had the perfect vision described in the Bhagavad- gita, that within the body of every living being, not just a human being but even a little insect, even a blade of grass, lived a complete person, someone just like me, being dragged through the different species of life, sometimes feeling pleasure, sometimes pain. I suddenly understood that I wanted this vision too. I wanted to be part of a movement that brought compassion into this hard world.

One morning, shortly before leaving for Paris, Hanuman and I went on a morning walk with Prabhupada. I mentioned to Prabhupada that on the street we got arguments from Christians.

“Tell me what they say,” said Prabhupada, “and I will give you the answers.” “They say ‘Jesus’ is the only name,” I said.

“They are nonsense,” said Prabhupada. “Then I say ‘Krishna’ is the only name.” “They ask why Jesus did not mention the name ‘Krishna.’” “Do you know my father’s name?” asked Prabhupada “No.” “I have not told you my father’s name, but does that mean my father does not have a name?” “When you develop some intelligence,” Prabhupada continued “you will be able to answer these questions yourself.” In my mind I smiled. I knew it was a principle in spiritual life, followed even by great sages and wise men, even by Lord Chaitanya Himself, to consider oneself a fool in the presence of the spiritual master. Real knowledge cannot enter a heart that thinks itself learned.

Prabhupada also talked about Jesus himself. “His character was sublime. He was not a drunkard or a woman-hunter.” We talked about the name “Christ,” not actually Jesus’ name but added later. It comes from the Greek christos, “the anointed one.” As christos sounds a little like “Krishna,” Prabhupada spoke of a linguistic relation. “In India there are people who call Krishna ‘Krishta.’ Christos means anointed, and Krishna has tilak.” The subject turned to loving God. Love, said Prabhupada, must be given voluntarily. It cannot be forced. Prabhupada stopped walking and put his hands on my shoulders. “You cannot say, ‘You must love me! You must love me!’” he said, shaking my shoulders to show the force that cannot be used. It was a rare privilege to be touched by Prabhupada, and with such a powerful grip. I felt encouraged, reassured of the eternal bond between my master and me, reassured that Prabhupada cared about me the way he cared about the disciples on his altar, reassured that I could meet whatever challenges might lie ahead in France. (Prabhupada, although small-framed, was so strong I was sure he could have picked me up and thrown me into the ocean, some miles away.) Soon, a married couple came down from Berkeley, near San Francisco, to join us on our way to Paris, a young Frenchman named Serge and his American wife, Gerri. They were soon initiated as Suridas and Jatila Devi Dasi. Jatila found a cheap flight from San Francisco to London. Then it would just be an overnight train ride to Paris. I wrote to Mukunda in London to tell him we were coming, and he wrote back telling me where to go on arriving.

## Chapter 22 - London and Paris

When we arrived in London we took a bus, as requested in Mukunda’s letter, to Tittenhurst, the country estate of the Beatle John Lennon, where the devotees were living in some houses at one end of the large property. Prabhupada was coming soon, so we decided to wait instead of immediately going to France. The next day the sunny weather turned into a gloomy drizzle.

As I wandered around one afternoon, I came upon an old newspaper article on the floor of an empty room. The article spoke about the brahmastra weapon mentioned in the ancient epic Mahabharata. (The Bhagavad-gita is eighteen chapters of the Mahabharata.) Prabhupada also mentions the brahmastra in his books, describing it, astonishingly, as a nuclear weapon. At first I had my doubts. Could the brahmastra, however powerful, really be called nuclear? Maybe Prabhupada was stretching the comparison.

But now I was kneeling on the floor, reading the same thing in a newspaper. The trees were charred from the tremendous heat, and people exposed to the radiation lost their hair and fingernails.

What else besides a nuclear weapon could do that? But, continued the article, how could a book written five thousand years ago talk about nuclear weapons? I saw proof here, and from a materialist, that the Vedic civilization already knew about principles that our scientists today give credit to themselves for discovering.

After a few days, Prabhupada came from Germany and stayed at the property. But before I had a chance to speak with Prabhupada, I met with Hayagriva, who had come with him but this time with a shaved head and sikha (ponytail) and no beard.

“Where’s your beard?” I asked him.

Hayagriva laughed. “My beard? I can’t grow my beard again after what Prabhupada said.” “What did he say?” “He said, ‘You look like a demigod. Before this, you looked like a demon. The demigod was covered by the demon.’” “First, I just shaved my head,” Hayagriva continued. “I had a sikha and kept my beard. When Prabhupada saw me he laughed. He said Muslims do that.” One day as Prabhupada met with the disciples in his room, he talked about the drizzly weather.

“It says in the Vedas that a place where the sun does not shine is a condemned place.” I remembered walking on the street with Prabhupada on a sunny afternoon that first summer in New York when Prabhupada turned to me. “The sun does not shine like this in Europe, does it?” “The same as here,” I said. But after a week of constant drizzle in dreary old England, I too was beginning to wonder.

But the drizzle did not dampen the spirits of two-year-old Saraswati, the daughter of Malati devi dasi and Shyamasundar das from the old San Francisco temple. All eyes were on her when she danced during a kirtan in Prabhupada’s room, holding up her arms and turning around and around.

“Do not think the dancing is in vain,” Prabhupada said afterwards.

And in fact a couple of years later in Paris I would hear how Saraswati’s dancing melted the heart of a powerful yogi. One autumn evening I was sitting with Hanuman, the young Canadian, around a table in a devotee-owned restaurant with a few others. Hanuman had just come back from India and was telling us how he had met the yogi at a cultural program where the devotees had been invited chant.

“Did you preach to him?” someone asked.

“Did you debate with him?” asked another.

Debate? Previously in India proponents of different schools debated to prove the superiority of their own path. I thought of Lord Chaitanya’s defeating a great scholar five hundred years ago on the bank of the Ganges. The scholar, when challenged, immediately composed a Sanskrit poem of one hundred verses in praise of the Ganges and recited it “like the blowing wind,” an extraordinary feat considering the intricacies of the Sanskrit language. But without a moment’s hesitation the Lord defeated the scholar by pointing out a subtle error in the sixty-fourth verse.

“Did you defeat him?” someone asked.

“You cannot defeat these yogis,” said Hanuman. “They know all the scriptures. They start studying Sanskrit when they are five.” I nodded.

“But Saraswati defeated him,” Hanuman continued. “When he saw this little girl dancing in kirtan, he started to cry.” We all understood Saraswati’s victory. Most yoga methods require years of study, but even a small child could sing and dance in Krishna Consciousness.

My mind went back to this drizzly day in England: “Do not think the dancing is in vain.” Prabhupada was reminding us that devotional service endears the performer to Krishna, even something as simple as dancing by a two-year-old. Lord Krishna is kind to His devotees, say the scriptures, always grateful and magnanimous. “Do not think Krishna is some ungrateful man,” said Prabhupada on another occasion.

“But why is Krishna special?” one may argue. “After all, who wouldn’t be charmed by the dancing of a little girl?” True enough, but who would feel eternally grateful for it? Krishna even feels grateful for service offered by an enemy as a ploy to kill Him. The demoness Putana smeared poison on her nipples to kill baby Krishna, but because she offered Him her milk, even with evil intent, He accepted her as His mother. She even attained the spiritual world.

“You should appreciate whatever the devotees do,” Prabhupada once told a temple manager.

And I saw that Prabhupada followed his own advice. His letters to his disciples were so enlivening that I felt encouraged just by reading what he wrote to other people. The road to Krishna Consciousness can become hard to navigate at times, especially when the material desires that you have been trying to control suddenly gang up on you and overtake you. You begin to doubt your own sincerity and your ability to reach the goal. You don’t know where to go, whom to turn to. But Prabhupada’s letters could always set your mind at ease and get you started in the right direction again. It is true that today more disciples are beginning to follow Prabhupada’s example of encouragement, but it wasn’t always that way.

I remember a boy, about eleven, at the New Vrindaban community in West Virgina. Everybody said he was a bad kid. He wasn’t coming when I started teaching class to the boys, but after a few days, when he showed up, I dreaded the worst, especially when he pulled out a pocket knife and started playing with it. But this “bad kid” turned out to be one of the nicest boys in the class. His only problem was a short attention span, but that’s no crime. I should have said something. I should have told him, “You’re not a bad kid.” I should have let him know someone thought well of him. What must it be like — people always telling you you’re a bad kid? What heavy feeling was he carrying around? Some years later, I saw his sister, now grown up, in Mayapur.

“How’s your brother doing?” I asked her.

“Not well at all,” she said.

I have mentally kicked myself many times for not helping this kid, for not saying the words that might have changed his life, that might have eased his burden.

And I have to admit that there were times in the early days in Paris when I unfairly criticized Hanuman and found fault with the efforts he made. I don’t know where he is now or what has become of him, but maybe some day I will have a chance to tell him I’m sorry.

On that drizzly day in England, when I watched Saraswati dancing, someone handed me a photo of the painting that had inspired me on Krishna’s Birthday — Krishna surrounded by clouds. I was happy to see it again, but some people had doubts about it. Where do the scriptures say anything about Krishna looking out from behind clouds? The next day I showed the photo to Prabhupada.

“Is this all right?” I asked.

“Yes,” said Prabhupada.

“This is the picture that brought me back to Krishna Consciousness,” I said.

“Krishna can appear anywhere,” said Prabhupada.

Shyamasundar, little Saraswati’s father, was hard at work remodeling the temple in London. He had no time to meditate. “I went to see Prabhupada,” he said, “and I told him I’m so busy working on the temple that I don’t have time to think of Krishna, and Prabhupada said, ‘You are building a temple for Krishna. How can you say you are not thinking of Him?’” Soon Hanuman, Suridas, and I left for Paris. Suridas’s wife, Jatila, would come later. It was now September 1969, three years since my initiation.

One of the first things we did in Paris was to go hear a lecture by an Indian swami, already popular in the United States. Maybe we would meet people interested in Indian spirituality. But I was astounded to hear a lecture without a word about Krishna or the Bhagavad-gita. Worse yet, the swami compared chanting a mantra to smearing dirt over dirt. “When you bathe,” he said, “you cover the dirt with another kind of dirt, called soap. Then you rinse away both kinds of dirt. No need for a mantra when you have attained the truth.” I cringed. Not only did he ignore the Bhagavad-gita but he outright contradicted it. The repetitive chanting of a mantra, says the Gita, is no different from Krishna Himself. Great devotees, even Lord Chaitanya, chanted the Hare Krishna mantra constantly.

Then the swami spoke at length about the organs of the body, and when the French translator, a nervous woman in her forties, stumbled over the word for “thymus,” Suridas turned to me. “We are so fortunate,” he said. I could only nod in agreement. I thought about Second Avenue and how I had argued with Prabhupada about his refusal to compromise. Now I understood. I thought about the poor listeners in the audience, trying to attain enlightenment through the thymus. After my first day with Prabhupada I had learned more than this so-called teacher could teach in a million years. This poor swami couldn’t even tell a mantra from a bar of soap.

But we were dressed as devotees, and when we went to meet the swami after the lecture, he smiled and greeted us with the Hare Krishna mantra, so the evening was not a complete loss.

Suridas and I were sitting outdoors at a sidewalk cafī one evening, when a friend of Suridas’s saw him and came up to us. I thought he was a homeless wanderer on the street, maybe mentally ill.

He was young and had nice features, but he was disheveled with a dazed look. Later Suridas told me that the young man had once taken a teaspoonful of pure LSD hoping to achieve a permanent high (a normal dose was one drop). I had once thought I wanted a permanent LSD high. Now I was glad it never happened.

After the three of us arrived in Paris, we stayed with a friend of Suridas’s until Suridas moved in with an aunt. Hanuman and I were left wandering on the street, and with almost no money. When we left Los Angeles for Paris, we went through San Francisco, where the devotees were supposed to give us two hundred dollars to help us get started in Europe, but unfortunately, they gave it to us in the form of a heavy box of coins. It was inconvenient but better than nothing till we discovered in London that banks only wanted paper money. So Hanuman and I were left with only whatever paper money we had managed to bring, and it wasn’t much. I left the box of coins in England.

Hanuman and I could not afford even the cheapest hotel, so I put our suitcases in paid storage at a train station and we lived day and night on the street. Hanuman carried a sleeping bag with him and slept under the bridges with the bums at night, but I was afraid of being picked up by the police and thrown out of the country. It was just a year and some months after the student riots of May 1968, and the police were still edgy. A foreigner walking on the street at night would seem normal, but not a foreigner sleeping under a bridge. I was sometimes stopped by the police, but my papers were in order, so I never had any trouble. Hanuman was frequently picked up, and I would hear the policemen joking about his Canadian accent and his romantic-sounding family name — Jolicoeur (zho-lee- CURR). It means pretty heart. “Hey! It’s Jolicoeur!” But they never gave him any trouble.

While Hanuman slept under the bridges — the Hotel of the Beautiful Star, as the French put it — I strolled along the sidewalks in the swinging St. Germain neighborhood, stopping people and asking whether they could put me up for the night. “The young people all talk about San Francisco,” Suridas told me, “so say you have just come from there.” Sometimes I found a place, sometimes not. If I was still on the street very late, I might run into a friendly young Nigerian man, always dressed in a suit and tie, and he would treat me to a cup of hot milk in some cafī. If I stayed up all night, I went to a park in the early morning and slept sitting on a bench.

One chilly morning my faith in Prabhupada got another boost, this time from, of all places, a Roman Catholic church. I took refuge from the cold in the Church of St. Sulpice, famous for the beauty of the morning sunlight coming through the windows. I walked around a bit inside the church then sat down. I looked up. High on a wall was a beautiful white relief of the Virgin Mary, covered with black dust.

My mind went back a year or so to Montreal. The forms of Krishna on the altar, Prabhupada was telling us, are not dead statues. They are Krishna Himself. Our material eyes see only wood and stone, so Krishna may come to us looking like wood or stone to let us see Him and serve Him, but it is Krishna nonetheless. And the same applies to great devotees like Jesus or the Virgin Mary. I looked again at Mary covered with dust, sadly neglected in her own church by her own followers.

I remembered a Christian family giving me a ride to a bus station near Los Angeles, their Bible lying on the floor of the car. “Why do you put your Bible on the floor?” I said. “It’s a holy book.” “Not the ink and paper,” they said.

Their answer reminded me of the time Hayagriva, sitting on the floor, pushed a typewriter away from him with his leg. “Do not touch that with your leg,” Prabhupada told him. “It is a holy thing.” And now here I was, in the Church of Saint Sulpice, looking at the Virgin Mary covered with dust.

What a shame! As I walked out of the church into the chilly morning, I said a little prayer of thanks to Krishna for giving me Prabhupada.

## Chapter 23 - Getting Started in Paris

Suridas’s wife, Jatila, came to Paris and soon found a place for our Sunday programs: The American Center for Students and Artists, once a social club for well-to-do Americans but now a hangout where young people of all nationalities came to sit around or play music or look for traveling partners. It was a perfect place for us to get started. There was just one problem. One of us had to become a member, but the membership fee of five francs, though only the equivalent of a dollar, was more than we could afford. I paid the fee with an hour’s work in the office.

We held the programs in the basement of the center, in a cabaret run by an Armenian-American named Hampar. Every Sunday afternoon when we went there to prepare things, we found a mess, the remnants of Saturday’s Armenian Night, where Hampar sang and played the oud, a Middle-Eastern stringed instrument. (On the Govindam recording, played every morning in ISKCON temples, it’s Hampar playing those little musical frills on his oud.) So we cleaned up the place. About thirty people came each time and chanted with us, heard about Krishna, and had prasada, fruit salad with yogurt. Afterwards we cleaned up again. Some years later I talked about those days with Hampar. “I was always glad when you guys came,” he told me, “because you cleaned up the place.” But we had simply followed Prabhupada’s advice: “Cleanliness is next to godliness.” It worked.

The American Center hired me to work regularly in the office and to play the records for the Saturday night dance in the auditorium. Now Hanuman and I could afford to live in a small hotel.

After a few weeks, though, I had to stop going to the dance, as they had started projecting pornographic photos on a large screen. I managed not to look, to my own amazement, but I knew I would give in sooner or later.

Our neighbor at the hotel turned out to be Burton Greene, the pianist who had invited us to his concert at Town Hall in New York. Burton wanted to make a salad for us one night, and I told him, “No onions, garlic, or vinegar.” But Burton, well-intentioned and gentle as he was, put leeks in the salad, not knowing they were a kind of onion. I took only a few bites before I noticed, but it was enough. Throughout the evening I felt the taste of onions running through my blood. I didn’t like it. It felt dirty.

Before I met Prabhupada I never worried about what I ate. When Prabhupada said not to eat meat, fish, or eggs, I took it as a ritual, part of the Eastern tradition. I didn’t mind anyway, since the prasada Prabhupada was giving us tasted so good. Onions, garlic, and vinegar? Easy to give up if I had to. But after I ate the leeks that night, I realized it was more than a ritual. It was self-purification.

Suridas was working on a job passing out advertising when he noticed a co-worker, a girl named Joelle (zho-ELL), carrying a book on Zen Buddhism. “If you are interested in spiritual things,” Suridas told her, “we have a yoga program every Sunday.” Joelle came the next Sunday with two girlfriends, Monique and Evelyne. A nineteen-year-old American boy, Josh, started coming and immediately took to Krishna Consciousness. “What have you done to Josh?” people would ask me.

“He’s walking around with stars in his eyes.” Soon Joelle and Monique went to London. They wanted to try living in a temple, but all we had in Paris was a weekly meeting.

Joelle recalls: “Prabhupada was there, and he was eager to meet the first devotees from France, so they brought us to see him. He asked us, ‘Can you type?’ “I was shocked: ‘What! A spiritual master wants to know if we can type?’ I couldn’t imagine why a spiritual master cared about typing.

“One evening Prabhupada became very angry at an Indian man and was yelling at him. Monique and I thought, ‘That’s it! Prabhupada cannot control his senses,’ so we decided to leave the next day.

We didn’t understand what Prabhupad was saying because he yelled at the man in Hindi. The next day, during class, Prabhupada said he had asked the man to teach the devotees because the man knew Sanskrit, but Prabhupada found out that the man was teaching them impersonalist philosophy. A devotee is non-violent and controls his senses, Prabhupad explained, but in some situations, like protecting other devotees, he can use anger, just as Krishna urged Arjuna to become angry and fight for the sake of protecting the world from evil leaders. So we stayed.” Soon Monique came to Paris to get the third friend, Evelyne, and take her to the temple in London. I felt sad seeing the face of Evelyne’s widowed mother as she watched her young daughter leave home. Then one day as I was at my job in the office of the American Center, a letter came for me. It was from the three girls. Joelle was now Jyotirmayi Devi Dasi, Monique was Mandakini Devi Dasi, and Evelyne was Ilavati Devi Dasi. Hampar and Josh also went to London. Josh became Yogesvara Das, and Hampar became Harivilas Das, now a member of the governing board of ISKCON.

In December, Hanuman and I went to London for a visit, but we traveled separately. When I went to the temple at Bury Place, Malati opened the door. “Prabhupada is here!” she said, “Right in this building!” I went up to the top floor to see Prabhupada. “Go see the deities,” he told me. I went downstairs to see the large marble deities I had heard about, but the altar was closed, so I went back upstairs to see Prabhupada. While we were talking, Hanuman came in, and a tear jumped out of his eye. “He is a sincere boy,” Prabhupada said to me. “Take care of him.” “What time does the sun come up in Paris?” Prabhupada asked.

“About 7:30,” I said, but I wasn’t sure. I did not want to mention that I had been sleeping late.

Again Prabhupada knew just the thing to say. I decided I had better start getting up early.

“Here it is about eight o’clock,” said Prabhupada. “Paris is farther south.” Then Srila Prabhupada mentioned that he had been asked to give a lecture series in Philadelphia.

“The winter will be cold in Philadelphia,” I said.

“I do not mind the winter if I have a good bathroom,” said Prabhupada, “but the bathrooms in Europe are not good.” I knew what Prabhupada meant. The bathrooms in Europe at the time were generally not as well furnished as their American counterparts, often drafty and somewhat shabby, more like an afterthought than part of the house itself. I didn’t like them either.

Later that day I saw the beautiful deities, Sri Sri Radha-Londonisvara (the Lord of London), almost lifesize and carved in white marble. These large deities marked the beginning of a new phase in our worship. The small brass deities already being worshiped in our temples were just as powerful, just as beautiful, just as much a manifestation of Krishna Himself, but the large deities could be seen even from the back of a crowded room.

That evening I went back to Paris. Hanuman returned soon afterwards.

I found some surprise visitors one Sunday at the American Center. Tamal Krishna Das from Los Angeles was traveling in Europe with his new American wife, Madri Devi Dasi, and had come to Paris to get us organized. With his help, we finally printed a French magazine, but that wasn’t enough.

He also had another project in mind. He wanted to find a wife for me. “She should be French,” he said, “maybe one of the French girls in London. I think Ilavati would be the right one for you. She’s very neat and clean, and she’ll take good care of you.” I took his word for it. I had only met Ilavati once or twice and only for brief periods. “Okay,” I said. “Call London and see if she agrees.” Ilavati recalls: “Guru Das, the temple president, called me into his office. ‘Umapati wants to marry you,’ he said. And I thought, ‘Well, he’s an old devotee — three years. It might not be a bad idea.’” The day after talking with Tamal Krishna, I took a bus to the house in the suburbs where Ilavati had lived with her mother and older brother, Jean-Pierre, to tell them we were getting married.

Before Ilavati went to London she had supported the family by working at a nearby grocery store. Her father had committed suicide a few years earlier, stuck a gun in his mouth and pulled the trigger, and her brother had his own psychological problems and could not work. After Ilavati left, the family had to scrape by on meager funds from here and there.

The house was in disrepair. Wide strips of wallpaper were peeling off the walls in many places, the bottom half still attached, the top half curling downward. Ilavati’s mother, Simone, did her best to clean away the dust that gathered between the loose wallpaper and the walls, but all she had to work with was an old mop, so the house had a faint smell of dust.

While Ilavati lived in London, her mother sometimes invited the devotees to her house for lunch. First we had to shoo the cat off the dining room table. Then we had kirtan. Simone folded her hands and sang the prayers with us in a loud clear soprano voice. During lunch, the dog, a brown spaniel, joined us to beg for scraps. Sometimes Ilavati’s brother joined us, but he mostly stayed in his room.

Ilavati came back to Paris the following Saturday, and we were married the next evening at the American Center in a large room on the top floor. We had a good crowd. Everybody was curious to see a Hare Krishna wedding, people even standing on the piano to watch Tamal Krishna light the sacrificial fire. Then as he recited the mantras, ladling ghee onto the fire to keep it burning, Ilavati and I fed the fire by throwing in pinches of grain. A couple of times the fire grew so big he had to ladle water onto it to keep it from becoming dangerous.

After the ceremony, Ilavati and I were the last ones to leave. As we walked down the quiet lamplit street, I saw a man with no pants following us and trying to get close till I shouted at him and he ran away. In a few minutes we got to our hotel. We went to our room, then upstairs to see Tamal Krishna and Madri. When we got back to our room, the lock was jammed. No locksmith would come at that late hour, but the hotel had an empty room where we could spend the night.

Now, at last, I was alone in a hotel room with my beautiful young bride on our wedding night.

What would happen next? “Do you know the rules for sex in Krishna Consciousness?” I asked her.

“No,” she said.

I explained that sex was only for having a child, and the fertile night was calculated according to the woman’s cycle. We were not going to have sex that night. “You’re not disappointed, are you?” I asked.

“No,” she said softly.

Disappointed? Hardly. She was relieved, as she told me years later. She would have done the needful, but she did not really want sex with a man she hardly knew, even if he happened to be her husband.

The next morning we told the others about the naked man and the jammed lock. Tamal Krishna reminded us about having to pour water on the fire. He smiled. “When things like that happen on a wedding night,” he said, “it’s auspicious.” And auspicous it was. Ilavati and I stayed married until I reached fifty, and then Ilavati urged me to accept the order of sannyasa although that meant giving up her company, renouncing family life to live as a wandering monk, devoted completely to meditation and preaching. Women generally remain in a sheltered situation.

During our marriage we sometimes talked about how nice it was to have gotten married the right way, never together until the wedding night. It was something special between us, something sweet, something we got from Prabhupada. I married a girl I hardly knew, a girl I had only met a few times and never alone. I wanted to show to others, and to myself as well, that a marriage based not on romance but on intelligence, determination, and Krishna consciousness would succeed.

The Paris community started to grow, and we were having regular harinamas, chanting on the street. Our favorite nighttime spot was in front of a cafī at a busy intersection on the Boulevard Montparnasse, next to a grimy fire eater who sometimes blew a bit of fire our way. One night a young American man named Lou joined us. I had often run into him at the American center, and he always asked me for a magazine. “Tomorrow for sure,” I promised each time but then forgot. I could not imagine that this debonair young man in flashy clothes was interested in Krishna consciousness, but now here he was chanting with us. He started coming regularly and soon invited the devotees to move into his apartment. He became initiated as Locanananda Das \[lo-chen-AHN-ahn-da\].

But a storm was brewing that would send Ilavati and me scurrying for shelter in New York.

One day I went to Tamal Krishna’s hotel room to speak about an idea I had. Organizationally, I was considered the head of the Paris group, the temple president.

“I think Suridasa should be the temple president,” I said. “It would be better to have a French president for France.” Tamal Krishna nodded. “Let me think about it.” I discussed it with him because he was one of the main managers of ISKCON, but he hesitated because Prabhupada himself had sent me to Paris. Finally he wrote to Prabhupada and Prabhupada agreed. Now Suridas was the head of the group, but Tamal Krishna still took care of the management.

He had an imposing, charismatic presence, and people naturally followed him and listened to him.

Then two problems arose. First, Ilavati and I could not get a legal marriage. To marry a French person, a foreigner had to work his way through an endless maze of bureaucracy and fees, dealing with people who seemed more like robots, their talk sounding like a recorded message. But we had to get married. The problem would become serious if we were to have children.

The storm that drove us out, though, was the second problem: internal politics. After Tamal Krishna left, the people would not listen to me anymore because I no longer had a position. It should have been obvious that Tamal Krishna and I were good friends. We were always laughing and joking together, but now the devotees were saying that Tamal Krishna had kicked me out of my position because I was incompetent. They would not listen to Suridas either because he was my friend.

Anger came crashing down on me. Those arrogant little twerps! Who the hell did they think they were! They even called me a leech. But what did I ever take from them? Was I incompetent? Well, maybe. No charisma? Maybe so. But when I came, they had nothing, and I gave them Krishna.

Hanuman surely would have been a peacemaker had he not left for India. Locanananda stayed neutral.

But now I can see that the problem was my own mistake. Being a new devotee myself at the time, not even four years, I did not understand the patience needed for taking care of others, especially new arrivals. I should have given them time. People need time to take in the great volume of knowledge that comes with Krishna consciousness, much of it going against ideas they hold dear. It doesn’t happen in a day. I have seen devotees who even after many years still favored Darwin’s theory over the Srimad Bhagavatam. It takes time to admit in our hearts that we know nothing, absolute zero, but this realization is the door where spiritual knowledge comes in, carrying the perfect science and logic of the scriptures. But it takes time. I should have known that time would straighten out the situation. And it did. A few years later, we were all friends again.

But I did not wait. I wrote to my parents: if they would send me money for tickets I would bring my new wife to meet them. I had maintained contact with my parents the whole time though I had said nothing about Krishna. At the beginning I had written that I was studying with a teacher from India, but by now the Hare Krishna movement was well known, often from sensational news reports about poor innocent young people being imprisoned in temples and brainwashed by constant haranguing, although I never saw such practices in any of our temples, and people were always free to leave. Rising early in the morning, which used to be considered healthful and virtuous, was now a form of torture, and the vegetarian diet was supposed to make people weak-willed and easy to dominate.

Then a new breed of terror-monger arrived. They called themselves deprogrammers, and for a hefty fee from some fine, flag-waving, upstanding all-American parents, they would kidnap their teenage child from a temple, hold the child against his will, usually in some remote location, and re- indoctrinate him into standard American values by constant haranguing and sleep deprivation or starvation. Most of the time it did not work.

“Don’t worry, Wally,” said a cousin passing through Paris on vacation. “The whole family knows you’re a Hare Krishna.” I wondered what they were imagining.

My parents sent me money, and in January of 1971 Ilavati and I left Paris for New York, a sad ending to what had been a happy beginning. “The head of a tiger but the tail of a snake,” goes an old Chinese saying.

In the years that followed people sometimes asked me about the beginning, how I managed to perform the austerities — walking on the street at night stopping young people and asking if they could put me up, sometimes getting a place, sometimes walking till morning and then sleeping sitting on a park bench or in the subway while Hanuman slept under the bridges with the bums, sometimes the two of us just eating bread and peanuts for lunch. But I never felt any inconvenience. The pleasure of fighting on the front line, breaking new ground for Prabhupada, waking people up to Krishna consciousness — it far outweighs any external discomfort. It defeats any other pleasure this world can offer.

Those were wonderful days. I would do it again in a minute, and a million times over.

## Chapter 24 - Back to the United States

As soon as we arrived in New York, we went to the temple, a large, spacious, three-story house in Brooklyn Heights, where the president, my old friend Bhavananda Das, the interior decorator who painted the Los Angeles temple, welcomed us and offered us a room. A few days later we had our legal marriage ceremony at the Salvation Army. Oh good old America! No problem. No hassle. So easy when you were three thousand miles away from that hopeless tangle, that overturned bowl of spaghetti called European bureaucracy. Suridas and Jatila also came to New York, moved into the temple, and had a legal marriage.

Then news came that Prabhupada was displeased with me for leaving Paris. I had not thought of that. I suddenly felt this emptiness in the pit of my stomach that I get when I realize I have done something I should not have. But the group in Paris, small as it was, had become so riddled with politics that I thought everyone would be happier if I left. Still, why bother Prabhupada with politics? So I wrote Prabhupada a letter explaining that I had come to America to get a legal marriage and let my parents meet my wife. If Prabhupada wanted me in Paris, I would go back; otherwise, I had no plans.

Now we needed money, but my career in recording was finished. No one would hire someone like me who had quit so many times. Suridas and Jatila had an idea. Life would be easier in San Francisco. First Ilavati and I went to Chicago, where Mom and Dad cordially received us in their new suburban house. They invited relatives to come see us, and we talked a little about Krishna Consciousness, but they asked mostly about the difference between Krishna Consciousness and Buddhism because a cousin was studying Zen.

In San Francisco, I found a temporary job copying data, letter by letter, into little squares to be read by an optical computer reader, and Ilavati and I moved into an apartment (it really was cheaper) in the Haight-Ashbury district, at 635 Ashbury, where devotees had once lived and where Shyamasundar had carved the deities of Jagannatha, Baladeva, and Subhadra. But now we were three thousand miles farther from Paris. The temple had moved to the Mission district, near my job, and one day as I was leaving work, I saw Ilavati at the street corner.

“Come quick!” she shouted. “Prabhupada is at the temple!” We hurried over and arrived in time to find Prabhupada in the lobby.

“Are you working?” he asked me.

“Yes.” “Are you giving fifty percent?” “No.” “Are you spending it all on household life?” he said.

“I don’t make very much.” “You must still give fifty percent,” he said.

So I started giving half of my earnings, but as hardly enough remained even for food and rent, I stopped. The real problem, though, was that I did not understand Prabhupada’s concern for me. My spiritual master was trying to pull me out of the material world. Where was my service to Krishna now, if I was only working and eating? (The standard of fifty percent comes from the example of Sri Rupa Goswami, one of Lord Chaitanya’s closest followers, who gave half of his accumulated wealth for the service of the devotees.) Soon it was time to take Jagannath, Baladeva, and Subhadra on their yearly procession through Golden Gate Park. As Ilavati and I watched Prabhupada eat the lunch that was served, Ilavati turned to me. “Prabhupada always seems to be having a good time,” she said.

“You know,” she said on another day, “you were the first person who told me Prabhupada was nice.” I knew what she meant. Certain managers and leaders of ISKCON often portrayed Prabhupada in a harsh light to justify their own rough dealings with others.

Now I had a job, we had an apartment — one of those big, light, airy San Francisco apartments with large windows — and we were visiting the temple. Everything should have been all right. But no, something else was bothering us. Devotees from other places would visit San Francisco, telling stories about children being beaten for small offenses. Men, some of them leaders, would brag about beating their wives. And of course, it was all because “Prabhupada said...” So I decided to write to Prabhupada, who by this time had gone to Los Angeles, but I did something stupid (again). Instead of simply asking Prabhupada his opinion, I wrote the letter as a criticism of ISKCON. “I do not understand this movement,” I began.

Prabhupada replied quickly. “Men who beat women and children are not an example,” he wrote, “so why are you giving them as an example?” The letter was in a stern tone. Why was I criticizing others when I was doing nothing myself? “You say you do not understand this movement,” the letter ended, “but if you do not understand after so many years, when will you understand? The whole movement is meant for service.” I should have seen Prabhupada’s kindness in taking the time to write, urging me to stop criticizing and get back into devotional service. Not only that, but he gave me the answer I wanted: no one should beat women and children. Still I felt disappointed. Instead of getting back into devotional service, I retreated. I felt that something was wrong in my life. Yes, and Prabhupada was telling me what it was, but I looked for something else. Maybe I needed something besides Krishna consciousness. But Prabhupada had been right when he told me on Second Avenue, “You cannot get away from us.” Ilavati and I went to see Rayarama, one of the first devotees from 26 Second Avenue, long departed from Krishna consciousness and living nearby with some hippie friends. But he still had pictures of Krishna in his room. “You can’t forget how nice it is to love Radha and Krishna,” he told us. At home, as I leafed through some books he had lent us, I was surprised to see a book about Tibetan Buddhism written by someone named Lama Govinda. A Tibetan Buddhist with a devotee name like Govinda? “Everything in these books comes originally from the Vedas,” Ilavati said, “so why not just go to the Vedas?” We walked over to the temple every Sunday, and Ilavati chanted sixteen rounds on her beads every day, as she did throughout our marriage.

In spite of all my traveling, I still had the deities of Jagannath, Baladeva, and Subhadra that I had bought in Montreal. I gave them to Chandravali Devi Dasi, one of the original San Francisco devotees, and she took them to Mexico City, where she and her husband, Chitsukhananda, had opened a temple. I never expected to see the deities again, and I eventually forgot about them. But years later I visited the temple in Mexico City, and I suddenly recognized them, or rather, they recognized me first and their innocent smiles lit up my remembrance. It was a delight to revive my relationship with them. I thought about what Prabhupada had taught us, how we forget our relationship with Krishna when we come to the material world, but Krishna remembers.

Ilavati and I were visiting Suridas and Jatila one day when a musician, a friend of their neighbor’s, invited us to the nightclub where he would be playing. But when I opened the door to the club that night, I thought I had stumbled into the wrong world, a world made completely of sin, like a painting of hell in a Christian book. True, the atmosphere was calm, even sedate, just a few people sitting around in the dim light while a young man on stage played a guitar and sang. But it was sin, a turning away from Krishna. The feeling was real and solid, more concrete than the nightclub itself.

Sin, I finally realized, did not have to be wild and orgiastic. I understood, at long last, that little old ladies lifting up their teacups, pinkies out, were also sinful because they were looking away from Krishna. I talked about it the next day with Ilavati. She had had the same vision.

Now I knew I would never go far from Prabhupada, and Krishna kept reminding me. When I went to my job downtown every day, I walked by a large billboard showing the deities in London, Radha-Londonisvara, to promote a record album made by the London devotees. (The Govindam recording played in ISKCON temples every morning is from that album.) Then, as I turned down a side street, I was again reminded of Krishna by an advertisement showing a boy’s face with jewels, a turban, and eye shadow.

When my temporary job came to an end, I went to another temporary-job agency. “Are you a Hare Krishna?” asked the girl at the reception desk.

I smiled. “Yes. How did you know?” “You look like a Hare Krishna.” It was another reminder that I could not go far from Krishna.

The agency found me a job that, unknown to any of us, would lead me back to the lotus feet of Prabhupada. It was at a radio station, KFRC, that had just hired a computer-accounting firm.

Computers in those days were expensive and big — no laptops or notebooks — and many companies would have their computer work done outside, but the radio station was finding mistakes. Then an idea came to me. If someone could make so much money writing a computer program that did not work, I should be able to make even more money writing one that did the job. So I took a six-month course in computer programming. When I tried to find work, though, I found out that San Francisco had more out-of-work programmers than computers. A bigger city would surely have more opportunities.

## Chapter 25 - From Los Angeles to France

In the summer of 1972 Ilavati and I moved to Los Angeles, into a little hotel near the temple.

Every morning the summer sun and the palm trees greeted us as we strolled on our way to hear Prabhupada give class. I don’t know where Prabhupada noticed me, maybe in the class one morning or maybe in the small crowd of devotees that followed him out of the temple afterwards, but one day one of his servants spoke with me: “Prabhupada told me, ‘I thought I saw Umapati.’” I began to wonder what I had been doing wrong all these years. What had kept me from seeing Prabhupada’s kindness? Was it sex desire? But all fallen souls had that. Then one day as I chanted on my beads, sitting cross-legged on the vacant stage in the temple auditorium, I thought about the mental gymnastics I had cultivated in the early years to make myself feel high. Yes, here was the problem. I was still attached to being high. I was trying to use Krishna to attain what was easy to find in the material world, looking for broken glass when my diamond had been in front of my eyes the whole time. Now I would have to finally turn away from the duality of high and non-high and learn to see Krishna in every situation.

I remembered how I had been struck by the beauty of the Srimad Bhagavatam and how this beauty always stayed with me, offering to take me beyond my stale, dry conception of feeling high. I remembered the picture of Krishna on His birthday in 1967, how Krishna had shown me that He was all I ever needed. And today this was coming to me not in any ecstatic revelation or mystical vision, though I certainly had nothing against such things, but in a quiet thought as I sat chanting on my beads.

Amazing, wasn’t it, what a simple thought could do? A day or two later, I was talking with Syamasundar, one of the first devotees to go to London and now one of Prabhupada’s staff. “There really is nothing but Krishna consciousness,” I said.

“There just isn’t anything else in life.” The next day I saw him again. “I told Prabhupada what you said,” he told me, “that there is nothing in life but Krishna consciousness, and Prabhupada said, ‘Maya is very strong.’” (Prabhupada was talking about how Maya, the illusory energy, had pulled me away.) Then one evening at the arati ceremony, in my mind’s eye I saw rays of transparent light coming from Radha and Krishna while the devotees worshiped Them with dance and song. This was the right way to live, worshiping the absolute truth, Radha and Krishna. Ilavati also saw something. When I mentioned the arati, a few days later, she knew what I was talking about. Krishna had taken us back.

After the morning and evening arati now, we worshiped the tulsi plant. The scriptures tell us that tulsi plants, also known in English as holy basil, are incarnations of a cowherd girl named Vrinda from Krishna’s village of Vrindavan. In fact, the very name “Vrindavan” means Vrinda’s forest. Tulasi plants grow up to a few feet tall and have many branches with small oval leaves. Tulsi is often referred to by her name as a goddess: Tulsi Devi.

A devotee would offer a small arati — some incense, a ghee lamp, and a flower — to a potted tulsi on a table in the middle of the room while all the others sang a song about the mercy of Tulsi Devi, begging her to take us back to the spiritual world. Then the devotees walked and danced around Tulsi as each one poured three drops of water into her dirt and then with the middle finger of the right hand touched the dirt and then touched the finger to the forehead in respect. I liked the jolly feeling of the ceremony, dancing around Tulsi and singing about her power to cleanse away the devotee’s past sins. Was it just a coincidence that I always felt fresh and clean afterwards? My old friend Sudama, the black man in ISKCON’s first interracial marriage, came again to Los Angeles. But now he was Sudama Swami. He and his wife had separated to let him become a sannyasi carrying the traditional danda, a staff made of four thin bamboo rods bound together with saffron cloth. Because a sannyasi turns away from family life and all other material pleasures to devote himself to preaching, he is praised as the emblem of renunciation, even called Maharaja, a great king who brings the most powerful kings of this world under his control. These powerful kings, the scriptures tell us, are not the ones who hold scepters and sit on thrones, as these kings can be overthrown. The real kings are the senses of the living entities. Everyone obeys them. One who can bring the senses under his own control is therefore an even greater king, or Maharaja.

Thus it is the custom to offer respects to a sannyasi. But as we sat and talked in the empty auditorium, Sudama told me how awkward he felt accepting respect from others. A householder walked into the room. He bowed then knelt before Sudama. “Oh Maharaja,” he said, “can I touch your holy danda?” Sudama looked at me. “I can’t stand it.” A couple of days later, while I was sitting outside, Sudama came and sat next to me. “I went to see Prabhupada,” he said, “and I told him I couldn’t stand it when people bowed to me, and Prabhupada said, ‘They are not bowing to you.’” Prabhupada reminded Sudama that people were bowing before him in honor of the renunciation that he represented. In my mind I vowed that if I ever took sannyasa, I would remember this at every moment.

But I was not a sannyasi yet. I was a householder, and I needed a job. “Why don’t you work for Spiritual Sky?” someone said. Spiritual Sky, a devotee enterprise manufacturing incense, cologne, soaps, and other scented products, put me to work in the shipping department for a tiny salary of twenty dollars a week and a rent-free apartment. But it was enough to survive. One day Prabhupada came to tour Spiritual Sky, and as he looked around, he turned to me and said something I did not quite make out. I thought he said, “You should have done something like this.” Other people who heard him thought he said other things.

Then Spiritual Sky got a computer called Basic 4, the smallest computer in the world, a mere four feet high and two feet across on each side, using disks about a foot and a half in diameter at a cost of two hundred dollars each. I would assist Stoka Krishna Das, an experienced programmer.

After a time, Stoka Krishna left to take an outside job, and the computer was all mine. I even got an assistant, a friendly, intelligent young man named Frank. One day he heard Prabhupada explain that work done for Krishna is not different from Krishna Himself. “You should worship your work,” Prabhupada concluded, so Frank started bowing to the computer at the start of every workday. I could not have imagined anyone bowing to the computer in some office down the street, but as Krishna’s wife Queen Rukmini said, no materialist can understand the mind of a devotee.

One day I was talking about the computer with Dayananda Das, the householder who had gone to Florida to study computers in 1968. “When we were planning to get the computer at Spiritual Sky,” he said “we first asked Prabhupada, and Prabhupada said, ‘Get a computer, and Umapati can run it.’” But at the time Prabhupada said that, I had had no contact with him for many months. How did he know I would come back to him and live in Los Angeles? And how was it that he was still thinking of me? “The disciple may reject the spiritual master,” Prabhupada once wrote to me, “but the bona fide spiritual master never rejects the disciple once accepted.” No matter what stupid things I did, Prabhupada kept our relationship eternally alive.

One day I went with Prabhupada on his morning walk along with a few other disciples including an Indian brahmachari named Svarup Damodar Das (later Goswami), studying science at a university in Los Angeles. Every morning before the class, Prabhupada would ask for him: “Where is our scientist?” That day, Prabhupada turned to me as we walked by the sea. “Can your computer tell you how many grains of sand there are on the beach?” “No, Prabhupada,” I said. “You would have to tell the computer.” Svarup Damodar spoke up. “Does that mean there is a brain superior to the computer?” “The computer’s not a brain,” I said. “It’s just a stupid machine.” Prabhupada said no more about it. He must have been satisfied that I did not stand in awe of computers, as many people did at the time. But this was the first thing they taught us at computer school.

The salesman from the computer company would regularly visit us, supplying us with paper, disks, and so on. “What is this religion about?” he asked me one day. So I took him downstairs to the book warehouse, and a young brahmachari showed him some books.

“I’ll take this one,” he said. “How much is it?” “Five dollars,” said the brahmachari. The salesman handed him a twenty-dollar bill.

“Uh, do you want change?” said the brahmachari.

“Of course I want change,” said the salesman, obviously annoyed.

I grabbed the brahmachari and pulled him outside. “Give the guy his change!” I said.

“Come on, Umapati,” said the brahmachari. “You gotta get the guy’s money.” Then Ramesvara Das, the head of the book department, came and gave the salesman a hard sell and convinced him to buy twenty dollars worth of books.

“Did you see that?” devotees were saying. “Ramesvara is really empowered.

This happened in 1974. After a time, I left Los Angeles. Fifteen years later, I had come back and was walking downtown, when I ran into the salesman.

“Wow!” “Oh! How ya been?” “Great to see you.” “Hey,” said the salesman, laughing, “remember the time you guys beat me out of fifteen bucks?” It was a good lesson for me.

“President Nixon just had his birthday,” a devotee said on a morning walk in a park, still in 1974, “and he offered the first piece of cake to his dog.” President Richard Nixon at that time was embroiled in the Watergate scandal, which finally forced him to resign.

“Yes,” said Prabhupada. “When someone is in trouble he often turns to God.” “No, Prabhupada,” someone said. “Not God. His dog.” “Oh!” Prabhupada said as he laughed loudly. “Dog!” We all laughed. We had often heard Prabhupada talk about yogis who claim to be God: “If he claims he is God, then he is dog.” So the accidental switching of “God” and “dog” gave us all a good laugh.

Prabhupada played with words. One morning during class, he was talking about false spiritual masters. “He is not teacher,” Prabhupada said, “he is cheater.” As we walked along in the park on another morning I began to think about how stupid I was.

Here I had this wonderful jewel in Prabhupada, but how many times had I gone away? How many times had I disobeyed him? What a worthless bum I was! Then I saw Prabhupada turn his head toward me with a worried look on his face. I realized I was putting Prabhupada in anxiety, so I looked up and became cheerful again. Prabhupada had taught me a lesson: It’s good to know you are fallen, but self-deprecation should not become an indulgence.

“Srila Prabhupada,” a man said, “you mentioned that it is easy to become Krishna conscious.

What is hard is to become determined.” “Yes,” said Prabhupada.

“Then how can you become determined?” “By austerity,” said Prabhupada. “That is why we have the four regulative principles.” I thought about the four rules that all devotees vow to follow: no meat, fish, or eggs; no illicit sex; no intoxicants (not even coffee or tea); and no gambling.

“What if someone cannot follow the four principles?” another man asked.

“Then he is a cat or a dog,” said Prabhupada, “and should not accept initiation.” One day, Ilavati and I went to the nearby supermarket and bought some grapes to offer to Prabhupada. We were on our way to a meeting with Prabhupada in his office, arranged by the temple president, but we would have no time to take the grapes home and wash them. Anyway, Prabhupada would surely have his servant wash and prepare them. As soon as we went into Prabhupada’s office we bowed and I handed him the grapes, but Prabhupada did not call his servant. He immediately took a grape and put it into his mouth.

I was aghast to see Prabhupada eat an unwashed grape. Still, I could not stop him. I knew he wanted to give me the mercy of immediately accepting my gift, and I could only hope that Prabhupada’s kindness would override any displeasure Krishna might feel toward me for giving Prabhupada something unwashed.

Then we sat in front of Prabhupada. Ilavati spoke first. “Srila Prabhupada,” she said, “I really like to read your books.” Prabhupada turned to me. “You have a very nice wife,” he said. “You should take care of her.” “Srila Prabhupada,” I said, “I understand that you were unhappy when I left Paris.” “Yes,” said Prabhupada.

“Should I go back?” “No,” said Prabhupada. “Stay here. You have a good house, a good wife, a good job. Why should you go somewhere else?” “I was not able to find a good job in San Francisco,” I said.

“That is because you did not want to give fifty percent,” said Prabhupada. “You wanted to keep a hundred percent for yourself.” I wish I had discussed the matter further with Prabhupada. Unless one is making good money, it is not possible to live on half a salary. Ilavati and I had tried, but it left us with barely enough for the rent, not to mention the other necessities of life. On the other hand, as Prabhupada wrote to me, if I kept all the money, what was the difference between me and a materialist? The next day, as Prabhupada was about to start the morning class, he did not see me sitting at the other end of the temple. “Umapati does not come?” he asked, and someone pointed me out to him.

One morning, as Prabhupada was giving class, I stood behind the vyasasana, the spiritual master’s sitting place, with a few other men when we saw a cockroach walking on the fringe of the umbrella-like cloth dome over Prabhupada’s head. We tried to catch it before it walked to the front and maybe fell on Prabhupada. Try as we might, though, it eluded our grasp. How five grown men could not catch a little cockroach may be hard to imagine, but we were afraid to pull on the fringe at the risk of pulling down the dome. I watched helplessly as the cockroach disappeared around the curve of the dome going toward the front. The cockroach walked all the way around and came to the back again, but we still could not catch it. And again it came around. Finally we did not see the cockroach any more, but I could not breathe easy until I saw Prabhupada get up and walk away.

I remember watching Prabhupada offer arati (incense, camphor lamp, ghee lamp...) to his own spiritual master, Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Saraswati Thakur. Although Prabhupada was performing the same motions as anyone else offering arati, there was a quality of worship there that no one else had.

He really meant it. Later Srila Prabhupada started to weep as he spoke about his spiritual master.

“Thank you,” he said to us in a voice choked with emotion, “thank you for helping me serve my spiritual master.” One day in class Prabhupada mentioned a billboard he had seen advertising cigarettes. “It cools your brain,” he said, quoting the advertisement. He paused. “You are smoking fire,” he added. “How can you be cool?” “Have you seen Krishna smoking?” he said another time. “Have you seen Krishna operating a machine?” Prabhupada talked about the hopelessness of trying to be happy in the material world. Not only Krishna Consciousness but also other spiritual teachings emphasize leaving this world. “No one says, ‘Be happy here,’” said Prabhupada.

“There are no problems in Krishna Consciousness,” Prabhupada said on another occasion. “If there are problems, it is the creation of the mind.” Prabhupada talked about Devaki, the mother of Krishna. Her cousin-brother imprisoned her and killed her first seven children. “Why did Devaki suffer?” said Prabhupada. “She has no karma. She is Krishna’s mother. But just because she came into this world she had to suffer. Suffering is the nature of this world.” One day, Prabhupada acted out both parts of a mock conversation: “Do you want to come to the spiritual world?” \[deep voice\] “No.” “You don’t want to be with Krishna? Eat kachories \[a delicacy\]?” \[deep voice\] “No.” “Then what do you want?” \[deep voice\] “I want to become... a... pig... and eat stool.” Prabhupada smiled as the rest of us laughed at the little comedy routine.

Every morning at the end of Prabhupada’s class, someone brought a plate of freshly baked cookies, and Prabhupada gave a cookie to each of the small children. But there was a catch. The children had to bow to Prabhupada first. The children, of course, considered the cookie far more important than the bowing. “I’m going to bow to Prabhupada,” said a little girl to her mother, “so he’ll give me a cookie.” We would all laugh along with Prabhupada at the silly antics of the children. “We do not mind when the children act foolishly, chewing their thumbs and so on,” said Prabhupada in class, “because we know they have foolish bodies.” Prabhupada was not saying anything new. Everyone knows that children are foolish, but I had never thought about it in terms of a foolish body. It gave me a new insight into what Prabhupada had been saying for years. We don’t grow. We change bodies. And these children would later exchange their foolish bodies for intelligent ones. I remembered an illustration in one of Prabhupada’s books showing a strip of movie film. What I would see on a movie screen as one picture that moved was really a series of still pictures passing before my eyes. What I saw as one growing body was really a succession of bodies.

And death, says the Bhagavad-gita, is simply another change of body: “As the embodied soul continuously passes in this body, from boyhood to youth to old age, the soul similarly passes into another body at death.” “If you say the animal has no soul,” said Prabhupada, “then you would have to say the child has no soul. They have the same intelligence. If you say the child will develop intelligence when he changes his body, so will the animal.” Now it all made sense. And I remembered something Prabhupada had said in one of his early lectures: “Once I was a baby and everyone was kissing me.

Now I am an old man.” Then a scandal broke. Sridhama, who had once been Prabhupada’s personal servant and himself a married man with a little daughter, had gotten involved with another man’s wife and finally ran away with her and her newborn son to San Diego. The angry husband went there one night and shot Sridhama, killing him. Early in the morning, Prabhupada was informed.

That morning after Prabhupada gave out cookies to the children, there was a cookie left over.

Prabhupada looked to his right and then to his left, where in front of him were Sridhama’s real wife and the little daughter. For an instant Prabhupada stopped, as if struck by a thought. Then he gave the extra cookie to this little child who had just lost her father and was too young to even understand it.

A day or so later during class, Prabhupada spoke of Arjuna’s desire to forgive his enemies on the battlefield. “But now,” he continued, “we are lamenting an incident because the boy did not forgive.” Another day he said that a man should be satisfied with his own wife. “But you have to take someone else’s wife,” he said in a tone of sarcasm, “make an enemy — Boom! Boom! — get yourself killed.” When Prabhupada left Los Angeles, I went with the crowd of devotees that accompanied him to the airport. At the gate waiting for boarding, Prabhupada sat in a seat while the disciples sat on the floor and I sat directly in front of Prabhupada.

“Are you giving class?” he asked me.

“Yes,” I said.

“Do you give purports?” he asked.

“First your purport,” I said, “then mine.” Soon Ilavati received a letter from her mother in Paris saying that Ilavati’s brother, Jean-Pierre, was in the hospital in a coma. So Ilavati went back to Paris. Then Spiritual Sky gave up the computer and subscribed to an outside service, so I no longer had my job. Ilavati wrote me that the devotees in Paris wanted me to go there and help with the translation of Prabhupada’s books into French, but I answered that Prabhupada had told me to stay in Los Angeles. Later she wrote me that Prabhupada was in Paris and said that if I was staying in Los Angeles only because he had told me to, then I could go there.

So in September of 1974 I flew back to Paris. By this time Prabhupada had left, and when he came back, I translated his first talk into French as he spoke. Prabhupada was looking at the deities: on one side, large marble deities named Sri Sri Radha-Paris-isvara; on the other side, Jagannath, Baladeva, and Subhadra, all exquisitely dressed. “I do not have to ask whether you are happy,” Prabhupada began. “You are serving Krishna so nicely that I know you are happy.” Later in Prabhupada’s room the temple president, Bhagavan das, spoke to Prabhupada about me. “Umapati is helping with the French translation,” he said. “He is checking the translation against the original English.” “Nice engagement?” Prabhupada asked me.

“Everything is nice here,” I said.

“Then stay here,” said Prabhupada.

After a few days, Prabhupada went to the devotees’ rural community, New Mayapur, a three- hour drive south of Paris. When Prabhupada sat on the lawn and spoke, he had no proper chair, so a Brazilian devotee named Chatur Murti Das and I used our hands to push from behind on a backrest so he could lean back. At one point Prabhupada leaned his weight against the backrest. Although his frame was small, he was strong, and it was all we could do to keep our hands steady.

One day three young people, two men and a woman, came to New Mayapur. They were dressed as Muslims from the nineteenth century, wearing loose pants gathered at the ankles. The woman did not wear a veil. They were well-mannered and polite, and whenever devotees spoke to them about Krishna Consciousness, they answered with similar points in Islam. One afternoon when Prabhupada was lecturing, they kept asking questions and then saying, “We have something similar in Islamic culture...” Finally Prabhupada answered in an exasperated tone: “If you like Islamic culture so much, then follow Islamic culture. Why compare, compare, compare?” They left shortly afterwards.

It was now the summer of 1976. Ten years had gone by since that first summer on Second Avenue. Ilavati and I began to think about going to India, but we had no money. Then Rshabhdev Das from California, a devotee businessman whom I had introduced to Krishna consciousness a few years earlier, came to France. “You did me a favor once,” he said, “so now I’ll do one for you.” Then he reached into his wallet and pulled out five hundred dollars, enough to buy one-way tickets to Delhi for Ilavati and me.

## Chapter 26 - India At Last

I felt Ilavati poking me to wake me up. As I looked out the airplane window and saw the Delhi airport below us, I could hardly wait to land. India, at last! After years of waiting, listening to sweet tales of the holy places from Prabhupada and from disciples who had been here, we would finally have our turn. We would tell our own tales. We would relish our own memories.

But my exhilaration turned to anxiety as I remembered other tales, tales of Western devotees being held for hours, even days, by the immigration authorities. Would it be our turn for this too? But when the immigration officer smiled and said, “Hare Rama,” I felt relieved, and again when the customs officer waved us through. “For us,” he said, “you are like diplomats.” Here indeed was the land Prabhupada had spoken of. Even the officers at the airport knew about Krishna.

A devotee in Paris had told us to go from the airport to the India Gate bus station, but the taxi driver told us there was no bus station at India Gate. We would have to go to Kashmiri Gate. Was he cheating us? No way to know. We would just have to take a chance. But what a beautiful city! Cheated or not, it was worth the ride. The rainy season had just finished, and greenery was everywhere, as if the city had halfway merged into the jungle. But Delhi? A beautiful city? People usually say something else. I can only think that on this first ride, India revealed its essence to Ilavati and me. Even today if I think about it, I can still see the glory of that first ride, the glory of ancient India beyond the rubble and congestion of Dehi. It’s still there.

Kashmiri Gate was indeed the right place, and we took a bus bound for Mathura, next to Vrindavan. As the bus left Delhi, I looked out the window for my first glimpse of life in the Indian countryside. It was only 8:30 in the morning, but the sun was already bright and people were well into their day, everything happening outdoors — the barber cutting the man’s hair, the people shopping at the open-air markets and the little streetside stores, the men driving oxcarts loaded with merchandise. Even the water buffaloes almost seemed busy as they milled around. But as much as I enjoyed the newness of what I was seeing, I was most impressed by the familiarity. I was seeing the same sun I had always seen, the same sky, the same grass, the same trees, the same people doing the same things. I remembered a letter from Prabhupada to a young man who wanted to travel around the world. “You will simply see Maya \[illusion\] in a different dress. People everywhere are eating, sleeping, mating, and defending.” After a couple of hours in the bus, Ilavati turned to me. “The closer we get to Vrindavan,” she said, “the happier the people seem to be.” I thought about 1966. “As soon as you get off the train in Vrindavan,” Prabhupada told us, “you become Krishna conscious.” In Mathura we took a bus to Vrindavan. We would go to Krishna-Balarama Mandir, the ISKCON temple. The bus stopped at a place that looked more like a field than a town. I saw a ricksha go by carrying Westerners.

“Where are we?” I asked out loud.

“We’re in Vrindavan,” a man answered. “This is the closest stop to Krishna-Balarama Mandir.” Ilavati and I got down from the bus, and in a few minutes a ricksha came by and drove us along Chatikara Road (now Bhaktivedanta Swami Marg). I could hardly believe my eyes. Krishna consciousness was everywhere: the holy men in the street, the Indian-looking buildings, the sign that said “Sri Krishna Bhakti Ashram.” It all looked like a world I wanted to live in. I remembered what devotees had told me in France: “No matter how much you have heard about Vrindavan, you won’t be disappointed when you get there.” And there was nothing to be disappointed about. I was thrilled.

Ilavati too. “I like everything about this place,” she told me.

The next day Prabhupada came. I went to see him and handed him the new French magazine.

“What are you doing here?” asked Prabhupada.

“I’ve been a devotee for ten years,” I said. “I wanted to see India.” “And how are you seeing it?” “It’s wonderful. Vrindavan is the most beautiful place I’ve ever seen.” Prabhupada smiled.

“Do you have any children?” Prabhupada asked.

“No,” I said.

Then the local manager, Gopal Krishna Das (now Goswami), came in and saw me. “We need Umapati in India,” he said.

Prabhupada held up the French magazine. “He is engaged elsewhere,” he said.

A week went by. Still no sign of return tickets coming from France, so I went to see Prabhupada again.

“Our return tickets have not come,” I said. “Can we stay longer?” “You can stay,” said Prabhupada. His mood was stern and aloof, not the smiling, welcoming mood I had seen a week earlier. But I knew that I could not ask to see Prabhupada in whichever mood I wanted. Let Prabhupada walk and talk as he saw fit. And let this little disciple bow his head and gratefully accept it.

Ilavati and I moved into the ISKCON guesthouse, but after a few weeks some devotees invited us to live in their little community in a large house on the edge of town. It wasn’t far from the temple, and we could attend the programs every day, but other devotees were advising us against the move.

“Everybody there has malaria,” a woman told us, “and their life is so austere that it takes them all day just to take care of their bodies.” But we decided to give it a try, and as soon as we settled in, we knew this was our home. Nobody had malaria, but you can get that wherever you live anyway, and the big austerities were that we drew water from a well and walked to one end of the property to use the toilet. The house was L-shaped with the short side facing the road and the long side bordering a large grassy courtyard with a big tree in the middle. Squirrels scampered about all day accompanied by little birds that looked like sparrows but twice as big. We called them “puffy birds” because they often fluffed out their feathers making themselves look bigger. An occasional peacock would visit, and on a few occasions a cobra slithered in. Generally, the scriptures prohibit the killing of animals, but they make an exception in the case of snakes and scorpions.

The locals told us that when you kill a cobra, you must burn the body, especially the head because the eyes will take a photo of the killer, and the mate will come, look in the eyes, and take revenge. A Westerner may laugh at this kind of talk — Hmpf! Primitive folklore — but when you live in India you begin to realize that many things in this world defy the Western limits of reality. Besides, when you see a dead cobra at your feet, you would rather not put the theory to the test.

Animals seemed more intelligent in India. I often saw cows and buffaloes walking unaccompanied along the road, going home after a day in the pasture. Monkeys stole tape recorders and radios to take to the market and trade for fruit. Then one afternoon as I walked out my door, I heard a sudden noise. A hawk had swooped down from the tree and was sitting on a squirrel. The hawk looked at the quirrel’s face a few times, and each time he did, the squirrel raised his head and the hawk looked away. My first thought was to scare away the hawk, but I remembered the story of King Sibi: The hawk also had a right to his food, so I stood and watched. Another squirrel stood a few yards away chattering at the hawk. Then a bunch of puffy birds flew onto a branch above the hawk, chirping furiously. Suddenly the puffy birds flew down and attacked the hawk, and the hawk flew away, leaving the squirrel behind, and the squirrel ran up into the tree. India was showing me the other side of life, bringing me closer to nature than I had ever been, even in Santa Fe. I had never before felt so much welcomed by a place as by Vrindavan.

The community was managed by a couple: Dhananjaya Das from Scotland and his American wife Bala Gopala Devi Dasi. They had started a business making clothes for the deities in ISKCON temples around the world, and I became their bookkeeper though I knew almost nothing about keeping books. I would sometimes watch the Indian craftsmen, Muslims as well as Hindus, beautifully embroidering a cloth stretched as taut as can be on a low table big enough for a few of them to work on one large piece. They sat on the floor around the table, one hand above the cloth and the other beneath it, pushing the needle up and down through the cloth at an amazing speed.

I also did the daily grocery shopping, mostly at a big outdoor market. Although many of the merchants spoke some English, I learned to count in Hindi so I could hear how much the locals were paying and avoid being overcharged. I often saw a man with a horribly deformed body, so twisted he had to halfway wrap himself around a pole, buying things and dealing normally with other people.

“In the West,” said Ilavati, “they would put him in a special home for deformed people so others would not see him and we all could enjoy the illusion that everyone in the world was beautiful and healthy. In India, he is accepted as part of society.” Ilavati shared the chores with the other women, sometimes doing most or all of the cooking.

The women cooked outdoors on little individual charcoal stoves that appeared to have been made from coffee cans. They cooked sitting down and always with a pail of water nearby in case a sari would catch fire. The little stoves were better for cooking the chapati flatbreads, they told me, because the fire formed a solid circle instead of a ring. And what a taste! O for one of those chapaties again, made from freshly ground wheat berries and cooked on a charcoal flame. The special aroma and taste of freshly ground wheat disappears after a few days, so you have to experience it yourself, on the spot.

Prabhupada wanted help from experienced Western devotees in setting up new temples in India, but there were often problems in getting long-term visas from the Indian government. And not only in India, but also in many other countries, devotees and others faced the same difficulty. “This is dog consciousness,” Prabhupada said one morning in class. “When a dog leaves his own place and goes to another neighborhood, the dogs in that neighborhood chase him out. It is the same. ‘This is my country! Arf! Arf! You cannot come in! Arf! Arf! Why do you want to come? Arf! Arf! All right. Stay for three months. Then get out! Arf! Arf!’” One day as I was talking with Harikesh Swami while he typed for Prabhupada outside the entrance to Prabhupada’s house, Palika Devi Dasi came by. Palika had joined in Los Angeles, but now she was part of Prabhupada’s staff, cooking and offering other personal services. “Palika,” said Harikesh Swami,” “I want you to tell me everything that’s going on.” After Palika finished telling him about the illicit romances among the devotees, Harikesh Swami peeked through the crack in the door into Prabhupada’s living room. “Oh no!” he said. “I saw Prabhupada walking away from the door. He heard everything.” “If you have illicit sex in Vrindavan,” Prabhupada said in class the next day, “you will take birth as a monkey in Vrindavan. It is Krishna’s mercy. You have taken shelter of Vrindavan. So stop this monkey business.” Another time, after recovering from a fever, I met Prabhupada as he came out the side door of the temple.

“Where were you?” he asked.

“I was sick, Prabhupada,” I said.

In the fall, Ilavati and I went to visit Kuruksetra, the place where Krishna spoke the Bhagavad- gita to Arjuna at the beginning of a great battle. As I looked out the train window and saw the land become level, stretching flat as far as I could see, I tried to imagine the grand armies assembling there to fight the eighteen-day war. In the town were many temples to Arjuna and Krishna, and at a place called Jyotisar stood a large banyan tree. Here, it is said, under this magnificent tree, Krishna stopped the chariot and pacified His distraught friend Arjuna with the eternal teachings. In the town was a small temple to the sun god, so small you could not walk in but could only look in from outside. Next to it was another small temple with exquisite deities of Dhuva Maharaja, the five-year-old prince who went to the forest to ask God for a kingdom, with God standing before him as four-handed Narayana holding a club, a lotus, a conch, and the chakra, a disc-like weapon. The deities looked at each other with loving smiles, happy to finally meet. They had bodies the color of old marble, and they were alive, radiating a vibrant sweetness. Though it has been almost forty years now, they still come into my mind.

Back in Vrindavan I took up a new service, something I had never done before. Every morning I dressed the small brass deities of Sri Sri Radha Krishna. Afterwards, I would prepare the vyasasana, the spiritual master’s sitting place, for the arrival of Prabhupada and set up the items for offering arati to him. Occasionally I myself offered the arati. But we did not know as many rules in those days, and I did not know that a man should wear a piece of cloth around his shoulders on the altar.

One day, Prabhupada came in a little early and found me bare-chested preparing the arati items. “You should put something over your shoulders,” he told me, and someone gave me a cloth. But I thought Prabhupada wanted me to wear something because I was going to offer the arati that day, so the next day when Prabhupada came in I was again bare-chested. Prabhupada turned to me sharply and angrily. “I told you not to come without a wrapper!” he shouted. “You should listen and learn!” A few devotees came running up to me. “What did he say? What did he say?” I could not speak. If my life air had left my body at that moment, I could not have felt more devastated. It took me several days to recover my composure. But looking back, I relish that moment.

A rebuke from the spiritual master is another kind of mercy, especially if the disciple is too stupid to learn any other way.

Another morning, as I was offering the five-wick ghee lamp to Prabhupada, the fire went out on two of the wicks. With his eyes, Prabhupada motioned to me to re-light the wicks. Again, I felt pleasure at receiving a direct instruction from Prabhupada.

Then news came from France that many devotees were dissatisfied with the management of the temples there, and some devotees came and gave Prabhupada a ten-page report on the problem.

Bhagavan Das, an American in charge of ISKCON in France, was in India at the time and met with Prabhupada. Prabhupada announced that devotees in France working on the translation of his books into French should continue their work even if they did not want to live in an ashram. This included me, as I would soon be returning.

In the early spring Ilavati and I decided to visit Jagannath Puri, the home of the original deities of Jagannatha, Baladeva, and Subhadra, though we knew we could not go into the main temple there.

The managers of the temple considered foreigners contaminated and unfit to see Jagannatha. But the town had many other temples and places to visit.

First we went to Delhi and then took a train to Calcutta. Then it was just a short train ride and bus ride to Mayapur, where the devotees had acquired a large property. Prabhupada was there, and though I did not speak with him directly, Ilavati and I heard him give class every morning. After the class there would be kirtan as Prabhupada led the devotees dancing around the altar. Each time Prabhupada passed around the back of the altar and came to the front, he rang a large bell hung from the ceiling. Even today, as the devotees dance around the altar in Mayapur during kirtan, someone carries a small form of Prabhupada and when they come to the front of the altar, another person helps Prabhupada ring the bell.

Then we left for Jagannath Puri. The first stop would be Calcutta, where we would change trains. We found ourselves with a few hours’ time on our hands as we waited for the next train.

Should we go to the Calcutta temple? It was far, maybe too troublesome. But then, maybe it would be better to go. We hesitated and then finally decided to go and see the beautiful deities. As we stood in one of the temple offices and talked with the devotees, a young man at a desk spoke to me.

“What is your name?” he asked.

“Umapati,” I said.

“I have something for you.” He pulled an envelope out of the drawer and handed it to me.

It was our return tickets to Paris. They had finally come after seven months. But why in Calcutta? The devotees in Paris had sent them to Delhi, and the devotees in Delhi, knowing we were traveling in the eastern part of India, had sent them here. But in any case, now we had them. There was only one problem: Our flight would leave for Paris on Kuwait Airways in just two days. We could never make it even if we tried to get there immediately. When we left the temple, we stopped in at the offices of another airline, KLM, and asked for advice. “Send them a telegram,” said the gentleman at the counter. So we sent a telegram. Now we could relax. We would take care of the matter when we returned to Delhi.

The most direct route to Jagannatha Puri from Calcutta was a train to Bhubaneswar, where we took a ricksha from the train station to an area called Nayapali. The road ran through a green forest thick with trees and knee-high grass till we came to a clearing where some devotees were living in temporary houses. Construction would start soon on a temple here under the direction of Gaura Govinda Swami Maharaja, a lifelong devotee born in the region. Maharaja welcomed us and treated us like vising royalty.

From there it was just a short bus ride to Jagannath Puri. The town itself was small, and you could easily walk from one end to the other. As expected, we could not enter the main temple, but there were many other temples where we were cordially received. As Ilavati and I walked along the main road one day, we saw a sign indicating the birthplace of Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati Thakur, the spiritual master of Prabhupada. We went and found a house with one room occupied by some young men from the Gaudiya Math, the organization founded by Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati himself. The rest of the house, the devotees told us, was occupied by a family of demons and the house was in litigation, the Gaudiya Math and the demonic family both trying to take possession. I wondered whether the people could really be so bad as to be called demons.

When it was time for the noon arati, we all went into the courtyard, where there was a small altar. The demonic family was sitting around, laughing at us while a woman with a broom splashed dirty water on us from a puddle. Well if they weren’t real demons, they were sure trying hard to fit the bill. The scriptures say that in this age, known as kali-yuga, the demons and the devotees look alike, but the people in that family had the ugliest faces I had ever seen on human beings, twisted and misshapen without being actually deformed. In the end, the devotees won the court battle, and there is now a temple at that place.

We stayed a few days visiting temples and meeting hospitable people like the gentleman who offered us chairs on his veranda when we got caught in a sudden rainstorm. Then we went back to Bhubaneswar, where some devotees offered to drive us up to Mayapur. Prabhupada would still be there. I had only a brief conversation with him.

“Are you going back to Vrindavan?” he asked me.

“Yes,” I said. That was the last conversation I had with Prabhupada.

Then we took a train to Delhi and went immediately to the offices of Kuwait Airways. I showed our tickets to the woman there.

“This has passed,” she said gently.

“I sent you a telegram,” I said. “Didn’t you get it?” “No,” she said.

My mind started racing. If our visas ran out and we didn’t leave India, we would surely be arrested, and I was traveling with an attractive young wife. I had heard stories about Western women having trouble with men in India, and of course, not all policemen were fine conscientious moral citizens. Was our beautiful trip to India going to end in jail? or worse? O Krishna! O Krishna! Please help us! Then the woman spoke again. “When would you like to leave?” Oh thank You, Krishna. Thank you Prabhupada.

We fixed a date for the following week and went back to Vrindavan. A few days later, someone gave me a note from the local immigration office. “Your stay in India has ended,” the note began.

## Chapter 27 - Prabhupada Departs

In April 1977, after seven and a half months in India, Ilavati and I flew back to Paris and started working as bookkeepers for Spiritual Sky, the French counterpart of the devotee-owned company I had worked for in Los Angeles. The company was run by, of all people, Harivilasa Das, the devotee I had fought with a few years earlier. But now we were all friends. I also continued working on the French translation, as Prabhupada had instructed.

In the summer, word came that Prabhupada was sick, and as the days passed, the sickness worsened. Devotees all over the world were praying for Prabhupada’s recovery. Every day Ilavati and I would try to find out the latest news.

In the fall, one day, as we walked into the office, an employee stopped us.

“Prabhupada...” he began to say.

No need to say more. We understood. Prabhupada had departed.

I felt suspended in mid-air, not knowing what to think, what to feel next. In the days that followed I went through the motions of life but hardly aware of it. One night I dreamed it was all a rumor, that Prabhupada had not really left. I ran everywhere, up and down, shouting the good news.

But it was only a dream.

But as the days passed I came to understand that through my chanting and reading and serving I would still be talking with Prabhupada. The lamenting was finished. It would be no compliment to my spiritual master. Here, and I had known this almost from the first day I met Prabhupada, was the real meaning of being a disciple: the spiritual master’s mission had to go on.

Ilavati and I didn’t talk about it until she broke the silence. “You know,” she said one day, “It seems that Prabhupada is more present in his pictures now.” I had noticed it too.

“Krishna Consciousness is not a material thing, and cannot be broken: it cannot be burned or wet or dried or stopped at any stage.” (letter from Srila Prabhupada, September 5, 1967)

I look forward now to the next life, the next world, when I can be with Prabhupada again. I’ll do better. I will.
