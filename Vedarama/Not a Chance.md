# Not a Chance

## Not a Chance

Reflections, by David Osborn

Does anything ever happen by chance, or is that merely the way we explain away things we do not undestand. I think the latter. What does this then say for modern science, which prides itself in explaining our world, weening us from the bottle of our superstitions, good luck charms, and our “faith,” yet at the same time authoritatively informs us that life itself arises by chance? What kind of double talk is this?

It seems that some scientists are using the concept of chance in a way that misleads the public. Unable to explain the origin of the universe by physical laws, they assert that it was somehow *caused* by chance. But such statements are meaningless.

To make any statement about a chance event meaningful, many repetitions of the event in question are required. And these must be observable. For example we can flip a coin many times and note the results. We can see that they correspond to a statistical pattern indicating a 50% probablility that heads will turn up rather than tails. The word *chance* therefore does not actually refer to a cause—it refers only to a certain type of pattern in the results of an operation repeated a sufficient number of times. Upon recognizing such a pattern we can say. "There is a 50% chance that the tossed coin will come up heads."

Now imagine we could toss a given coin only once and it came up heads. If someone asked why that result happened, we might give a causal explanation or say that we don't know, but it would not be meaningful to say it happened by chance.

So now, what about the universe? It is not possible for us to observe more than one appearance of a universe, if that—we can only see, at best, the one we're in. The origin of the universe is thus a one-time event, and statements about it that involve chance are meaningless according to the rules of quantitative science. Nevertheless, some theorists continue to speak of universes emerging from the quantum mechanical vaccuum *by chance.* To be quite frank, this is another limitation of quantum mechanics, which is based upon the concept of probability. It may be valid to apply chance to events that can be repeated and observed in the laboratory, but in the case of the universe, where such repetition and observation are impossible in principle, “chance” is meaningless. Thus if you want to use quantum theory to explain the origin of the universe, good luck!

If we really want to substanciate chance theory relative to universal origins perhaps we could postulate a hypothetical trans-universal consciousness, that is capable of observing the origin of many universes and computing statistics about them, thus rendering statements about the probability universal origin meaningful. This is not too farfetched for theoretical physisists. These days the line between science and fiction is very thin, especially in the theoreitcal arena.

In order to obtain such information from the hypothesized trans-universal consciousness we would have to be able to communicate with it, and it would have to be personal. But what kind of person? Certainly one whom we would hold in veneration at least as much as we already do those amongst us who exhibit greatness in areas of say, sports, music, or politics. Worshipable? Perhaps, after all, think of the nature of the knowledge we could receive from such a super conscious entity. But wait a miniute, this is starting to sound like religion. And what will modern materialistic science say about that? Not a chance!
