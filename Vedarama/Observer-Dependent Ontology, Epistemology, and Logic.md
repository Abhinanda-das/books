**Summary and Overview of the Article**

The Vedic conception of reality doesn’t comport with the Western ideas of reality widely enshrined in modern science and taken for granted by almost everyone reading the Vedic texts at present. Reality, by definition, in the West is something observer-independent. Anything personal and private is by definition not part of reality. In contrast, reality is observer-dependent in the Vedic texts. Even those parts of reality that seem observer-independent (i.e., observable by our senses) are the body of an observer. Without the observer, the body will not exist. Rather, the body is developed based on the observer’s history and is hence observer-dependent. But since the history is not the observer, therefore, identifying an observer through the body is called māyā which means “that which is not”. Most people studying Vedic texts skip the understanding of māyā and assume that all reality is observer-independent.

In this article, I will discuss the Western ideas on reality and what is wrong with those ideas, even as everyone takes them for granted. We will discuss how all epistemologies based on these ideas have systematically failed leading to the abandonment of the search for truth in science and its replacement by pragmatism, which means claiming victory in the quest for truth through success in technology.

We will then discuss alternative ideas of reality found in Vedic texts and how they lead to a complete epistemology. I will try to contrast the Vedic ideas of reality to their Western scientific counterparts. The article will be useful to those familiar with Western ideas of reality to see their contrast with the Vedic ideas. The article may open the eyes of those who assume that “reality” means the same everywhere.

**The Birth and Demise of Western Realism**

Realism is defined in Western philosophy in three ways: (a) reality is that which exists outside the observer, (b) reality is that which works without an observer’s observation, and (c) reality is objective, which means it is not open to personal interpretation. Realism has many further variations such as Naïve Realism and Scientific Realism. The latter has many divisions that we will briefly discuss below.

Naïve Realism says that the reality that exists outside the observer in just the same way as the observer knows it. For example, if you see a red and round apple, reality is a red and round apple. The redness, roundness, and appleness of the thing seen by the observer aren’t a subjective view of the observer. They are also the very nature of reality. In stark contrast, Scientific Realism says that what we call redness, roundness, and appleness is an illusion created by the observer’s senses and the mind. The reality is instead mathematical objects (such as particles and waves) and instrument-measurable properties (such as position and momentum). The world is not anything like the way we see it.

Naïve Realism is problematic because everyone doesn’t perceive the world in the same way. The shades of colors can seem darker or lighter to different observers. The tastes can seem more or less sweet or bitter to different observers. Two observers might disagree about whether something is a sedan or a wagon. To uphold Naïve Realism, we have to say that one of many observers seeing the world differently is seeing it correctly. Or, we might decide to vote to determine where the majority opinion tilts. Either way, we would have refuted the idea that the world exists outside the observer just the way we see it. At most, we would be able to say that some observers know the reality while others are illusioned. This is not necessarily a false view. It is just hard for anyone who is called “illusioned” to accept it.

Enlightenment thinking chose to move away from Naïve Realism. It tried to replace human sensations with instrument measurements and human concepts with mathematical constructs. This move slightly reduced the problem of Naïve Realism but did not totally eliminate it. For example, who is to say that some instruments are better than other instruments—a counterpart of the question of whose senses are giving the true nature of reality? Who is to say that some mathematical constructs are better than others—a counterpart of the question of whether someone calls the same thing as a sedan or a wagon?

Enlightenment thinkers solved this problem through the institutionalization of knowledge. It says that measuring instruments will be selected by a committee of informed people who shall designate some instruments as standards to be used for all measurements. The mathematical constructs will be selected by a committee of informed people who shall designate them as the preferred models of reality. The scientific method of institutionalization of knowledge borrowed heavily from established methods of the Church. Just as Christianity had institutionalized religion through the Church, similarly, Enlightenment thinkers institutionalized science through a reformed and reformulated system of universities.

Institutionalization doesn’t guarantee truth. Debate among well-intentioned members of the institution may eliminate obvious mistakes, but every institution has an ideology. Nobody knows if that ideology is true and the ideology is therefore accepted on faith. Scientific Realism chose an ideology of materialism, reductionism, and mechanism. Materialism says that reality comprises objects that exist independent of observers. Reductionism says that complex things must necessarily comprise simpler things. Mechanism says that the objects and simple things conceived by science must follow mathematical laws.

The ideology of science has now comprehensively failed. Mechanism is false because mathematical systems cannot deal with sentences in the same way they deal with objects. A sentence has a meaning, a reference, and a truth value, but an object has none of those. If these are described using numbers, then contradictions are created because a number now has many interpretations. Therefore, a world comprising sentences cannot be modeled by mathematics. Even the most rudimentary cognitive system is outside mathematical reach. Reductionism is false because we cannot always separate complex things into simpler things. A system that cannot be so reduced is called quantum entangled. Materialism is false because the outcome of experiments depends on the observer’s state. We cannot call the outcome of an instrument measurement an observer-independent state of reality because other observers performing the same type of measurement using the same instrument come up with different results.

After the comprehensive failure of Scientific Realism, there arose a new breed of philosophies that tried to rescue science from its quagmire—Operationalism, Instrumentalism, and Pragmatism. Operationalism says that words like mass or energy have no meaning outside of the instrument we use to measure them. Instrumentalism says that a theory that aggregates such concepts is just a tool whose worth has to be measured not by whether the theory is true or false but by whether it explains and predicts the data. Pragmatism says that the value of science is that it contributes to socio-economic-political progress.

The main conclusion was that reality and truth are not the focus of science. The focus was building technology, fueling economic growth, reducing social disparity, and bringing political stability. Nobody who knows science at present says that science gives us the nature of reality or the absolute truth. Their argument for the continued funding of science is that it has improved the lives of people. Along with this shift, those parts of science that do not explicitly contribute to such goals started to be defunded.

**The Birth and Demise of Western Idealism**

The demise of Scientific Realism has led to the revival of interest in Idealism which was considered dead and useless during Enlightenment. Idealism is defined in three ways: (a) whatever we call the world exists inside the observer, (b) the world in the observer works due to its observation by the observer, and (c) the world inside the observer necessarily depends on the observer’s personal choices or will.

Idealism arose from the problems of Realism. Factually, (a) we can never know reality as it exists outside our observation because we have no access to a world behind the observation, (b) everything inside our observation is subject to our interpretation and defined by our choices, even if those include the choice of measuring instruments and scientific theories, and (c) nothing works the same for all observers even if the differences across different instruments and measurements seem negligible in most cases.

However, Idealism has a profound flaw: (a) while living our lives, we don’t enjoy suffering, (b) we don’t voluntarily create our suffering, and (c) we want to get out of suffering. Therefore, in so far as there is suffering caused by the world, the world cannot merely be our choice, interpretation, or creation. The world should instead (a) be forced upon us, (b) exist outside our control since nobody willingly creates their personal suffering, and (c) must, therefore, be accepted as an external reality.

The falsification of Idealism, however, doesn’t end its allure. It has given rise to a new breed of hybrid Realism-Idealism theories that can be called Simulated Reality or Virtual Reality philosophies. The rising popularity of such themes can be seen from the fact that popular magazine articles that assert that we must be living in a Simulated or Virtual Reality go completely unchallenged. A thought experiment called Brain in the Vat says that we could be brains submerged in a beaker filled with some preservative fluid with probes connecting different parts of the brain to a computer that injects electrical signals to create the illusion of a rich worldly experience when the only reality outside the brain is a beaker, preservative fluid, probes, wires, and a computer with a programmer writing control software.

Thought experiments like Brain in the Vat cannot be refuted through any kind of argument or evidence because all arguments and evidence assume that there is truth, justice, and goodness in the world. If truth, justice, and goodness are replaced by delusion, injustice, and malevolence, then we have no foundation on which to base our arguments and evidence. Therefore, thought experiments like Brain in the Vat are irrefutable. There is no evidence for them. But there is no counterargument against them either.

Simulated Reality, Virtual Reality, and Brain in the Vat are not Idealism because there is something outside the observer. Since we can never know what is outside the observer, therefore, these theories are consistent with the failure of Scientific Realism. Even though we might reject Idealism because it is contrary to suffering, the fact is that most people trying to escape suffering fail in their endeavors. Failure is not the end of the story. Instead, a theory is created to rationalize the failure—our failure to understand reality and escape suffering is not our fault but the fault of the system that put us in it.

The human mind has an exceptional need for rationalization. It rationalizes failures too. People are drawn to Simulated Reality because it rationalizes their failure to find the truth and escape suffering. We cannot find the truth because Simulated Reality makes it impossible to know the truth. We cannot escape suffering because Simulated Reality doesn’t allow us to escape its control. The need to rationalize failure makes Simulated Reality inevitable especially because there is no counterargument against it.

Simulated Reality is a form of Nihilism in which life has no purpose other than helpless suffering or pleasure and death is nothing more than whatever puts an end to this suffering and pleasure. Nihilism is not the rejection of reality—after all, the Brain in the Vat is some reality. It is not the rejection of the self—after all, the self may be enjoying and suffering through the Brain in the Vat. Nihilism is a rejection of the value in asking questions of truth, justice, and happiness. It is not that answers to such questions exist that we don’t know about. It is also not that the questions are unanswerable. It is rather that the questions have been comprehensively answered by denying that they are meaningful questions.

Nihilism creates a sense of helplessness and hopelessness. You cannot find the truth on your own. You cannot put an end to your suffering. You cannot choose to create the reality of your liking. You exist because you haven’t died. You have no control over your life. The West has now formally entered the stage of boundless intellectual Nihilism. We see it in the cynical attitude with which every proposal is received as merely a person’s opinion. There will be no end to social decline unless we can re-establish the possibility of finding the truth and escaping suffering clearly and unambiguously.

**An Overview of Vedic Realism-Idealism**

Most of us can accept that we exist, seek happiness, and work to create happiness for ourselves through our actions. We don’t need to know anything other than ourselves to accept these claims, provided they are true of us. Of course, they are not universally true for every person. Some people are too lazy to do anything, even for themselves. Some people are too masochistic to seek their own happiness. Some people are too asleep to even perceive their own existence. Outside of these exceptions, for most people, our existence, our quest for happiness, and our readiness to work for our happiness can be taken for granted. Each person can decide if this is true for them. If it is, then it is confirmed for them.

Assuming you are aware of your existence, seek happiness, and are prepared to work for that happiness, you would seek the company of like-minded people. You would not want the company of lazy, asleep, or masochistic people. They deserve to live with their own kind. You may be awake, but not seeking pleasure, and not working towards it. You would seek the company of like-minded people. You would not like to be with people who are asleep, masochistic, or working to hurt themselves. You will also not prefer the company of people who are awake, seeking happiness, and working toward it.

The Vedic system elevates this simple idea of people living with like-minded people to a universal fact. The birds of the same feather are flocked together. The soul with the bird mentality is born as a bird to live with birds. The soul with an elephant mentality is born as an elephant to live with other elephants. The demons and demigods live on different planets. Those with the human mentality are born into a human body to live with other humans. They don’t mix with all other humans. Instead, they are separated by societies, cultures, religions, ideologies, professions, and interests for the most part, mainly because they want to live with like-minded people. There are infinite different universes to facilitate different kinds of lives. Those who transcend the material world, live with those of their mentality on planets suited for a different life with that mentality. Segregation of life forms based on their mentality is the big picture of Vedic cosmology. It describes infinite realms, universes, planets, and classes within which life forms with different mentalities live together with others of a compatible mentality.

Within this big picture of reality, there is a minor picture of different kinds of living forms interacting occasionally. Some people keep dogs and cats as pets. Some people tend to domestic animals. Some people ride horses. Some people work in circuses with different animals. Due to globalization, people of different cultures, societies, religions, and ideologies have begun mixing recently. However, even as we see the mixing of different life forms, we find that they generally prefer to live within a species if what they want is available within it. The Vedic texts explain that a desired mixing between different kinds of living forms is the result of their previously diversified mentalities and the result of prior good actions. When mixing is undesirable, it is forced due to the results of one’s previous bad actions. Still, the diversity of experience created by such mixing is infinitesimal compared to the diversity across planets, universes, and realms. Segregation of life across the cosmos is infinitely bigger in comparison. The minor picture of life forms mixing with each other is either the fulfillment of the desires in a previously diversified mentality due to good actions in the past, or it is the result of undesirable mixing as a result of previous bad actions.

The big picture of the cosmos and the segregation of life forms indicates that there is no universal reality. Consequently, there is no counterpart of the word “reality” in Vedic texts because everyone has a different personal reality. The word “reality” appears in the Western lexicon under the assumption that there is some universal truth regarding all times, places, circumstances, and persons. That idea is alien to the Vedic system.

We find ourselves in a world because of our previously acquired nature and the results of our actions. If we were radically different, then we would be in another world. The way we perceive, interpret, and understand the world is also the result of our previously acquired nature. If we begin perceiving, interpreting, and understanding the world differently, then we will gradually develop a different nature, and upon death enter a different world. The process of the change of life, change of species, and change of place is just birds of the same feather flocking together. There is a fixed place for each kind of bird. There is a simple process of flocking the birds.

Karma, reincarnation, transmigration across species, and the existence of varied life across the cosmos are features of the Vedic system that even newcomers can see from miles away. We don’t need a specific sūtra, verse, or doctrinal name to spell it out. Every Vedic text presents such descriptions. But we have to assimilate this idea better. We have to understand that what we see in this species, life, and planet, is not an observer-independent reality. Rather, what we see, why we see, how we see, where we see, and when we see are the result of who we are. These may not be our eternal unchangeable nature. But it is our current nature. The world simply mirrors who we are. We are in a specific world because of who we are. If we evolve, then we will change our world.

We can call this Observer-Dependent Realism. There is a world of living forms with commonalities. Those forms are not confined within my awareness because I don’t control what they do. They have to be accepted as distinct realities because they are beyond my control. Idealism in the sense of something that exists solely in my awareness has to be therefore rejected. But that rejection doesn’t establish an observer-independent reality. The reality that I see is that which conforms to my nature. If I had a different nature, then I would see a different reality. Therefore, my reality is not universal. My nature filters all that exists cosmically into an infinitesimally small subset that corresponds to my nature.

We are not blank slates passively observing the world from a place that is nowhere and nowhen. We are actively filtering the cosmos during observation to see something compatible with our nature. Our senses, minds, and intellects are filters through which we see limited parts of the cosmos in a limited way. The study of senses, minds, and intellects has to begin with the idea that they filter the complete truth and present a narrow picture, which we arrogantly elevate to the status of universal reality.

Humility is required to understand that there are infinite other types of realities that we don’t see presently. Humility is required to accept that our senses, minds, and intellects are acting as filters of the complete truth. Humility is required to acknowledge that we need to change the filters of our senses, minds, and intellects to even see what is being filtered right now. The problem of not seeing things found in other parts of the cosmos cannot be solved if we insist on using the same filters.

This problem is sometimes called The Frog in the Well. We are frogs in the small well we see presently. We are in the well because we are frogs. If we were bigger fish, we would be in the river or the ocean. But the frog in the well thinks that even rivers and oceans are just like wells. The frog cannot imagine that the fish in the ocean see millions of different species of life that remain invisible to the frog in the well. Observer-Dependent Realism is a fancy term for the simple idea stated by the frog in the well.

**An Overview of Vedic Epistemology**

Modern Western epistemology was developed to counteract the problem of sin in Christianity. The problem of sin says that we are all sinners (having inherited the sins of the sinful Adam and Eve) and therefore we cannot know the truth by ourselves. The truth has to be revealed to us by God and His messiahs after which we have to simply accept their conclusions on faith. Meanwhile, the priests were secretly crafting the “revealed truth” calling themselves the ones uniquely blessed by the Spirit of God. Enlightenment thinkers decided to break away from this trap of sinning, being blessed by a spirit, and revelation. They postulated that every human can know the whole truth by reason and observation.

All Vedic epistemologies accept reasoning, observation, and revelation as methods to the truth. In contrast, Western scientific epistemology rejects revelation and Abrahamic religious epistemology rejects reasoning and observation. Despite this stark contrast, nearly all modern students of science and religion have disregarded the methods of reasoning and observation and equated the Vedic texts to revelation (if not something much worse, such as fanciful imagination and speculation). Little to no effort has been made to explain how reasoning, observation, and revelation work together. The Western science-religion methodological dichotomy is always taken for granted.

Therefore, let’s spend a moment trying to understand how the Vedic epistemology reconciles reasoning, observation, and revelation. To do that, we need to distinguish the discovery of truth from the verification of truth. Revelation pertains to the discovery of truth while reasoning and observation pertain to the verification of truth. There is a famous P≠NP problem in computer science. It says that we require non-polynomial (NP) time to discover the truth while we need polynomial (P) time to verify the truth. An example of a computer password can be given. Discovering the correct password through trial and error requires non-polynomial time but verifying the password requires polynomial time. Revelation is like telling a person the password after which they can verify the correctness of the password through reasoning and observation. Revelation, reasoning, and observation all require polynomial time.

Reasoning and observations can be further divided into—(a) what we can think and perceive under the current senses, minds, and intellects, and (b) what we can think and perceive if the filters in the current senses, minds, and intellects were removed or changed. Accordingly, there are grades of verification by reasoning and observation. Some things can be verified using current senses, minds, and intellects. If that establishes trust in the revelation, then one can pursue the path of removing or changing the filters in the senses, mind, and intellect, to confirm that even more revelation can be verified. The process culminates in the conclusion—after the progressive removal of current filters—that every revelation can be confirmed either by unfiltered or appropriately filtered senses, minds, and intellects.

This is Observer-Dependent Epistemology. Every revelation cannot be verified by every sense, mind, and intellect. However, every revelation can be verified by every person, because the person is not the sense, mind, or intellect. The former is the person seeing and the latter is the goggles of seeing. We change the goggles to see something different. Since the reality we see depends on the goggles we wear, therefore, epistemology is observer-dependent. However, that confirmation is neither confined to some observer, nor does it have to be accepted on blind faith, nor can it be gotten by everyone without changing goggles. Observer-Dependent Epistemology is simply a fancy name for trying to change oneself to see what one cannot see right now. What we see is not common for all observers. It is limited to those who want to see, will endeavor to change their goggles of filtering, and use them to see in a new way.

Before we see something new, we learn to see whatever we see right now in a new way. Some new ways are better while others are worse. The better way of seeing predicts and explains more data than the worse way of seeing. Therefore, we can also ladder the better and worse ways of seeing by the capacity to predict and explain more or less data. We always introduce a new way of seeing based on the fact that it explains and predicts more data. We establish that this new way of seeing is already better with the currently available data before we ask anyone to pursue this way of seeing to its logical conclusion. The Observer-Dependent Epistemology therefore moves slowly unlike the leaps of faith made in speculative sciences and religions. We are not interested in quick wins. We want to establish that a new way of seeing is a pervasively better way of seeing and the old ways of seeing must therefore be discarded.

Vedic epistemology is a trust-but-verify system of knowledge. It is not the Abrahamic religious system of trust without verification. It is not the modern scientific system of verification without trust. Bundling Vedic epistemology with the epistemologies of modern science or Abrahamic religions is a fundamental category error. Without knowing all the categories, we are likely to make such erroneous judgments.

**Effects of Vedic Realist Epistemology**

A grounding in the theories of reality and epistemology in the Vedic system is necessary to understand all other descriptions. These include the descriptions of Prakṛti in Sāñkhya that present the nature of sense objects, senses, mind, intellect, and even further personal levels of reality. The basic principle is that we have to understand everything in three ways if we want to understand it completely. These three ways can be called perceiving, generating, and filtering, based on what we have discussed so far.

Let’s begin with our senses. The senses generate dreams while we are sleeping, and they can generate hallucinations while we are awake. The senses also perceive the truth. Finally, the senses filter the reality to conform to their nature. Every sense cannot perceive everything even if it exists. We don’t have to be shocked by this idea. Thermometers cannot measure speed and speedometers cannot measure temperature. Material instruments are capable of measuring specific properties only. Each instrument is capable of measuring to a different level of accuracy. We cannot use a meter to measure millimeter lengths. All material instruments spuriously generate measurement results even when nothing is being measured. They are just like the hallucinations and dreams in the senses. Therefore, even ordinary instruments measure reality, generate reality, and filter reality. Our senses also perceive, generate, and filter. The difference is only that senses deal in qualities while instruments deal in quantities.

Similarly, the mind generates thoughts, which we call creativity. The mind perceives thoughts in other minds, which we call sixth sense intuition. The mind filters reality to what it is interested in. Everyone is not interested in science, philosophy, sports, music, movies, or literature. Those things may exist in the world but the mind filters them to see what it is interested in. The mind blocks ideas alien to its culture, religion, and society. Due to the filtering of the mind, people may not understand each other. Modern psychology uses the idea of unconscious bias to describe how people attend to what they want to attend to, ignore what they are not interested in, and interpret others based on their preconceived notions.

Every element or component in Sāñkhya possesses this tripartite nature of senses and the mind. The intellect generates truths, perceives truths, and filters truths. The ego generates intentions, perceives intentions, and filters intentions. The mahattattva, or the moral sense, generates values, perceives values, and filters values. The tripartite nature of senses and minds pervades the entire psyche.

The same tripartite nature of perceiving, generating, and filtering exists in the elements that constitute the gross body. Sāñkhya describes how in the process of the creation of the world, the Ether generates Air, the Air generates Fire, Fire generates Water, and Water generates Earth. It then describes how in the process of the dissolution of the world, Water absorbs Earth, Fire absorbs Water, Air absorbs Fire, and Ether absorbs Air. What is not discussed, but can be understood just like the other elements, is that these five elements also filter other elements due to which elementary properties don’t get mixed up into one complex property. The complex property can always be partitioned into elementary properties. The taste, touch, smell, sound, and sight don’t get mixed into one property. They remain distinct.

In physics, the filtering property of matter is seen in the mutual independence of physical properties such as mass and charge. Applying an electromagnetic field to a mass particle doesn’t change the mass and applying a gravitational field to a charge particle doesn’t change the charge. A charge particle moves in a gravitational field as if moving in empty space and a mass particle moves in an electromagnetic field as if moving in empty space. The filtering property of matter is therefore taken for granted in modern physics. What doesn’t exist in modern physics is the perceiving and generating property of matter.

Sāñkhya emphasizes the perceiving and generating properties of matter and the filtering property is simply taken for granted. It should be noted that these three properties of matter are not mutually independent in Sāñkhya. That is, when properties are mixed, they don’t always remain independent. However, that is a deeper issue that we will not get into here as it will distract us from our focus.

The tripartite way of looking at everything appears in three varied descriptions of the chitta, when it is described as (a) the generator of waves just like an ocean, (b) the reflector of images just like a mirror, and (c) a blocker of an observer’s vision just like dirty goggles. Chitta perplexes people because we are not accustomed to anything that can be simultaneously called an ocean, a mirror, and goggles. However, this is not so perplexing if we know that everything is a perceiver, a generator, and a filter. The chitta is an ocean because it is a generator. The chitta is a mirror because it is a perceiver. The chitta is goggles because it is a filter. A tripartite approach is necessary to understand the meaning of chitta.

**The Three Modes of Sāñkhya Philosophy**

The basic thesis of why one thing is three things at once has deep roots in the rest of Vedic philosophy. Prakṛti, for example, is described as comprising three qualities or guna called Sattva, Rajas, and Tamas. Sattva-guna contributes to the perceptual capacity, Rajo-guna contributes to the generative capacity, and Tamo-guna contributes to the filtering capacity. For example, a mind with Tamo-guna prominence filters everything and is therefore said to be asleep. A mind with Rajo-guna prominence is preoccupied with self-generated thoughts and is said to be distracted. A mind with Sattva-guna prominence is awake but silent and is said to be an alert perceiver. The three modes are mutual opposites of each other.

- Sattva is neither asleep nor distracted, hence opposed to both tamas and rajas
- Rajas is neither asleep nor alert, hence opposed to both tamas, and sattva
- Tamas is neither awake nor distracted, hence opposed to both sattva and rajas

By different prominences of sattva, rajas, and tamas, many kinds of sense objects, senses, minds, intellects, egos, and moral senses are produced, with different capacities for perceiving, generating, and filtering. A combination of these capacities then becomes numerous mind-body complexes of a person, which are then aggregated into different realms like birds of the same feather flocking together.

The logic that describes the senses, mind, intellect, ego, moral sense, and then the body compatible with the observer’s cognitive apparatus, is Observer-Dependent Logic. The logic that describes the body independent of the observer’s cognitive apparatus is Observer-Independent Logic. Aristotelian logic is Observer-Independent Logic. The logic involved in Sāñkhya is Observer-Dependent Logic. The former type of logic describes how something is either inside or outside something else. The latter type of logic deals with how the world is both inside and outside the senses. The world inside the senses is at once one with the senses and distinct from them. We can call it non-binary logic.

We cannot conceptualize the three modes of Prakṛti in Aristotelian binary logic because (a) one thing has to be just one thing and not three things, (b) one thing cannot be opposite things at once, and (c) nothing can be neither opposite such as asleep and awake. Two opposite things can be added to each other without canceling each other. The uncancelled sum of opposites can generate those opposites. The non-binary logical properties of the three modes of Prakṛti are inconceivable within binary logical thinking. That doesn’t mean they are inconceivable per se. The problem of conceivability is created by Aristotelian logic.

**The Three Aspects of Vedic Personhood**

The three qualities of Sāñkhya are not the only tripartite ways of thinking in the Vedic system. There is also the tripartite description of a person as sat-chit-ānanda. Everything in the Vedic system is these three properties of personhood, which means that everything is a person. For example, based on this tripartite distinction of personhood, whatever people called “matter” in modern science or ordinary thinking is described in three ways as Śakti, Prakṛti, and Māyā. Śakti is the sat aspect of material nature, Prakṛti is the chit aspect of material nature, and Māyā is the ānanda aspect of material nature.

The Śākta texts emphasize Śakti, the Vaiṣṇava texts emphasize Prakṛti, and the Śaiva texts emphasize Māyā. This emphasis is relative. No text excludes the discussion of the other two. But the subject matter is so complex that they have been separated into different schools quite like the departments of physics, chemistry, and biology are separated in modern academics. Practically nobody at present says that the departments of physics, chemistry, and biology must be giving contradictory theories of reality. But almost everyone at present says that Śāktism, Śaivism, and Vaiṣṇavism must be incompatible schools.

In modern academics, dozens of departments study different secular subjects. One department of religion studies all the religions together. Within the religious study departments, the study of the Vedic system remains almost an afterthought, a box that you have to tick to prevent the appearance of bias. No religious studies department has to date spent any effort in trying to understand the different concepts of personhood. They take the Western definition of personhood—as someone with rights and capable of negotiating contracts—as the only theory of personhood. Since rights and contracts are the subject matter of politics and law, therefore, theories of personhood come out of law and political science departments rather than religious studies departments. Religions simply assume the legal and political definition of personhood, rather than delve into the spiritual meaning of personhood. God, in Abrahamic religions, for example, is defined as a person who enters into a legal contract with the whole of humanity, in contrast to humans who generally have limited contracts with fewer persons.

Given the abject neglect of the study of personhood and capitulation to a political and legal dogma about personhood that originated in Jewish Law, there is little understanding of how a person is defined differently in the Vedic system. Then the idea that everything in the cosmos is a person because it originates in a Supreme, Complete, and Original Person, also escapes notice and discussion.

Rivers, mountains, oceans, lands, and planets are persons in the Vedic system. Since a deity made of stone, wood, or metal can embody a Supreme Person, therefore, seemingly dead things can also be persons. In the Ramayana, Ahilya was cursed to become a stone until she was touched by Lord Ramachandra’s feet. The Vāstu Śāstra personifies buildings and other man-made things through the presence of a Vāstu Puruṣa. Nature and Earth have historically been called mothers in all traditional cultures. Every noun in Sanskrit has a gender (masculine or feminine). There are separate word-form tables for these genders and nouns with different genders follow different word-form tables. Essentially, even geology and oceanography are sub-departments of the department of personhood.

Impersonalism of nature is the gift of Abrahamic religions. They removed deities, demigods, karma, and reincarnation from pagan religions and depersonalized nature. The impersonalism of modern science was developed from the impersonalism of Abrahamic religions. It pervaded the world via colonization and became an accepted norm. Abrahamic religions also developed legal and political doctrines of personhood. Since they denied rights and contracts to trees, animals, birds, and fishes, therefore, nature was depersonalized through mathematical laws in modern science. Without a firm alternative idea of personhood, there is no capacity to understand how traditional cultures personalized nature.

That alternative requires us to reinvoke the principle that one thing is three things. Sat is the relational capacity in persons. Chit is the cognitive and conative capacity in persons. Ānanda is the intentional, emotional, and willing capacity in all persons. A person can be described as a complex of will, intention, emotion, cognition, conation, and relation. If someone has a problem with this terminology, they can use the shorter terms sat-chit-ānanda. There are no rights or contracts. Nobody is born with a Bill of Rights in their hands. They are born with a capacity for will, intention, emotion, cognition, conation, and relation. Therefore, to say that everything is personal is to say that everything has the capacity for will, intention, emotion, cognition, conation, and relation. That capacity may be diminished in things that seem non-living but it is not absent. The stark distinction between living and non-living doesn’t exist.

Sāñkhya elaborates personhood into 24 components. Tantra extends these 24 components into 36 components. The grossest of these elements called Earth, Water, Fire, Air, and Ether are embodiments of smell, taste, sight, touch, and sound which means that even the grossest of material things exist for perception, they were generated from the senses, and are filtered to varying extents by different perceivers. The observer is central to this understanding. This was not a strange idea in traditional cultures that personalized nature. It is strange to Aristotelian logic, Abrahamic religions, and modern science since they have depersonalized nature for the last 2,500 years.

A person doesn’t produce anything impersonal. But a person can depersonalize a person by modeling him as a substance, object, thing, particle, wave, field, equation, idea, force, process, and many such speculative constructs. All such constructs prove inadequate to model a person correctly and completely. Each of these constructs is false, illusory, and deceptive. And yet, in the material world, this is the primary way in which everyone thinks, which hides the true nature of reality—a person. The collection of all such illusory, false, and deceptive constructs is māyā. The material world is māyā because everyone uses one or more of the speculative constructs different from the true ontology of personhood.

Māyā is not an ontological truth. Māyā is an epistemological delusion. That delusion is created in the process of trying to depersonalize a person. The models created by such depersonalization are not true. Hence, they are “that which is not”. To the extent that this delusion exists within us, we can say that we are deluded about the nature of reality. The world is not false. Our conception of the world is false. Reality is not impersonal. But our modeling of reality is impersonal. Since the impersonal model can be falsified (as it fails to model a person), Māyā does not eternally delude anyone. Escaping Māyā is knowing the truth and ending the suffering created by delusion. The theory of salvation is not separate from the theory of the world. The two theories are identical.

Depersonalization of reality begins from a deep-seated egotism that makes a person selfish. A selfish person depersonalizes other persons and models them as substances, objects, things, particles, waves, fields, equations, ideas, forces, processes, and so on. A selfless person removes such models and sees the person as he is. Thus, everyone will not personalize reality. The egotistic, selfish, and individualistic persons will depersonalize others while the humble, selfless, and altruistic persons will personalize others. This is also Observer-Dependent Epistemology that allows personal choice of models.

Those who depersonalize others will be flocked with like-minded persons who will depersonalize them in turn. Those who personalize others will be flocked with like-minded persons who will personalize them in turn. Nobody wants to be depersonalized. They just want to depersonalize others. But they must be flocked with like-minded people to correct their flawed ideas. When a person is flocked with those who depersonalize him, he realizes the flaws of his thinking. The Vedic system therefore has no problem with any ideology because it says—you can live with people of your ilk. Of course, it warns—choose your ideology carefully because you will come face-to-face with it.

When the basic model of reality is personhood, then the contradiction between Realism and Idealism disappears. After all, there is something other than me, which is a person. Therefore, realism is just like idealism. This is not equivalence with the Western ideas of Realism or Idealism. In the West, Realism is a multi-observer theory and Idealism is a single-observer theory. Western Realism is reductionistic, materialistic, and mechanistic but Western Idealism is not. The Vedic system violates these categories. It is a multi-observer instead of a single-observer theory. Therefore, it is like Western Realism. But it is not reductionistic, materialistic, or mechanistic because both observer and observed are persons. Therefore, it is like Western Idealism.

**Overview of the Problem and Its Solution**

Aristotle systematized Western philosophy by dividing it into epistemology, ontology, and logic. Initially, Abrahamic religions and then modern science adopted Aristotle’s system of systematized philosophy. Their ontology is observer-independent realism, their epistemology is based either on revelation or reason and observation, and their logic is binary. The Vedic ontology is observer-dependent realism, its epistemology is reason, observation, and revelation, and its logic is non-binary. Since neither logic, nor epistemology, nor ontology match, therefore, it is impossible to understand the Vedic system if anyone carries over the vestiges of Aristotelian philosophy, Abrahamic religions, or modern science.

But since the Aristotelian system of philosophy is deeply ingrained in modern science and Abrahamic religions, and the assumptions of this system have been globalized during colonization, hence, practically nobody is free of this thinking at present. They carry its assumptions consciously or unconsciously, try to judge and fit the Vedic system into their assumptions, and fail to comprehend almost everything.

Since they cannot understand how one thing is many things at once, they see conflicts between different Vedic schools that describe the same thing in many different ways. Since non-binary logic appears self-contradictory in binary logical thinking, therefore, they assume that the Vedic system can only be accepted on faith. Since Vedic Realism is something in between Western Realism and Idealism, they cannot subsume it either under Realism or under Idealism. Since Vedic epistemology integrates reason, observation, and revelation, which are separated by Abrahamic religions and modern science, therefore, they are unable to categorize the Vedic system as either science or religion. Since the Vedic idea of personhood is spiritual rather than legal and political, therefore, followers of legal and political definitions of personhood are unable to understand how everything in the universe is modeled as a person.

The problems are easily identified but they are harder to diagnose and fix. The diagnosis of the problem is that the Vedic system has nothing in common with Aristotelian logic, Abrahamic religions, or modern science. The fix to that problem is that there is an alternative ontology, epistemology, and logic under which the apparent problems disappear. Therefore, the problems are simply the result of trying to fit the Vedic system under the categories created by Aristotelian logic, Abrahamic religions, or modern science, rather than studying it as an independent system with its own epistemology, ontology, and logic.

The virtue of studying Aristotelian logic, Abrahamic religions, or modern science is simply that we can understand their flaws and failures and irrefutably establish the need for an alternative. However, the study of Aristotelian logic, Abrahamic religions, or modern science is not going to make our study of the Vedic system easier because we have to leapfrog the previous systems given their proven failures.
