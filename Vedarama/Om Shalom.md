## Foreword

Yehudah ha-Levi, a Jewish poet/philosopher from the Medieval period, wrote an important theological treatise in which he compared Judaism to other well-established world religions. The book, entitled *al-Kuzari* (“Book of the Khazars, or Book of Refutation and in Defense of the Despised Faith”), has since become a classic, and Jewish philosophers, to this day, refer to it as *the* authority on Jewish interreligious dialogue. *Al-Kuzari* is divided into five parts and takes the form of a conversation between a pagan (khazar) king who is converted to Judaism and the Jewish sage who gives him instruction. Representatives of Christianity, Islam, and Karaism are also included, and the dialogue that ensues serves to define Judaism as the preeminent path of spiritual awakening, at least for followers of the Bible.

*Om Shalom: Judaism and Krishna Conscious-ness* is similar in content. While its tone is more ecumenical and its conclusion more nonsectarian than *al-Kuzari,* it shows Judaism in dialogue with the Hindu *bhakti* religion, better known as “Hare Krishna.” Both Judaism and “Krishna conscious-ness,” as it is sometimes called, are analyzed with great verve and enthusiasm, with sobriety and scholarship. Consequently, there is at least one likely outcome of reading this work: basic knowledge of both religious traditions. Moreover, a deeper appreciation of these traditions awaits those who ponder the inner significance of these conversations.

Satyarāja Dāsa (Steven J. Rosen), the initiator of these talks, comes from a Jewish background and has been studying Krishna consciousness—academically and as a practitioner—for the last seventeen years. Feeling the need to resolve the tenets of his acquired faith with that of his birth, he proceeded to interview rabbis and Jew-ish theologians from New York, Israel, Spain, and France, including one who lived among the Jews of India. This was Rabbi Jacob N. Shimmel, a *halakhic* scholar originally hailing from Prague, Czechoslovakia.

A rabbi for over twenty years, Shimmel was the perfect candidate to enter into dialogue with Satyaraja Dasa, especially because of his working knowledge of Indian philosophy—an uncommon phenomenon in the Jewish community. As Founder and Director of the Talmud and Torah Beth Rabbinical Association, Rabbi Shimmel has authored many books, including *Judaism and Other Faiths*, *Jewish Eschatology*, and a commentary on the classic devotional text, *Hag ha-Samahot* (“Festival of Rejoicing”).

As readers become absorbed in the pages of *Om Shalom*, they will be impressed with the clarity and precision that Rabbi Shimmel and Satyaraja bring to their discussion. The outcome is an educational and enjoyable piece of literature—a composite of many months of recorded conversations. Shimmel and Satyaraja have one major theme in common: a passionate pursuance of the Truth. If the reader catches a fragment of this mood, all parties concerned will consider the dialogue worthwhile. Om Shalom!

—The publishers.

## Introduction

*By Sylvia Rosen*

Although I'm not a practicing Jew, I'm proud of my Jewish heritage. And so seventeen years ago, when my son, Steven (Satyaraja Dasa), told me that he wanted to join the Hare Krishna movement, I was shocked and quite upset. What could “Hare Krishna” offer him that his own religion, Judaism, could not?

Steven's brother and sister had never had the religious calling, nor did my husband or I. But Steven was always a seeker. He started reading philosophical books and religious classics from a very early age. He was almost mystically drawn to them. So, too, was he drawn to the big questions: Why am I here? Is there a God? Is He a person? And if so, what does He look like?

I'm not saying that Steven wasn't a normal, active boy—he had many friends and was always running around with them. But his true love was to sit down with a good book or to engage in philosophical discussion. Steven was always asking “why?”.

His inordinate search for “the truth” had, by 1972, led him to the International Society for Krishna Consciousness (ISKCON). I knew that sentimental arguments wouldn't sway him, nor would pulling rank, as his mother. If he had indeed made the decision to join, he must have had good, well thought-out reasons. But as a mother, and especially as a *Jewish* mother, I was concerned. I wanted to make sure that he knew exactly what he was doing.

My first impulse was to challenge him: “If you want to become serious about religion, why not embrace your own, Judaism?” I was pretty sure that I knew his answer, though. Only two weeks earlier we had had a lengthy discussion about “the *shabbes* goy”—a non-Jew who is hired on the Sabbath to do things that Jewish people are forbidden to do.

Jews, on the Sabbath, are not allowed to turn light switches on and off. The reasoning goes something like this: We are forbidden to work on the Sabbath. Building a fire is work. Turning on an electric light is tantamount to building a fire, for by doing so one gets the benefit of light. So turning on an electric light switch constitutes work! I have to admit—the logic here is quite elusive. True, an electric light is like fire in that it gives us light. But turning one on is easy while making a fire could be considered work.

The logic (?) extends further. Elevators work by electricity. Therefore, you can't use them on the Sabbath. Here we go again—electricity equals fire equals work. The ironic thing is this: if my son wants to visit me on the fourteenth floor (which is where I live), and it happens to be the Sabbath, he'll have to use the stairs instead of the elevator. When he walks into the apartment after stepping up all of those flights—he looks like he “worked” quite a bit to me!\*

Most contemporary Jews, I think, find this tradition wanting. But what's worse is that many go so far as to hire a non-Jew to do the “dirty work.” My own father used to pay a young boy to come into our home on the Sabbath just to turn the light switch on and off. Steven found this sort of hypocrisy distasteful, as did I. But the *shabbes* goy syndrome is hardly representative of true Judaism. I came to find out that Steven knew this. And when I finally confronted him about his decision to join the Hare Krishna movement, his answer had little to do with the *shabbes* goy or the exaggerated Sabbath observance.

Steven's answer reflected not so much what Judaism *didn't* have, but what Krishna consciousness *did* have. In Krishna consciousness he felt that he had actually found a way to reach the goal of Judaism—and that of all religion. The word “Jew,” Steven told me, is derived from *yehudah*, which means “one who exalts the Lord.” Originally, then, a Jew was merely one who glorified God—this is according to the deeper implication of the word, divorced from its ethnocentric connotations. But in order to glorify the Lord properly, Steven suggested, one must look toward a practical—living—example, a person who is one hundred percent dedicated to God.

Anticipating my next question, Steven assured me that such pure devotees must exist within the Jewish tradition—but that he, Steven, could not find them. However, when he met His Divine Grace A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, the founder of the Hare Krishna movement, he immediately felt that he had found the superlative example of one who “exalts the Lord.” In fact, Swami Prabhupada's whole teaching centered around glorifying God by chanting His many names.

Steven felt that this was Judaism in the strict-est sense of the term. It was Judaism as it should be—beyond mundane ethnicity and unwarranted sectarianism. To hear him tell it, he had found the Absolute Truth and the essence of spiritual-ity. I couldn't agree with him in totality, but what he said did make sense.

I remember that as I listened to Steven's explanation, I became proud. Maybe I was just being a sentimental mother. But in his soliloquy on Krishna consciousness he didn't contradict the basic tenets of Judaism in any way. On the contrary, as he explained to me that the Vedic tradition was originally monotheistic and that “Krishna” was one name for the Lord—although He has millions of names—I began to see that the teachings of Krishna consciousness very much paralleled the teachings of Judaism.

One analogy that Steven used on that particular day stands out in my mind. It made his choice to become a devotee seem clearer. “The Vedic literature can be compared to an unabridged dictionary,” he said, “and the other great religious writings can be compared to so many pocket dictionaries. All of the books are in ultimate agreement—but the unabridged dictionary holds more information, giving every nuance of the word described. While the Bible tells us that God is great, the *Vedas* give us details of His greatness. While the Talmud offers some indication of 'the world to come,' the *Vedas* give elaborate descriptions of the Kingdom of God.” Who was I to argue? I had never made a genuine comparative study. He had obviously done his homework.

Still, it seemed to me that there were discrepancies between Judaism and Krishna conscious-ness. But Steven insisted that minor differences exist between religions due to cultural orientations—but that the essence remains the same. He pointed out that there also seems to be discrepancies between a primary math course and that of a college student. In elementary mathematics one is taught that larger numbers cannot be subtracted from smaller numbers. And this premise should be accepted by all who study basic arithmetic. In advanced mathematics, however, you indeed learn that you *can* subtract larger numbers from those that are smaller: these are called negative numbers.

Steven's point was well-taken. Analogically, he was telling me that prophets and sages reveal religious truth selectively, according to time, place, and circumstance. And, on minor points, you may find that one prophet deprecates a certain activity while another, from another tradition, endorses it. The essence of developing love for God, however, remains the same. Any religion that leads to this end, Steven said, should be considered genuine.

Although he convinced me *philosophically*, I still worried for him, as I guess mothers tend to do. In 1975, he was initiated by Swami Prabhupada, and he was given the name “Satyarāja Dāsa.” To this day, I cannot properly pronounce it. But in spite of many of my early worries and personal doubts, I see that my son, seventeen years later, is happy, healthy, and productive. He has a lovely home and satisfying work. He's finally a *mench*! Who could ask for more?

The Jewish tradition—ethnicity and all—is part of my life. It always was and always will be. But I've come to fully accept Steven's devotion to Krishna. It's his life—and he's living it well. His being a devotee doesn't seem to get in the way of his Jewishness, either. Actually, it augments it, since the essence of Judaism is to love and honor God with all of one's heart, mind, and soul. And, as a devotee, it is this to which Steven has dedicated his life. In conclusion, for both Steven and myself, our Jewish background will always have a place in our hearts—but, then again, so will Krishna.

Now...there are four ingredients, if you will, that give our eternal relationship with Krishna a de-finite shape. They enhance the *sthayi-bhava*. Rupa Goswami ingeniously compares *rasa*, or the spiritual relationship, to *rasala*, or a kind of sweet drink. The *sthayi-bhava* is compared to the yogurt, the substance of the drink, and the *vi-bhava*, *anu-bhava*, *satvika-bhava*, and *sanchari-bhava*, are compared to sugar, pepper and various condiments that give the drink its special flavor. These must be combined according to a particular recipe. One takes the guidance of the spiritual master, the scriptures, and the saintly persons. Although by this stage one is functioning according to his natural impulse, his natural spiritual relationship.

**Rabbi Shimmel:** So the awe and reverence devotees, those with an inclination toward Viddhi-bhakti, are not devoid of love of God. Because, in general, it would seem that theirs would be a relationship based primarily on fear of transgression. I think you even mentioned that.

**Satyarāja Dāsa:** Yes. **Rabbi Shimmel:** So this sense of closeness—seeing God as one's “equal”—is seen in a loving way. Total spiritual love.

**Satyarāja Dāsa:** Oh, yes. It is from closeness that *pranaya* develops. Intense closeness. One could not think of becoming abusive. It is spiritual, transcendental to mundane conceptions.

Anything is alright, as long as it is my Lord's desire. Then *anu-raga*, when the object of love, Krishna, is always before one's eyes. One can only see Krishna. This is the stage—the coveted goal. This, of course, leads to *bhava* proper—the original devotional sentiment. The emotion. Exhilaration and relish of the topmost order. Bliss. *Ananda*. The life of love. Total spiritual love. *Bhava*. On the inner platform it increases to *mahabhava*. But this is rare. Only for the most confidential associates. Radhika is the embodiment of *mahabhava*.

**Rabbi Shimmel:** So this is the highest...

**Satyarāja Dāsa:** Yes. But even this can be subdivided into more precise categories. *Mahabhava* is either *rudha*, the dawning stage, or *adhirudha*, which is more intense. And then this, *adhirudha*, is further divided into *modana* and *madana*. Separation. *Viraha-bhakti*. Really, it is endless.

Also, Bhaktivinode Thakur, in an article entitled “Nitya-nanda Suryodoy,” which was published in the 1800s, in his *Sajjana-toshani* magazine—he predicted that soon a time is coming when the chant-ing of Krishna's name will be heard in England, France, Russia, Germany, and America. Srila Prabhupada brought this to fruition. There is also one document from the fifteenth century. It is called the *Bhagavat Mahatmya.* In this book, Narada, the great sage, has a conversation with *bhakti* personified. In the course of this conversation, *bhakti* says, *idam sthanam parityajya videsham gamyate maya*. Literally, this means: “I will leave this country, India, and go abroad!” The same text informs us that this personified *bhakti* will go to South India and then leave to go a-broad after staying in Vrindavan. This is exactly what Prabhupada did. All scholars agree that this *Bhagavat Mahatmya* is a fifteenth-century text. And whether or not one can accommodate such a conception as the personification of *bhakti*, one thing is clear: in this text you find an early prediction of *bhakti* going to the West. This was clearly done by Prabhupada. He, and he alone, took *bhakti* to Western shores, where she waits for her appropriate reception.

## Appendix: The glories of the Holy Name

*It is the contention of both Rabbi Shimmel and Satyarāja Dāsa that all disparity between Judaism and Krishna consciousness can be minimized by the sincere recitation of the holy name. Despite various differences, this is one thing that practitioners of both religions can wholeheartedly engage in, and in this way join together, resolving superficial discrepancies.*

*Perhaps such spiritual camaraderie will never come to pass. But both traditions do exhort adherents to chant the holy name above any other spiritual practice. So, at least on this principle, there is an option for peace and harmony, for a concerted spiritual effort. This belief in the superexcellence of chanting is grounded in the Bible and in the Vedic literature as well. In an attempt to establish this fact, then, we conclude this book by presenting a series of scriptural quotations that show the primacy and efficacy of the chanting process. This listing may serve to induce sincere souls to find a common platform for engaging in God consciousness.*
