## Author’s Note

Ramayana – The Game of Life series presents an authentic rendition of the Ramayana in an absolutely modern and riveting manner. This book promises to enlighten loyalists and entertain modernists. Interspersing the story of the Ramayana with thought-provoking contemporary life lessons, the series guarantees satisfaction – a volume that can prove to be a lifelong companion. As you flip the pages, one thought is sure to cross your mind: never has the Ramayana been so applicable to my life!

The book series, while holding Valmiki muni’s Ramayana as a lighthouse, sensitively weaves in analogies and poetic ornamentation from the Kamba Ramayana and Tulsi Das’ Ramcharitmanas. In addition, the series also brings in folk tales called Loka Pramana that are in philosophical alignment with Valmiki’s Ramayana. These folk tales bring in background stories that enhance the understanding of the main story as well as its various characters and their moods. Without the knowledge of these tales, the original story would be much less dramatic and comprehensible. Moreover, the series also provides deep insights from the great acharyas who have studied the Ramayana thoroughly. Even if it is filled with so many elements, I have made sure that modern readers do not find it inapplicable or irrelevant in their personal life. This series shows readers how these epics have immediate relevance in our life, if presented properly and understood deeply.

Of course, I would like to state here that I have taken some creative liberties to keep the narration riveting and gripping. But rest assured, these creative liberties are minimal and do not, in any way, impede or supersede the original author’s work or the divinity of the epic and its characters. If anything, the creative liberties enhance the joy of reading this great work of literature.

Over the last few years, I have presented a series of seminars based on Ramayana –The Game of Life, where I expand on the innumerable life lessons presented in the books with a lot of practical examples and anecdotes. These seminars have made the book even more user-friendly and relevant to the thousands who have heard them. These seminars are freely available on YouTube.

Radiate Confidence essentially expounds the Sundara Kanda of the Ramayana. This is the most sought after and popular section of the epic. Many people prefer to read and re-read only this section. This is the most thrilling book of the entire series as it talks about the beginning of Hanuman’s heroic exploits. Every ‘Kanda’ or section of the Ramayana spans many months or, in some cases, many years. The Bala Kanda covers a period of almost 13 years, the Ayodhya Kanda, almost a year, the Aranya Kanda, stretches over roughly 13 years, while the Kishkinda Kanda describes a period of a little over two months. But the Sundara Kanda, the fifth book in the Game of Life series, covers just five days; and more than three-quarters of the book covers a two-day period when Hanuman leapt to Lanka.

Every character is a hero in his or her own right and has the power to arrest the attention of the reader in different ways, at multiple levels in various situations. The reader will be introduced to many new characters who will enhance their understanding of life and human psychology. This book is about Hanuman learning the art of “being patient and finding success,” as the subtitle suggests. Through the story of Hanuman, readers are allowed to explore their own journey and learn how to deal with obstacles on their path to success.

## Chapter 1

Flight Across the Wild Waters

between the devil and the deep sea

The energy levels had all but flatlined, but enthusiasm was at its peak. The excruciating month-long search mission had sapped every ounce of strength they had, but a newfound hope had lifted their spirits and rejuvenated them. As the thousands of vanaras scampered through the thick woods, they applauded each other with loud hoots and shouts. Raucous cries echoed all around, and all the faces lit up with exuberant smiles.1

Then suddenly, the loud crack of thunder startled the power-packed army to a screeching halt. The bewildering boom was all it took to silence the rapturous troop. The sound resembled the rumble of countless battle chariots. Or was it like the roar of thousands of beasts together?

As the army stood frozen at the outskirts of the forest, their breathing became heavier – thick air gushing in and out of their noses and mouths like the giant waves of the enormous ocean sprawled out in front of them. The vanaras exchanged anxious glances, as the nervous army edged its way toward its biggest obstacle yet: the colossal ocean.

The rogue waves seemed to dare them on. The wild breakers kept lashing the coast time and again, forcing the spirit-battered vanara army to step back. The waters spread far beyond their range of vision. Although several stretches appeared very still, the vanaras knew those tranquil waters were cradling the ocean’s deepest rage. Conquering this surging monster seemed impossible.2

One by one, the vanara soldiers dropped to their knees, defeated and demoralized. Angad, the chief of the southern search party, stood up to motivate his men. It was his duty and the need of the hour. But the roaring waters drowned out the positive vibes of his lofty speech and bullied the army into silent submission. The long silences transitioned into sleep, and sleep dragged in nightmares of watery graves.3

Angad woke up early and persevered with his motivational talk. Eventually, he goaded his men to reveal their personal best leaping statistics. He figured that would not only lighten up the tension but also help find the one who could leap all the way across the ocean.

The record disclosure (and bragging) began with one sheepish vanara admitting that he could jump only a mile. That confession motivated some more to speak up. One by one, the vanaras started declaring their best, until one stood up and said, “Five hundred miles. I could leap as far as five hundred miles in one go.” Everyone turned toward the leader Angad. Surely, he could outdo that. “I could easily do eight hundred,” Angad confirmed. The beach erupted with rousing cheers, and the vanaras began to dance with joy. Now with that said, the target destination, Lanka, was within reach. It was exactly eight hundred miles across the ocean! On seeing this sudden surge of energy among the soldiers, Angad realized the enormity of his responsibility. So he cautioned his army, “…but I am not confident I will be able to return.”4

The “but” had dragged them down just as soon as the “eight hundred” had lifted them up. Before Angad could even finish his sentence, everyone had slumped back into the sand. All the vanaras pinned his lack of confidence to faltering energy and ability. But the wise old Jambavan knew better. He knew that Angad’s skepticism about returning had nothing to do with fear of failure. In fact, it was because of an entirely different kind of fear – the kind no leader would dare to share with his subordinates or admit to himself.5 It was the kind of fear only a trained eye such as Jambavan’s could spot.

Angad wasn’t scared of leaping into and from the land beyond the ocean. Instead, he was afraid of falling right into temptation. As the son of Vali, succumbing to temptation was in Angad’s blood. He knew Lanka was a place of illusion and desire. He wasn’t sure if he could focus on the mission amid so many desirable distractions. Besides, he was unsure if being exposed to the razzle-dazzle would even make him want to leave. Angad and Jambavan exchanged glances. While the leader chose not to explain, the wise one decided not to reveal.6

Instead, Jambavan thought it best to speak about his own strengths. “Back in the day, I could do so much. But that’s all in the past now. Thanks to this frail old frame, I can’t even hop over a tiny puddle now, let alone leaping across the eight hundred-mile ocean!”7

Jambavan turned around, searching for a ray of hope among the listless faces. Soon his eyes rested on the one who hadn’t yet declare his leap figures. He had instead chosen a quiet spot for himself and decided against partaking in the pointless discussion. He sat on a rock facing the ocean, his eyes closed in meditation.

Jambavan walked up to the lone warrior, put his arm around his chiseled shoulder, and sat next to him. No words were exchanged for a while – the awkward silence shattered only by the sound of the waves curling over their feet and rushing back to the horizon. It seemed almost as if the ocean was luring them into a challenge.

Jambavan knew it was about time they took up that challenge. So he stood up to face the sinewy warrior, his eyes surveying this beacon of hope. He chose his words carefully. “Hanuman, you are the son of Vayu. Do you not remember shrinking the distance between the earth and the heavens to micro moments? Do you not remember as an infant seeking to catch the sun, mistaking it for a ripe fruit? Do you not remember swallowing the sun and casting Rahu into your mouth because he was about to eclipse the sun? The universe itself will never forget the day, for the king of the heavens had to intervene to undo the catastrophe your innocent hunger had caused. His thunderbolt had not only smashed your chin, but also hit your father’s heart. The subsequent tumble could not crush your enthusiasm; instead, it embellished you with blessings from many divine beings. You arose from the fall a much stronger and more vibrant entity.”

Jambavan’s words spurred the silent hero. His eyes began to sparkle. The more he heard about his exploits, the brighter his eyes shone.8

The crestfallen vanaras who had been sitting with their heads buried in their hands perked their ears and looked up at the two warriors and exchanged glances. There was hope, after all.

Jambavan went on. “You are Hanuman, the son of the wind god, the very wind that carries the breath of life. So, breathe life into our hopeless souls by storming into the land where Sita is captive.

Uthishta, Hanuman! Rise, Hanuman! If you could touch the sun with just one jump, surely, the ocean is a tiny puddle for you. Cross these rough waters and deliver us from this hopelessness.9

Hanuman was charged. Jambavan’s words had struck a deep chord. Something he had long forgotten came back to him. He rose slowly, stretching his limbs.

Watching him, the vanaras straightened up, too. Hanuman’s arms seemed to extend all the way up to the sky. “RAAAMAAAA.” He cried out to his Lord and Master. As the intensity of his full-throated call increased, so did the size of his body. Hanuman’s body began swelling up with the strength and power of Rama. Within moments, Hanuman had become this colossal being swatting clouds with his long tail. He pumped up his chest and let out a loud roar that ripped through the vicinity. His gaping mouth seemed like a mighty lion’s den. The vanaras were thrilled with this transformation. They had experienced such low levels of motivation that Hanuman, who was now looming large, seemed like the only one who could elevate hope and infuse courage in them.

Their friend was now this ever-growing behemoth figure. Every muscle in his body had expanded to extraordinary proportions. He was now ten times his size. It was unlike anything the vanaras had seen before. As they watched, Hanuman continued to invoke Rama incessantly with his eyes closed. By the time he opened his eyes, he was fifty times his size.

Hanuman was now mission ready. His eyes darted across the surroundings. He had spotted his launch pad – the tall and mighty Mahendra mountain. Three furtive leaps, and he was at the summit. As he stood on the peak scanning the vast ocean, it seemed as if there was one mountain atop another. To some, it appeared as if the Mandara mountain stood on the back of Kurmadev, the tortoise incarnation of Lord Vishnu. Hanuman’s shadow alone was almost fifty yojanas long. The vanaras felt tiny in comparison.10

glorious take-off

“Yes, I can do anything! Like my father, I can reach any corner of the universe and return in a moment. I can leap across all the oceans at once. I can disperse the clouds in the sky and dry up the oceans. I will reach Lanka in one jump and find Sita there. If I can’t find Her, then I will uproot Lanka and carry back the isle for us to search thoroughly. I can snatch the nectar of immortality right out of Indra’s hands. I can do anything in the service of Lord Rama. I am unstoppable. Lord Rama does not need to take the trouble of going to Lanka. I will crush Ravana with my thumb and carry Sita back from Lanka.”

The vanaras began to hoot and shout and started to gather around Jambavan. As the wise old warrior saw the prancing vanaras looking up at Hanuman all awe-struck, he said, “Go, Hanuman. Leap into the isle across the ocean and find Sita. Remember our lives depend on you. We will be glued to this spot and fast until your return. Our prayers are with you.”11

Hanuman folded his palms and bowed to his teacher Surya, the sun god. He then offered his respects to Indra, the god of the heavens, then Lord Brahma, the creator, and finally, he turned to his father, Vayu, the wind god. Then, turning south, he glared at the raging waters and the rough waves teasing him.12

From the beach, Hanuman was quite a vision standing atop the summit. Red, blue, black, white, green and yellow rocks were scattered all over the slopes. Everyone had heard and could now imagine the snakes of various sizes and shapes that slithered around the boulders on which Yakshas, Kinnaras and Gandharvas visiting the mountain with their consorts sat and made merry. However, little did they realize that this peaceful mountain would soon enough become the hub of extreme action. From a distance, the ascetics, Vidyadharas, Siddhas, Charanas and other awestruck celestial beings watched the colossal wonder-vanara prepare for the impossible with his eyes shut. Confidence, strength, faith and determination reflected off his poised frame.13

Hanuman kneeled on all fours, placed his palms on the mountain floor, brought his right foot forward between his hands, stretched his right leg forward, with his left leg and coiled his tail behind him, before finally digging his feet to the ground to prepare for the lunge. As he took position, he stretched and slapped his muscles to charge himself, vigorously shook the hair on his body and let out an earsplitting roar that rumbled like storm clouds. He then crouched and curled his toes, arched his neck and looked skyward, took a deep breath, pressed his ears against his head and transferred all his strength and power onto his ankles. He then tightly coiled his tail into his back, released it with a sudden thwack and skyrocketed upward. While the tail-strike blasted the mountain rocks into smithereens, the immense pressure of the lift-off sucked in all the trees, plants and animals in the vicinity.

The pressure was so high that the leaves, flowers and fruits of the trees were ruffled and jolted off the naked branches. Millions of flowers, leaves and fruits rained on the vanaras standing by the ocean below the mountain. Soon, the entire coast and parts of the ocean were strewn with plants, trees and animals that had fallen off Hanuman’s blazing trail once the pressure normalized.

From afar, the ascetics had watched the mountain tremble under the sudden immeasurable weight and force. The fragile blossoms on every tree on the slopes were startled off their stems. One moment the mountain was resplendent with multicolored rocks and pebbles, the next it was a flower carpet. The rammed mountain began to spew streaks of liquid gold, silver and antimony, shoot out sulfur boulders and eject horrified and howling beasts and humans out of their caves. Animal cries echoed across the land.14

The reverberations woke the venomous snakes from their slumber. They sprung out of their burrows with their hoods fanned out, hissing flames from their forked tongues. Dazed and disoriented by the chaos, the reptiles started to dig their fangs into the rocks. The poison was so potent that even the medicinal herbs on the slopes could not neutralize it. Instead, it oxidized the rock minerals, sparking a massive wildfire. The columns of smoke seemed like the mountain’s incense offering to Hanuman.

The commotion unsettled all creatures on the mountain. What’s going on? Is it a demon attack? No one seemed to have a clue, but everyone wanted to escape whatever it was.

The ascetics tumbled out of their caves and jumped off the rocks they were seated on. The Vidyadharas – their necks adorned with red flower garlands, their bodies smeared with red sandalwood paste and their restless eyes red and tipsy from a night of revelry – dragged themselves out of their cozy dens and escaped skyward with their women in tow, leaving behind them as litter their golden jugs of fine wine and shiny plates of exotic meals.

“Did you see that?” A vanara asked, his eyes still riveted on Hanuman, who was now only a speck in the sky. “That was powerful yet beautiful.”

Another agreed. “It seemed like the flora and fauna on the mountain were seeing a dear one off on a long, arduous journey. Almost as if the plants and animals on the mountain had accompanied Hanuman up to a certain distance, as one would a loved one.”

Yet another vanara said, “The trees resemble soldiers silently following their king!”

“Look at the ocean now! So many flowers in so many hues – the ocean resembles the night sky filled with twinkling stars.” A poetic vanara commented.

Seeing everyone lose focus on Hanuman’s flight, an inspired vanara spoke. “Forget the fallen ones; look at the one that has risen! With his arms spread out ahead of him, he is like a five-headed snake emerging from behind a mountain peak. Look at his face, glowing like the twilight sun. Those brilliant eyes are gleaming like the full moon. See his dazzling teeth impale even lightning. His intellect is as robust as his body. Even the wind slapping his muscular armpits sounds like thunder. His strong tail resembles the tough pillar erected for the worship of Indra. Look at Hanuman blazing through the sky like a meteor shooting across the galaxy.”

The excited vanaras broke into a resounding applause, celebrating the greatness of their wonderful friend. Amid the enormity of this momentous occasion, one thought soared high above all the others: Hanuman’s modesty. In all these years the vanaras had known him, Hanuman had managed to conceal his greatness and live a simple life among them, never once showing off through words or actions. His humility far outshone his heroism.15

Hanuman seemed like a boat sailing in the wind; his long shadow sprawled across the waters like a dense cloud. The sheer force of Hanuman zipping over the ocean caused the waters to part right down the middle into two tall, deep waves. The parting was so deep that it exposed the floor, making the aquatic creatures feel naked and unclothed.

Hanuman would disappear into and appear out of the large clouds, almost like the peeping moon. Sea serpents were petrified, assuming that their enemy Garuda was flying past. The Gandharvas, Apsaras, Devatas and the Danavas showered fragrant flowers on Hanuman. The sages standing atop the far-flung mountain woves verses to describe the majestic scene. The birds and the Gandharvas set their heartfelt verses to music. To make his journey comfortable, the harsh sun dimmed its glare and the wild wind hushed into a pleasant breeze.16

When all his brothers were toning down to facilitate Hanuman’s mission, Varuna, god of the oceans, wondered how he could contribute. Thus far, he had only been an obstacle to finding Sita. He now wanted to change that and be of help. After all, didn’t King Sagara of the Ikshvaku dynasty, to which Lord Rama belongs, create him? The ideal way of expressing gratitude would have been by being this large watery barrier and stopping Ravana from whisking away Sita. Back then, however, deep terror had choked him and rendered him inactive. A god-sent opportunity to assist the assistant of the descendent of his creator had fallen right into Varuna’s lap, and he didn’t want to miss this second chance. He assumed a weary traveler needed a good nap. So he summoned the services of the golden Mainak, the mountain of comforts, which lulled to sleep anyone setting foot on it. The mountain’s precious gift was many days of sound sleep. Varuna felt that during this long eight-hundred-mile journey to Lanka, a good rest would be a welcome break for Hanuman.17

Varuna spoke to the mountain. “O Mainak, do you remember how I saved you from the wrath of Indra? If you remember and are grateful for the shelter that I provided you for all these years, then it is time to reciprocate by helping me express my gratitude to someone who created me.”18

As Mainak stood there obediently, his palms folded, Varuna continued. “I do not want you to move from your position because you are covering the entrance to the patalalokas. The asuras have been waiting for a chance to open the portal to the patalalokas to gain easy access to the earth. Stay fixed and expand your dimensions. Rise high enough to be seen on the surface of the waters. As you grow, you will find coming your way the mighty Hanuman, who is on a mission for Lord Rama. Offer him your best services and make him happy. That’s all I expect.19

obstacle one: wingless mountain

Suddenly the blue sky was a shade of resplendent gold. Hanuman was astonished by the sudden change in hue. Piercing out of the deep ocean, right in the middle of the water surface, was a fascinating golden mountain. Hanuman was flying at very high speed and did not manage to slow down on time, and his barrel chest collided with the highest peak. The intense impact shook the mountain and pushed it aside by a considerable distance. Hanuman halted his momentum and pivoted to take a good look at the golden mountain that obstructed him.

It was gorgeous, with flowering trees and fruit trees, picturesque parks and shimmering peaks with palaces. Fresh water lakes dotted the mountain that arose from the salty ocean floor. The luster of the golden mountain was comparable to a hundred brilliant suns. Attractive Kinnara men and women sat in the parks and along the lakes, indulging in song and dance. Standing with folded hands atop the highest peak was an effulgent personality. The golden aura of the mountain seemed to emanate from his body. He was looking at Hanuman with deep veneration.

Hanuman wasn’t impressed. He was hardly a fraction into his eight-hundred-mile journey, and this obstacle was the last thing he needed. It surely looked important and impressive, but it was a barrier, nonetheless. Hanuman offered a reluctant yet polite halfsmile. Mainak began eulogizing Hanuman’s greatness and showering the flying hero with praise. “What you have set out to accomplish is beyond anyone’s imagination. Every hero needs a good rest to achieve success. I am Mainak, and I am here to provide you with a much-needed break. To make this huge jump after months of arduous search across the length and breadth of the land must have exhausted you. I am equipped with every comfort ideal to rejuvenate and invigorate a tired body. Just surrender yourself for a few hours, and I assure you of complete rejuvenation and much more strength for your onward journey.20

Varuna is so grateful to the ancestors of Lord Rama who created him that he wants to reciprocate by showing kindness upon anyone connected to that dynasty. You are the foremost of Lord Rama’s associates, and Varuna wants me to offer you a special treat. Gratitude needs to be expressed through action. Please allow, my master, to show his reciprocation to the Raghuvamsha by serving you during your service to them.

But I have a more personal and important reason. That alone is enough for me to serve you eternally, not just this once. Many years ago, every mountain in this world had wings and could fly freely to wherever they wished. Some of the mountains were so powerful that they could compete with Garuda and even Vayu in speed. Because of one or two freak accidents, all living things on earth developed a fear that the indiscriminate landing of the flying mountains put their lives in danger. They prayed to the devas, who promised to rectify the situation immediately. Instead of setting proper rules for flying and landing, Indra’s solution was to chop off the wings of every mountain and make them immobile and harmless. When he finally came to amputate me, your father Vayu came to my rescue. To stop Indra from hitting me, he blew a mighty breath of wind that dislodged me from the path of the thunderbolt and pushed me into the ocean bed. Varuna agreed to shelter me. By the grace of the wind and water gods, my wings were saved from destruction. Varuna had a vested interested. He uses me to close the portal to the patalaloka. But your father is just like you – without any agenda. Now is my chance to reciprocate your father’s kindness by showing you small acts of kindness. By worshipping the son, I worship the father.21

Our culture teaches us to worship even an ordinary guest as god, but you are no ordinary guest. Everyone you are connected to is worthy of worship; therefore, you are worthy of worship. Even if we do not consider your greatness, there are so many reasons to worship you. If you add to that even a fraction of your greatness, it gives limitless reasons to serve you right now. Please come and enjoy the fruits and flowers here. Relish the nectarine waters and refreshing fragrant breezes. Let me take care of your fatigue and rejuvenate your body. Surrender yourself to this mountain and allow the pleasure treatment to help you experience the rejuvenation of your body.”

After listening to Mainak patiently, Hanuman spoke his mind. “Just as you have an obligation to reciprocate, I have a duty to perform. Your obligation is not time bound, but my duty is. Every moment here is a moment of duty neglected. I will receive your thankfulness by simply touching you. Please understand that my father appreciates your love, your desire to serve and your genuine gratitude. Allow me to go ahead on my mission for my Master now.”

Even before Mainak could utter a response, Hanuman touched the golden mountain and flew away. He not only accepted Mainak’s offer but also did not waste any time reveling in comfort and neglecting his duties. He had been sensitive to Mainak’s feelings, yet he was alert enough about his duty in order not to allow the lure of comfort to derail it.

Hanuman had overcome the second obstacle. The first was gross, and the second was subtle. The first was the ocean, and the second was comfort. The first was tough, and second was sensitive. The first needed external power, and the second needed internal alertness. The first needed ability, and the second needed absolute focus on the goal.22

No sooner than Hanuman was out of sight, Indra appeared to Mainak. He hadn’t seen the golden mountain in ages. Varuna, the ocean god, was his protector, which meant Indra could not clip off his wings as he had for the other mountains. Mainak started to tremble. Just as he began regretting his foolish decision of leaving the protection of the ocean bed, Indra folded his hands and offered his respects to him. “You have done very well by offering to serve Hanuman who is on a mission in service of Lord Rama. I am extremely pleased with you, Mainak. From now on, you will have no reason to fear me. This one act of service has absolved you of all your arrogance and pride.”23

obstacle two: hungry serpent

Hanuman hadn’t flown too far before something that clearly did not belong in the ocean pierced out through surface. It was ghastly, slimy and whopping.

Hanuman stopped abruptly. A demoness lay sprawled in front of him, her mouth wide open. Her intent was to block his way completely with her expansive body. The odor from her mouth was obnoxious enough to kill.

“Glory to the gods! Today they have sent me a big bite. You are my meal for the day. Enter my mouth and satisfy my hunger, vanara. Know that I have Lord Brahma’s boon that allows me to make a meal of anything that crosses this ocean. You can neither undo the Supreme Creator’s blessing nor stop yourself from being my meal. I will savor you, my sweet vanara delicacy.”24

Hanuman had no time to waste arguing with the demoness. He decided to edge past her and carry on with his journey. But she side-stepped and blocked him again. She chortled as she expanded her body by ten times. “My dear vanara, you don’t have a choice.”25

Hanuman was in no mood for a conflict. “You must be Surasa, the daughter of Daksha. O great mother of snakes, please understand that I am on a very critical mission. I need to give Mother Sita, whom Ravana has held captive, a message from my Master, Lord Rama. After delivering the message to Her, I will surrender myself to you and become your meal. Until then, please allow me time. I promise you, I will return to fulfill your desire.”26

Surasa’s hood began to quiver in rage. She flashed her fangs and glared at him with her reddish-brown eyes. Hanuman steadied himself for a not-so-peaceful way of resolving the difference.

Suddenly Hanuman was ten times her size. His sudden resizing clearly shocked Surasa. Her gaping mouth became wider until it was almost twenty times his size. Hanuman then outmatched her by thirty times. She went a notch higher, and her mouth was now forty times as wide. Hanuman outdid her again. The one-upping between the two continued until the wonder-monkey forced her to open her mouth to hundred times its size. Her exceedingly wide mouth and head were now kissing the clouds.27

Hanuman realized outdoing her was futile, and he unexpectedly constricted himself to the size of a thumb. He then zoomed into her cavernous tunnel, reached right into her throat and zipped out. The shrinking, the entry and the exit all happened in an eyeblink, which left Surasa with absolutely no reaction time. Hanuman’s exit from the overstretched mouth of Surasa resembled the luminescent moon exiting the dark mouth of Rahu after the eclipse. Hanuman had depleted not just his size but his ego, too. He quit competing with Surasa and realized that it was wiser to take on a humbler approach.28

Lessons from Hanuman’s Jump

We can view Hanuman’s jump across the ocean from two main perspectives, and each offers valuable lessons.

*Life Perspective*

Everyone has some mission to accomplish in life. No matter how small or big the mission, obstacles will come along the way, making it a challenge to complete. Each mission has the same obstacles as the ones Hanuman faced.

Obstacle One: Everyone lacks confidence before executing a seemingly impossible mission. To overcome this, we need a Jambavan-like mentor to reassure us of our powers and remind us of past victories.

Obstacle Two: There is considerable distance between where we are and where we want to be. The gap seems so huge that it becomes difficult to decide on when and how to take the first step. To overcome this, we need to take a leap of faith. Before taking this leap of faith, Hanuman readied himself by praying. Praying is an act of mental preparation, which empowers us to take that leap.

Obstacle Three: From time to time, everyone faces a mental block. It’s called the golden-mountain block. Anyone intensely pursuing a mission encounters this golden mountain. This block slows us down during the mission as a warning that rushing at a frantic pace could burn us out. This golden mountain actually represents the mental inertia that tries to slow us down. It sometimes comes in the form of a breakdown, sometimes as a desire for comfort, sometimes as distractions and temptations. The only way to deal with this block is through single-minded focus on the goal. No temptation is worth losing the goal. For Hanuman, his mission was more important than his comfort. That obsession to reach our destination melts all blocks away.

Obstacle Four: Just when we are about to reach our goal, we experience a competitive mentality. People like Surasa love to instigate this mentality so that we lose sight of our goal and find ourselves stuck in needless competition. A competitive mentality forces us to think that victory is about proving and establishing that we are better than everyone around. The mentality becomes so fierce at times that we allow it to consume us at the cost of our goal. Hanuman dealt with competitive mentality by letting go. When we let go of the desire to prove our superiority, our mind automatically becomes tranquil and refocuses on the goal. By letting go of the petty desire to prove our superiority, we can embrace the superior desire to prioritize our mission.

Obstacle Five: Something that can often obstruct our mission path is envy. Envy begins with faultfinding and ends in cruelty. When we achieve any kind of success, we tend to become envious of anyone ahead of us. Flying high indicates achieving heights of success. Hanuman is flying high and Simhika is way below. The shadow represents our faults. No matter how highly successful we are, there will always be some fault or weakness. Instead of looking up and admiring the success of the person, people like Simhika prefer to look down on the faults of that person. Catching hold of the shadow represents criticism. Pulling down the person indicates defaming a person by criticizing him or her. Envy prevents us from being inspired by successful people. Hanuman did not allow envy to emerge. He checked it immediately. He made Simhika become humble. Rather than envying those who are successful, we should learn to celebrate their success. Celebrating others’ successes soon leads to celebrating our own successes. Plotting for another’s failure indicates our own failure.

Spiritual Perspective

Hanuman’s leap and the obstacles that he came across when seen from a spiritual point of view represent the challenges a spiritual aspirant (sadhaka) goes through before reaching his or her final destination.

Spiritually, the goal to be achieved is Sita, who represents bhaktidevi or the goddess of devotion. Unlike the life perspective, the obstacles here are all internal. These obstacles represent the inner struggles of a sadhaka while on the path of devotion.

Challenge One: When it comes to matters of spirituality, everyone struggles with self-doubt. At such a time, words of inspiration can work wonders. The words of inspiration of a mentor or shiksha guru serve as the impetus through our long journey of spirituality. Jambavan reminded Hanuman not only of his powers but also about why the gods first granted and later denied him those powers. The gods blessed Hanuman with unlimited powers when he was small, but a sage’s curse erased all memories of those powers. Hanuman had been abusing his powers for his entertainment. The sage cursed him that he would not remember anything about any of his powers until someone came into his life to channelize his powers for a mission. Jambavan is a guru that channelizes a sadhaka’s power to the service of the Lord.

Challenge Two: The vast ocean represents deha abhiman or pride in one’s own greatness, which is a big barrier to being completely devoted to God. All the great vanaras expressed their inability to cross the ocean, indicating that even the most powerful people have great difficulty in overcoming this big impediment. Hanuman considered himself like an arrow. An arrow by itself cannot achieve much and cannot travel a long distance. The distance an arrow travels depends entirely on the skill and arm strength of the shooter. When he jumped across the ocean, Hanuman felt like Lord Rama’s arrow. According to him it wasn’t his ability that enabled him to cross over the ocean, but it was Rama’s power that helped him do so. He chanted “Rama!” He started with “Ra,” and he ended with “ma.” When he reached “ma,” he was already in Lanka. To accomplish what even great people couldn’t, Hanuman had to have dhairyam or courage. As sadhakas, we should develop the courage to want to get out of the bodily concept of life that binds us in the shackles of our own greatness.

Challenge Three: One of the things that obstruct one’s vision of the goal is the lure of glitter. While practicing spiritual discipline, we are bound to come across some fringe benefits – adoration, worship, comfort and pleasures. Pleasure by itself is not bad, but when it takes us away from our spiritual pursuits, we must avoid it. We begin to take these fringe benefits seriously and lose focus on our goals. Good things are not always good. When good comes in the way of executing our duty, then it becomes bad. A sadhaka should develop drishtih or far-sightedness. Although the golden mountain offered to him a glittering proposal, Hanuman kept his vision focused on his goal of devotion and avoided all the fringe benefits.

Challenge Four: During our spiritual journey, we often find it difficult to control our pride and stay humble. Hanuman’s interaction with Surasa teaches a sadhaka these very insightful lessons. The first is that Surasa and Hanuman competed with each other to prove that one was bigger than the other. Becoming big represents the act of helping others. Two do-gooders tend to compete with one another to prove their superiority in doing good. Although doing good is important, good that makes you proud is not actually good. When Hanuman realized the futility of one-upmanship, he made himself very small, thereby shrinking his pride of being a doer. When doing good for others, we should become extremely humble and small. Rather than being arrogant about doing good, we should cultivate humility, for we are merely instruments and not the cause.

Challenge Five: For a sadhaka, it is a challenge to remain detached as well as use intelligence. Surasa told Hanuman that he was a detached sadhaka and he ought not bother about his body and allow her to eat him. Hanuman, however, responded by saying that he would offer his body as her meal but only after accomplishing his mission of meeting Sita, conveying Rama’s message to Her and Her message to Rama. He admitted that a sadhaka was neither afraid of death nor attached to the body, but an intelligent sadhaka knows better than to underestimate the worth of the body. A wise sadhaka would fearlessly give up the body only after it has served its purpose and accomplished its goal. Our body is like a boat that helps us cross the river of material existence. After we cross the river, the boat becomes useless and we should detach from it. A sadhaka should develop matih or resourceful intelligence. Although Surasa challenged him in many ways, Hanuman used his intelligence to overcome all the obstacles she posed and technically satisfied all her demands without compromising on his principles and goals.

We are never envious of another’s spiritual qualities, but rather of another’s material qualities. At the spiritual level, envy can never exist because we understand that every good quality is simply a spark of God’s splendor. Simhika caught Hanuman’s shadow, which represents a fault. She managed to find a fault where none existed. While the demigods had sent Surasa to test Hanuman, Simhika was sent by the demons to obstruct him. Simhika was the mother of Rahu. Just as her son Rahu was envious of the demigods’ success in retrieving all the nectar from the churning of the milk ocean, Simhika was envious of Hanuman’s success in flying across the ocean. Just as Rahu disguised himself and tried to cheat Mohini Murti, Simhika hid herself inside the ocean and tried to cheat Hanuman. While he dealt with Surasa using matih, he dealt with Simhika using dakshyam or expertise. He executed her without her realizing it. Envy is a demoniac obstacle. It must be eliminated to progress spiritually.

Like Hanuman used his intelligence to deal with various obstacles along his way, a sadhaka must use intelligence to counter subtle obstacles that come in the path of devotion. In overcoming each subtle obstacle, a sadhaka comes closer to the goal of life: loving devotion to the Lord.

Lessons from the Lankini Encounter

A single moment of association with Hanuman transformed Lankini much more than wisdom from countless books ever could. Great people deliver deep and life-changing messages with subtlety.

Satsang, or association with great people, transforms us in four different ways.

1\. The primary purpose of satsang is to challenge the belief systems entrenched within our intellect. Many of these deeply embedded beliefs are those that we follow without logicizing or questioning. We accept these as absolute truths. Lankini believed her loyalty lay with her master Ravana, who fed her. She sincerely performed her job of guarding Lanka keeping this belief in mind. When Hanuman punched her, he made her realize that her devotion to the master (swami bhakti) was good, but it was first important to ascertain who was a worthy master. She was following the principles of dharma without really understanding its spirit. Lankini believed that she was following the path of dharma by loyally serving her master when, in fact, she was merely justifying her belief that Ravana was her master and she was his servant. A good servant is one who can identify a worthy master. Until she met Hanuman, she believed Ravana was the master and anyone entering Lanka without his permission was a trespasser. But after her encounter with Hanuman, she realized that Ravana was the real trespasser.

2\. The second purpose is to reduce our attachments. Lankini was attached to Lanka and her role as the protector of Lanka. A moment’s association with Hanuman made her detach herself from her position and her attachment to the land that she worshipped.

3\. The third is to help one rise from a fall. Satsang does not allow you to remain fallen, but it encourages you to rise to a higher platform. Lankini fell when Hanuman punched her, but she also found the strength to stand up and thank him for bringing her to her senses by hitting her. Usually, no one considers being hit or humiliated as fortunate. Most find honor fortunate and dishonor unfortunate. But a misfortune that helps you remember God is a fortune, and a fortune that makes you forget God is a misfortune.

4\. Satsang removes the deep effect of negative association. Lankini was not bad, but her association with Ravana made her bad. Hanuman’s involvement undid that Ravana-linked damage to her consciousness and helped recollect her memories. She instantly remembered her past life in Lord Brahma’s company. Our relationship with God is not new, but we forget that bond. We need a saint like Hanuman to remind us of that link with God.

The presiding deity of Lanka, Lankini, represents egotism. Just as she guards Lanka under Ravana’s command, egotism is under the control of the mind. Just as she prevents the entry of Hanuman into Lanka, egotism prevents the entry of a guru into our lives. Initially, Hanuman tries to gently talk her into letting him inside, but when she displays her arrogance, his approach becomes tough. Similarly, the guru tries to transform our lives gently. But when we exhibit arrogance and aversion toward him, he weighs on our ego. When the ego is subjugated, whims of the mind will soon perish.

Four Reasons for Valmiki’s Detailed Description of Lanka

In the Ramayana, Valmiki describes the kingdom of Lanka in detail. Four reasons justify such lengthy descriptions.

1\. To imply that the night was moving very slow and that the search was an excruciatingly and painfully slow one for Hanuman.

2\. To compare Ravana with Rama. Ravana was a strong opponent and because of it he was a worthy enemy of Rama. It was important to describe the enemy elaborately so that people could appreciate the hero even more.

3\. To show that Hanuman wasn’t affected by either the opulence of Lanka or the temptations of women.

4\. To show the stark contrast between kama or lust and prema or love. This description shows the difference between the purity and the great sacrifice found in real love and the decadence and abuse that is part of lust. What Ravana has for Sita and his wife Mandodari is lust, which involves no sacrifice or control. What Rama feels for Sita is real love, which does not hesitate to make boundless sacrifices.

Half a Dozen Birds with a Message Each

On six different occasions in the Ramayana, six different birds convey six different messages.

The Ramayana begins with the krouncha bird being shot by the hunter, which inspires Valmiki to compose the first sloka of Ramayana.

In Kaushalya’s palace, the mynah Sarika urges the parrot Suka to bite the foot of the cat to prevent it from taking the offerings to the deities.

In Panchavati, Jatayu fights with Ravana to prevent him from taking Sita.

On the shore of the ocean, Sampati revives the hope of the vanaras by pointing the exact location of Sita.

The pingalika bird, sometimes called the vanjula bird, assures Sita in Lanka of the coming of Rama.

During the Rama–Ravana war, Garuda comes just in the nick of time to save Rama and Lakshmana from the nagastras.

In each of these situations, the birds come at the right time to help avert a crisis. These birds represent the sages or wise and spiritual men.

There is great similarity in between a bird and a sage. Just as the birds fly high across the vast skies, the sages fly high through the vast experience of godliness. Just as the bird flies because of its two wings, the sages fly on the twin wings of knowledge (gyana) and practice (sadhana). Just as the bird rests on the branches of trees, the sages rest on the various branches of the Vedas.

Hanuman decided the third option was the best. If he spoke the Awadhi dialect, Sita would immediately understand that it was someone near and dear to Rama. Palms Folded

In Vedic culture, when one folds one’s palms in front of someone, the palms are held at different levels depending on who that someone is.

In front of one’s mother, the folded palms are at stomach level.

In front of one’s father, the folded palms are held at neck level.

In front of one’s guru, the folded palms are held at face level.

In front of God, the folded palms are held above the head.

When Hanuman appeared in front of Mother Sita, he held his folded palms way above his head showing Her the highest level of respect.

Roles Reversed

Here Sita and Hanuman’s conversation brings out the essence of the guru-shishya (teacher-student) relationship. Sita represents a qualified disciple and Hanuman the bonafide guru.

Before trying to connect with Her, Hanuman makes a thorough check to determine Her consciousness. Only when he notices Her fully absorbed in the agony of separation from Lord Rama does he decide that it’s imperative to rescue Her. The bonafide spiritual master prioritizes that disciple who has the great eagerness to advance in spiritual life and reconnect with God.

Sita, on the other hand, does not accept Hanuman as Rama’s messenger either without thoroughly testing him. Just because he says he is a representative of God doesn’t mean that he is one. The test of a bonafide guru lies in his ability to describe the Lord. Unless he has understood and experienced God, he would not be able to describe God convincingly.

Only when both Sita and Hanuman are convinced about each other’s genuineness does Hanuman hand over the ring. Only when the guru sees that the disciple is qualified, does the guru offer the symbol of God. In the different stages of being a disciple, the guru offers different symbols to establish the disciple’s connection with God. This formal process is called initiation, wherein the guru formally connects the disciple with God.

In Sundarkanda of the Ramayana, there are two very beautiful things. One is God’s name and the other is God’s katha or glories. Hanuman offers Sita the ring with Rama’s name on it and he narrates Rama katha. Sita is in Ashoka vatika and praying for divine intervention to end Her shoka or lamentation. That is when Hanuman comes and offers Her Rama nama and Rama katha, which ends Her lamentation.

Sita is in Lanka where Ravana is willing to offer Her gold, but that doesn’t impress Her at all. But when Hanuman gives Her the small golden ring, She feels so inspired and affected. That is the difference between love and lust. A little offered with love holds more value than a lot offered with lust.

After appreciating Hanuman profusely, Sita asked him a question that had been troubling Her. If Rama is safe, if Rama is angry, if Rama has powerful weapons, if Rama loves Me, if Rama is capable, then why hasn’t He come to rescue Me yet?119

Hanuman convinced Her that the moment he returned with the information about Her exact location, the vanara army would descend southward and vanquish the Lankan army.

Much More Than Size

Size equals responsibility. When Hanuman says he can take any size for the mission of Lord Rama, he means that he can take any responsibility for His sake. Most people wrongly understand the concept of humility. They use false humility as a foil for their laziness. When given any responsibility, they shirk from it by calling themselves insignificant and unqualified. Hanuman, on the other hand, calls himself small but also makes it clear that he is quite capable of taking up bigger responsibilities and adapt accordingly. He knows well that although he is insignificant, the Lord can empower him to do things of great significance. Such dependence and faith are the real essence of true humility.

Hanuman prefers to keep himself small in front of Mother Sita. Even when he shows his huge form, he shrinks quickly and reassumes his small size. This is because he wants Her to consider him as Her youngest son. The youngest child gets the most favor and love of the mother.

Those who are big are unable to become small because they are so proud. Those who are small cannot become big because they are incapable. But Hanuman can do both because he is not only humble but also capable.

Becoming small when you are big is bhakti yoga. Becoming big while you consider yourself small is karma yoga.

Hanuman spoke again. “O Mother, please know that everyone else in the vanara army is either equal to me or better. There is absolutely no one in the army inferior to me. You must be aware that kings only send their most insignificant warriors as messengers to an enemy camp. Please be assured that Rama’s vanara army is well-equipped to rescue You. We don’t even have to wait until the army comes here. You could just agree to get onto my back, and I could take You to Lord Rama right away. Please come with me right now.”122

“No! No! I cannot come with you because I fear heights.”

In the process of fighting them, you may forget about Me, and I could fall into the ocean and drown. That’s why I don’t want to come with you.”

Hanuman couldn’t help but laugh again. Even this reasoning did not fit in with Sita’s personality. “O Mother, You are Bhumisuta. The earth is Your mother. Why would You fear the ocean, which is a beautiful way of becoming a part of Mother Earth? This definitely cannot be the real reason You don’t want to come.”123

Realizing that Hanuman was quite clever, Sita found another reasoning. “The real reason is that you are a para-purusha.124 I do not want to touch you and travel on your back.”

Hanuman found this reasoning funny too. “My dear Mother, I am Your son. For a mother, a son is never a para-purusha. Please come with me or tell me the real reason behind Your unwillingness to come.”

After much cajoling, Sita spoke up. She didn’t want to say this out loud for fear of embarrassing Rama. Hanuman was insistent, so She decided to tell him the real reason. “Have you considered what historians will write when the story of Rama will be written if I return with you now? They would say that Sita was brought from Panchavati to Lanka by a demon and brought back from Lanka by a vanara. The question on every lip would be: What did Her husband Rama do? I do not want anyone to point any fingers at Rama and criticize Him for not being able to protect Me from Ravana. It would be glorious only if Rama came here, defeated Ravana and took Me back with Him with dignity and grandeur.”125

Hanuman’s eyes welled up with tears when he heard Sita’s reason for not wanting to accompany him back to Rama. Hanuman was taken aback by Her willingness to forgo Her safety for Rama’s reputation. Realizing that there was no other way except for Rama to come and destroy Ravana, Hanuman readied himself to leave. No more waiting was needed. But before he left, he asked Sita to give him a token that would remind Rama about Her and reconfirm that Hanuman had indeed met Her. Sita smiled and assured him that She would give him two tokens that would beyond doubt convince Rama—a verbal memory and a physical token.

The story Sita chose to tell Hanuman was private and personal to Her and Rama. Even Lakshmana was unaware of this incident as it had occurred during an intimate moment between Her and Rama.

“While We were residing in the mountains of Chitrakuta during Our first year of exile, We spent considerable time together in the scenic forests. One day, Rama was lying on My lap and taking a nap. As I was admiring His beautiful sleeping face, a black crow began to attack Me viciously. Not wanting to disturb Rama’s slumber, I tried throwing stones and pebbles at the crow to shoo it off. No matter how much I tried, I was unable to chase it away. The crow’s attacks had become more and more vicious now. I began to bleed at various places from the crow’s pecking. As I turned and swiveled to avoid the attacks, Rama woke up, only to see Me covered in blood. He became wildly angry with the tormentor. Plucking out a single strand of grass from the ground, Rama chanted some mantras converting that simple grass into the most powerful missile in the universe, the Brahmastra. Once he threw the missile at the miscreant, it chased him throughout the universe, not giving him any relief from his sufferings. Only when the demon crow or kakasura came back and surrendered at the feet of Rama did the missile slow down.

After a lot of pleading and request for pardon, Rama reduced the effect of the Brahmastra, and it only took away one eye of the rogue crow. We decided to keep this story Our little secret.” Sita concluded. “The moment you mention this story to Rama, He will know that We have Met. As I remember this story of His love for Me and His anger toward those who tormented Me, I wonder why He hasn’t come to protect me from Ravana whose torture has been a million times worse than the crow’s. If He could send the Brahmastra across the universe to annihilate that crow, why hasn’t He sent even one to annihilate this demon in Lanka?”126

Sita began to sob yet again. Hanuman realized that he had to say something to stop Her from crying and give Her hope. “My dear Mother, Your complaint is that Lord Rama has forgotten You, but my observation is that Lord Rama has forgotten His nature. From the time He has lost You, He has forgotten His own powers. He has completely forgotten the power of His bow. You still remember His prowess, but He has forgotten His own capabilities. If only He remembered His own strengths, You would not be here until now. All day long, Lord Rama is immersed in thoughts about You and worries about You.”

Hanuman’s words felt like a salve on Her wounded heart. She told him, “My dear son, please do not remind Rama about Me now, instead go and remind Him of His powers. Remind Him of the strength of His mighty bow with which He had single handedly destroyed fourteen thousand demons.”

Hanuman was happy that his words had the desired effect. Sita continued recollecting Her private moments with Rama. “Once while We were in the forest, Rama noticed that the red vermilion on My forehead was smudged. He called Me to His side and said that He would rectify it and apply it fresh. He picked up a red ore stone He had found in the forest path and made a paste on His palm. He pulled Me toward him and held My face gently with His hand. Not only did He rectify the mark on My forehead, but He also applied the red vermilion paste on My cheeks.

I also recollect something that happened in Ayodhya much before the exile. One evening, I was waiting for Rama in the palace gardens. Although I waited for quite a long time, He didn’t turn up as promised. When He arrived, it was too late and I was very angry. When I asked Him the reason for the delay, He said that He was caught up in work. I was so angry that I told Him that He didn’t have any work but was late intentionally. I told Him that since He had done something very wrong, He deserved to be punished. I decided to bind Him as a punishment. I picked up a garland made of soft jasmine flowers and tied up His hands. The white garland turned red. I was so surprised to see how delicate Rama was. No one knows about these incidents but Us. When you remind Him of these incidents, He will surely know that You met Me.”

As a physical memory to show Rama that Hanuman had actually met Her, Sita took off the tiara on her head and placed it on Hanuman’s palms. “As soon as Rama sees the jewel, He will recognize this as My mother’s gift to Me on the occasion of Our wedding. I have been wearing this tiara every single day, and it is a symbol of My union with Rama.” During the abduction flight, Sita had dropped many of Her jewels to help Rama trace Her, but She had preserved the one dearest to Her. Hanuman carefully held it and inspected it closely. The symbol of Her marriage would now drag Her husband to Her rescue.127

Sita then gave a message for Rama through Hanuman. She said, “My dear Lord, I have been in this dangerous and despicable place for the last ten months, moaning constantly because of this separation from You. I cannot survive this ordeal much longer now. I give You a month to come and fetch Me. If You do not come by the end of the month, I will surely give up My life as I remember You. This is My firm resolve.”

one-month deadline

Hanuman panicked! They just had a month now! It took them more than two months to just get here. Here Sita expected him to return to Rama, inform Him, rally the entire army to the south, cross the ocean, wage a war and kill Ravana! All in a span of a month! It seemed impossible. But Hanuman knew there wasn’t time to argue about the impracticality of Her suggestion. They had to do their best now.128

Hanuman now just wanted to get going. He had to return at the earliest. He offered his respects to Her and assured Her that soon She would see Her beloved and the entire vanara horde destroying the kingdom of Lanka. All her sorrows would be eliminated. When Sita realized that Hanuman was preparing to leave, She felt sad. His presence had had such a soothing effect on Her. But right now, a naughty thought played in his mind. That thought only became naughtier as the swinging fruits all around him began to make him feel hypnotized. It was risky. But nothing good was ever achieved without risk. So he jumped…

Sita Sets the Timer

The Ramayana reaches its climax with this countdown timer that Sita has set. From now on, all events that take place in the Ramayana are in a rapid-fire mode keeping in mind the timer set by Sita. ## Chapter 5

Ravaged Garden, Enraged Sovereign

unexpected damage

“Hari! Hari! Hari! Hari!”

Sita’s Lie

Why Sita lied when questioned by the rakshasis is debatable. Was it right for Sita, a follower of dharmic principles, to lie? The Vedic principles allow one to lie during five situations of life.

To save one’s marriage – vivaahakaale.

Amid courtships – lovers are allowed lies that enhance love for one another – rathisamprayoge.

To save one’s life from some danger – praanaathyayae.

To save one’s wealth from being stolen – sarvadhanaapahaare.

To protect a friend in danger – mithrasya cha arthe.

To protect the life of Hanuman, She hid his connection with Her. If Ravana came to know that Hanuman had come for Her, his fury would escalate. Thus, considering what was beneficial for Hanuman, She lied.

On getting no answers from Sita whatsoever, the rakshasis went around Ashok vatika carefully inspecting the damage. As they walked all around the outer wall of the garden, they saw the security guards lying dead, smashed in various inhuman ways. From the way they lay, the rakshasis deduced that the poor guys had no clue what hit them and had no chance to retaliate. Once the security guards were knocked out, creating havoc in the garden became easy. But that still didn’t explain how none of them heard any noise of the destruction. Either they were too drunk or that the vanara had some mystical power to keep them unconscious. What really mattered now was how to deliver the bad news to Ravana and how to stop the still ravaging villain? Of the two, the second one was clearly out of their scope of action. As the rakshasi gang ran out of the Ashoka vatika toward Ravana’s palace, Sita smiled. Good days had finally arrived.136

information explosion

Ravana was immersed in a high-level meeting with his most important ministers. His wrangle with Sita wasn’t the best way to begin his day. He wasn’t over the harsh words spoken by Her. For the first time ever, Ravana burst into tears. No one in Lanka had ever seen a weak moment in his life. He was always this largerthan-life brave king to them. He flared up like a funeral fire sprinkled with oil. Tears dripped from his face like oil from a lamp. His anger was inflamed now…142

a pain for a gain

Hanuman knew he had hit Ravana where it would hurt him most. He could almost hear the demon king scream in pain... As he waited patiently for an eruption from inside the palace, he contemplated his next move.143

seeds of destruction

giant slayer

one warrior equals a million

In the Sundarkanda, the narration of the events related to Hanuman’s Lanka travel takes place twice. The first time Hanuman narrates it to the vanara army, and the second time Hanuman narrates it to Rama. On both occasions, Hanuman’s narrative is different. When he speaks to the vanaras, he talks about all his exploits starting from Mainak all the way until the burning of Lanka but mentions nothing about his discussion with Sita. On the other hand, when he speaks to Rama, he doesn’t mention anything about his exploits and only focuses on his conversation with Sita in detail because those details were meant only for Rama. While news about Sita made Rama happy, news about his feats thrilled the vanaras. He chose to omit any talk about his exploits to Rama out of humility. This shows Hanuman’s discretion in speech. He knows whom to speak to, what to speak, how to speak and how much to speak.

wordly wise

At the first meeting itself Rama had realized that Hanuman was very wealthy. His wealth was his choice of words. Whenever and wherever he spoke, he simply touched listeners’ hearts. Rama was so touched by Hanuman’s reply to his first question that He wanted to ask more. Rama next asked him, “Where is My Sita?”

Hatred and Love

There is a distinct difference between the reporting methods of Manthara and Hanuman.

In the beginning of the Ramayana when Manthara reports about Dasaratha’s decision to Keikeyi, she does so in a way that the information causes hatred and negativity in Keikeyi. When Hanuman reports about Sita, he does so in a way that the information evokes a great surge of love and unites Rama with Sita even more.

Merely reporting the truth is not enough. It is more important to know how that truth will impact the receiver of the report. Manthara was proud that of all the citizens of Ayodhya, she was the only one with the guts to speak the truth. But her perceived truth created hatred amid people who loved each other and caused a national disturbance. Manthara was an expert in the art of manipulating words, Hanuman is an expert in nurturing words. While Manthara’s expertise divided people, Hanuman’s expertise united two parted souls. It’s not enough to wear a blanket of pride for speaking the truth, it is important to be sensitive enough to gauge the impact of the truth.

Hanuman continued his narration. “Right in the middle of the southern ocean is an island called Lanka. Right in the middle of Lanka is a garden called Ashoka vatika. Right in the middle of that garden is a large circle of seven hundred rakshasis. Right in the middle of that large circle of rakshasis is a beautiful golden simshupa tree. Right under that simshupa tree is Mother Sita with two flowers on either side. While one represents Rama and the other is Lakshmana. Sita sits under the tree and cries for Her beloved Lord Rama. The Sita who had a more prosperous existence than Lakshmi herself now lives in grief. She looks emaciated and miserable, and Her hair is always in a single plait. She is determined not to succumb to Ravana but is vulnerable to the wounds of separation from You. When I offered Her Your ring, She was thrilled and excited. She clutched the ring as if She were holding Your handsome face in Her palms.

Rama’s Ring

The ring that Rama gave Hanuman to carry had His holy name inscribed in it. Hanuman carried the ring in his mouth implying that the holy name of Lord Rama should always be in one’s mouth.

When Hanuman saw Mother Sita in the Ashoka vatika, she had been praying to the Ashoka trees to help Her become ashoka or free from lamentation (shoka). When Hanuman gave Her the ring, he told Her that only Lord Rama’s holy names could help Her become free from lamentation.

Hanuman told Her that Rama’s name has three syllables. Ra, which represented agni or fire, Aa, which represented chandra or moon and Ma which represented surya or the sun. It means that in the scorching heat of lamentation, Rama’s name would give the coolness of the moon. In the darkness of lamentation, Rama’s name would offer the brightness of the sun. As far as the fire element in Rama’s name was concerned, Hanuman decided to use that to burn Lanka, which represented all material attachments that prevent one from chanting Rama’s name.

She sent You a gift that will serve both as tangible proof that I met Her and as a loving memory of Her since this was Her only companion for the last ten long months of separation. She also told me several very intimate stories that only the two of You would know.”

Hanuman narrated each event in detail. Finally, he brought out the chudamani Sita had given him and gave it to a teary-eyed Rama. Memories of His beloved came flooding back to Him, and He couldn’t be the sober, controlled leader anymore. He broke down. The entire vanara army assembled around the Lord also wept on seeing the plight of the person they loved so dearly. To lighten the mood, Hanuman narrated the episode of kakasura that Mother Sita had shared with him. On seeing Sita’s chudamani and hearing the story of kakasura, Lord Rama knew beyond doubt that Hanuman had indeed met Her. Hanuman shared with Rama that after narrating this story, Sita had asked him why Rama had not sent a similar astra this time to save Her from a more dangerous demon? Of course, Hanuman did not expect an answer to this question from Rama, so he continued with his story and shared the discussion he had with Sita. He then told Rama about Sita’s doubt if the vanara army could cross the huge ocean. He went on to explain how he had told Her that if the most mediocre messenger from the army could cross the ocean in one leap, the more competent ones would easily be able to do it. Rama smiled at Hanuman’s modesty. Finally, Hanuman spoke about Sita’s reasons for refusing to come with him into the loving embrace of Rama in Kishkinda, away from the clutches of Ravana. Of all the various reasons that Sita gave for refusing to come with Hanuman, when Rama heard Her final reason of wanting the glory of Her rescue to go only to Her husband, He burst into tears. Even amid such trouble and inconveniences, Sita was worried about His reputation and glory. Rama was touched by Sita’s greatness and purity.

Suddenly Rama’s facial expression changed, and He seemed upset about something. He looked at Hanuman and asked him a very unexpected question. He said, “But why did you burn Lanka? That was Our plan. When you had simply gone to search for Sita and give Her a message, what was the need to burn down the city?”

Hanuman smiled. His first words of clarification behind his mysterious smile bewildered Rama. “I burned Lanka because You made me burn it.” “How could I make you burn Lanka when I wasn’t even there?” Rama waited for Hanuman’s explanation, “After I spoke to Mother Sita, my mission was complete. But I had a doubt if my mission was complete. This was because I heard Trijata, one of the rakshasis in Ashoka vatika, narrate her dream to Sita. In her dream, she had seen a vanara burn Lanka. I wondered if that was Your will for me spoken through the mouth of a rakshasi. I decided that the only way I would know what You wanted me to do was if I didn’t have the freedom to choose from many options. If I have the freedom to choose, then it would be my desire. But if I have no freedom to choose then whatever happens is the will of Lord Rama. Thus, I allowed Indrajit to tie me to up with the Brahma pasha. Once I was tied, I began to pray for direction. Just then Ravana, influenced by the divine will of Rama, had this brilliant idea of burning my tail. Ravana could have easily done anything else like cutting it off or stabbing me. Why did he choose to burn my tail? Who influenced him to think about it if not the paramatma or the voice of God from within his heart? The moment Ravana began to order his people to get cloth, oil and torches, I realized that it was Your will to use Ravana’s organizational skills to burn his own city. Once I realized that You were providing me with all the ingredients through Ravana, I was sure that it was Your will. How else would I have got all those ingredients necessary to start such a major fire in Lanka?”

Hanuman’s logic and justification captivated the vanaras. That was deep thinking indeed. Rama was impressed with Hanuman, too, not only for his strength but also his wisdom, commitment, sensitivity, achievements and deep devotion. Rama stood up and expressed His gratitude to Hanuman for having taken the trouble of finding Sita. He told him that both He and the entire Raghu dynasty were grateful for his heroic acts.202

Rama continued speaking gracefully. “O Hanuman, I do not have anything valuable to offer you in appreciation of your tremendous service. For all that you have done for Me, I have only My loving embrace to offer. Please accept it as My all.”

Rama pulled Hanuman closer to His heart and embraced him lovingly. In the warmth of that embrace, Lord Hanuman melted inside. He closed his eyes and savored every moment of that embrace. He wanted the memory of this embrace to remain in his subconscious forever. He considered himself fortunate enough to receive the embrace great sages hankered for after performing severe penances for thousands of years. Hanuman thought there was nothing more valuable than this that Lord Rama could have ever offered him. With that embrace, Hanuman got the inspiration to dedicate his life to Lord Rama’s service and a desire to please Him in every possible way. He decided that his life’s aspiration was to receive another embrace from Lord Rama.

Five Blessings

When Hanuman met Mother Sita at Ashoka vatika, She gave him five blessings. She offered him complete strength, calmness, eternity, immortality and virtues. While She was offering these five boons, Hanuman remained silent. But as soon as Sita offered Her sixth boon, Hanuman spoke up. She was surprised by his silence earlier and this sudden waking up.

Hanuman replied that Her first five boons were like the five elements of creation. He was in the process of taking re-birth. The sixth boon that She offered was like life-air entering the body. As soon as life entered, he began to speak. And what was Sita’s sixth boon that made Hanuman alive and so eager to speak? That Rama would always love him. That boon gave life to Hanuman.

The next thing that Hanuman did was to ask Sita for something to eat as he was hungry. Once a child is born, the first thing it needs is nourishment from the mother. Now that he was re-born as the son of Sita, She had to feed him now. Sita offered Hanuman fruits to satisfy his hunger. This was not the hunger of the belly but that of the soul to experience love from the divine mother.

During Rama’s embrace, Hanuman expressed his gratitude mentally to Mother Sita because of whose sixth boon Rama had actually begun to love Hanuman. Before Hanuman left on the search mission, Rama had hardly spoken much to him. But when he came back, there was a very different dynamism in their relationship because of the grace of Sita. One’s connection with the divine father is firmly established because of the grace of the divine mother.

call to action

When the warmth of that embrace was over, the calamitous reality of the present dawned on him. Holding the hands of Lord Rama, Hanuman revealed Sita’s final parting instruction. “Mother Sita has given us a one-month deadline to claim Her back from Lanka, failing which She would end Her life. She told me to tell You that She considers You to be deenadayaal or the most compassionate one. At this point, She has given You one month to show Your compassion to Her. Since the day She uttered this, three days have passed. We only have twenty-seven days left now before we lose Her forever.”

When Rama heard of the time limit set by Sita, He sprang into action. Sita’s words conveyed through Hanuman’s mouth seemed to be a catalyst that charged Rama. Standing upon a rock on the Malyavanta mountain, Lord Rama pointed to the southern direction and looked at Sugriva. At the top of his voice with all the energy he could muster, Sugriva delivered his command.

“TO THE SOUTHHHHH!”

The earth began to vibrate suddenly. The vanaras had placed their first step toward the south amid cracking stones and mountains.

The Specialty of Sundarkanda

The Sundarkanda is special for many reasons

This kanda talks about the beauty of neither Rama nor Sita but of Hanuman. A vanara is never considered beautiful, but Hanuman is considered the most beautiful purely because of his utmost devotion and service to Lord Rama. Not only his form, but his actions and speech are also beautiful.

Hanuman plays the role of a guru who acts as a go-between and unites the living entity with God by offering hope.

Hanuman is transformed from being Rama doota to Rama dasa by the grace of Mother Sita.

This kanda reveals the greatness of Mother Sita and the power of Her purity, which is why Valmiki refers to the Ramayana as Seetaayah charitam mahat or the book that extols the virtues of Sita.

This is an all-encompassing kanda in that all other preceding kandas of the Ramayana are contained within it. This is because Hanuman narrates the entire Ramayana to Sita – from the beginning of Balakanda to Kishkindakanda. Reading the Sundarkanda alone gives the benefit of reading the entire Ramayana.

Hanuman’s journey to Lanka represents a sadhaka’s or a devotee’s journey to bhakti. Sita represents bhaktidevi and Hanuman represents a bhakta in search of bhakti. By carefully observing his journey, one learns about the various obstacles one encounters in one’s journey to find bhakti. Hanuman had to face demons, who symbolize the bad habits and negative thoughts that come as obstacles. This kanda makes an example of Hanuman to show one how to deal with each obstacle that one faces along the path of bhakti.

Hanuman is the perfect yogi, who uses karma yoga, gyan yoga and bhakti yoga, all to his advantage. As he performs his seva to his master Lord Rama, he uses various yogas at different times. When he becomes massive and lightweight to burn the city of Lanka, he is a karma yogi. When he increases in size and weight to fight demons in the ocean and in Lanka, he is an expert gyan yogi. When he becomes tiny to enter Lanka, he does so as a bhakti yogi. When he makes himself extremely tiny in front of Mother Sita, it is a symbol of total surrender. Only Hanuman knows how to use the best of all avenues in the service of the Lord.

Sundarkanda is about our purpose in life – our own search for a goal in life. Hanuman shows us that the goal of life is Sita, who he is searching for in this kanda. For a gyani, Sita is shanti or peace. For a devotee, Sita is bhakti. For a yogi, She is adishakti or subtle strength. The truth is that everyone is in search of Sita.
