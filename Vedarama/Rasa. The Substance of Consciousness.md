# Rasa. The Substance of Consciousness

## Charles A. Filion

Vedanta is not universally known for what it truly is, the best philosophical system in the world insofar as it acknowledges the existence of the soul and propounds a method to achieve self-realization, because by tradition it is learned only through the lens of a sectarian school of thought, as though the Vedanta sun were too bright to directly look at, and because the goal which is the academic understanding of Vedanta is different from the two main goals in putting the philosophy into practice:

1. The Advaitins’ goal of achieving liberation, through deep meditation,
2. The devotees’ goal of experiencing bhakti-rasa, the sheer joy of divine devotion, through devotional music or spiritual practice.

The theory which is the proper academic understanding of Vedanta, expounded below, differs from both the theory in Advaita-Vedanta and the theory in the bhakti sects. There are three reasons.

Firstly, the proper academic understanding of Vedanta doesn’t even exist in academia. It is lacking. What is taught at university is either Advaita theory or bhakti theory, whereas in truth Vedanta is the harmonized synthesis of both, as pointed out in the aphorism: ubhaya-vyapadesdt tv ahi- kundala-vat, “But, because of both kinds of statements, Brahman and the souls are like a snake and its coil” (Vedanta-sutra [3.2.27](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/3.2.27)). The souls have, in essence, the same transcendental nature as Brahman yet are different from It. A drop of water is not exactly the same as an ocean.

The Advaitins stress Vedantic texts that declare the concept of advaita (or abheda), meaning existence in the absolute sense is non-dual, as in the text: tat tvam asi, “You are That” (Chandogya Upanisad [6.8.7](https://vedarama.com/en/books/chan-upa/6.8.7)), thereby negating the existence of the souls. The devotees, however, emphasize Vedantic texts which declare the concept of dvaita (or bheda), that is, absolute existence can be manifold: the souls are real. Vedantic texts state that a soul is transcendental:

- mamaivamso jiva-loke jiva-bhutah sanatanah, “In the world of living beings, My spark which is a soul is eternal” (Bhagavad-gita [15.7](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/15.7));
- amso nana-vyapadesad anyatha capi dasa-kitavaditvam adhiyata eke, “A soul is part of Brahman, because there are many such statements in the scriptures” (Vedanta-sutra [2.3.41](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/2.3.41));
- vijnanam brahma ced veda, tasmac cen na pramadyati, sarire papmano hitva sarvan kaman samasnute, “If one knows that the vijnana-maya purusa (a soul) is transcendental, and if one makes no big mistake in life, one sheds off all sins upon giving up the body and fulfills all desires” (Taittiriya Upanisad [2.5](https://vedarama.com/en/books/taittiriya/2.5)).

Vedanta is, in fact, a philosophy of paradox (acintya-bhedabheda).

Secondly, sometimes the proper academic understanding of Vedanta is completely at odds with either Advaita theory or bhakti theory. For instance, Sankara rejects canonical texts of Vedanta. He writes: yat tuktam sastrarthavattvadibhir hetubhih svabhavikam atmanah kartrtvam iti tan na, “It was stated with several reasons, beginning from sastrarthavattvat (Vedanta-sutra [2.3.33](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/2.3.33)), that the doership of the soul is inherent. That is wrong” (Sariraka-bhasya 2.3.40).

In my article intitled “Why Advaita is not Vedanta”, I show more examples, including why Sankara is grammatically wrong in interpreting anadi mat-param brahma, “Brahman is beginningless and is inferior to Me” (Bhagavad-gita [13.13](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/13.13)) as anadimatparam brahma (Brahman is beginningless and supreme). He is wrong because the suffix mat\[up\] cannot be used at the end of a bahuvrihi compound. The point here is twofold:

1. Paramesvara (God) is an entity superior to Brahman;
2. Sakti (Mother Nature) belongs to Paramesvara, not to Brahman: parasya saktir vividhaiva sruyate svabhaviki, “It is heard that His transcendental potency is manifold and is inherent in Him” (Svetasvatara Upanisad [6.8](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sveta-bs/6.8)).

The bhakti theory, too, is sometimes blatantly wrong. According to the bhakti sects, the world is real in the sense that the existence of the world consists of the sat aspect of Brahman’s attribute of sac-cid-ananda (transcendental, lit. existing spiritual bliss). In my article “Unified Theory of Vedanta”, I demolish this idea by proving from Upanishadic texts that the concept of sac-cid- ananda is one in essence (eka-rasa): the three aspects cannot be separated from one another.

Thirdly, having the proper academic understanding of Vedanta is sometimes a major hindrance in trying to achieve one of the two other goals. For instance, Sankara and stalwarts of his doctrine border on misogyny; this ideology, though not accepted in modern society, inspires men to renounce the world and meditate. As an another example, the Niralamba Upanisad states: brahma satyam jagan mithya, “Brahman is real. The world is unreal (like seeing a rope as a snake).” This, too, greatly fosters complete renunciation, whereas in truth this Upanishad is rejectable because it is not one of the 11 major Upanishads, which are part of the prasthana-trayi, the canonical texts of Vedanta.

The bhakti sects, for their part, foster false beliefs in their followers by relying heavily on the Indian background of religion, which is deeply rooted in cultural beliefs in the caste system, in the absolute power of gurus, in the existence of gods such as Brahma and Indra, and in the idea that the Vedas, the Puranas and other scriptures are divine revelations. Once the spirituality of Vedanta is stripped of those false notions, it is almost impossible to experience an exalted bhakti mood.

The bhakti mood is the transcendental Rasa. The concept of Rasa (mood; aesthetic delight), first expounded in Natya-sastra, is central in Indian poetics. That is the Rasa experienced in material life. The transcendental Rasa, however, is in the scope of bhakti. In short, the term Rasa denotes the combination of ananda (joy) and camatkara (astonishment). Depending on the context, other synonyms are: enthusiasm, mojo, and groove.

Furthermore, my research on the topic of Rasa reveals that, in Indian studies as taught in academia, one important concept is missing.

Although the bhakti theory expounds the concept of transcendental Rasa, it restricts it to Paramesvara, who is the object of devotion, and to bhakti-rasa (sheer joy of divine devotion), the goal to be attained. This is the foundational text expounding transcendental Rasa: raso vai sah.

*rasam hy evayam labdvanand bhavati.*  
*ko hy evanyat kah pranyat?*  
*yad esa akase anando na syat.*  
*esa hy evanandayati.*

**God is Rasa. Upon experiencing this Rasa, one becomes blissful. Without this bliss in the sky of the heart, who could inhale? Who could exhale? Verily, this gives bliss. (Taittiriya Upanisad** [**2.7**](https://vedarama.com/en/books/taittiriya/2.7)**)**

However, Pandita-raja Jagannatha, in his treatise on poetics called Rasa-gangadhara (c. 1665), proposed the idea, based on the mystical experience, achieved by reading poetry, of connoisseurs such as himself, that the soul too has the nature of transcendental Rasa: cid eva rasah, “The consciousness of the soul is the Rasa” (Rasa-gangadhara).

I wrote a book, called Versified History of Sanskrit Poetics: The Soul is Rasa (2016), in order to highlight Jagannatha’s philosophical discovery, which is nowehere mentioned in other books on the history of Sanskrit poetics.

The experience of transcendental Rasa is the goal of reading poetry and is a once-in-a-lifetime epiphany. Sanskrit is the language most conducive to the experience of Rasa because Sanskrit facilitates the usage of puns and thereby often evokes astonishment and because romance in Indian literary culture is highly refined.

The process of the mystical experience of poetry is this: Once the pleasure of reading an awesome literary text takes place, the pleasure keeps increasing by reveling in the beauty of the text; the good vibes increase. That is the material mojo. In a moment of epiphany, one then thinks of a very astonishing implied meaning or implied play on words, usually in the scope of romantic love, and at once this huge astonishment triggers the joy of the soul to flood the mind. That is the mojo of transcendental consciousness. Rapture can also be achieved in the scope of humor, by watching a comedian on stage, and so on.

In his own treatise on poetics, Baladeva Vidyabhusana, the famed Gaudiya commentator on Vedanta-sutra, agreed with Jagannatha by rephrasing his dictum and replacing the word cid (transcendental consciousness) with atma (soul): atmaivanandamse vibhavadibhir bhagnavaranas tad-vyajta-raty-ady-avacchinnas tat-sannibhas ca rasah, “The soul whose layer of its aspect of bliss is shattered by the vibhavas (the persons and the things), the anubhavas (the bodily reactions), and the vyabhicari-bhavas (transient emotions) in the text is the Rasa, which is particularized by the foundational mood, implied by those ones, and which is like that bliss” (Sahitya-kaumudi 4.10) (Kavya-mala ed. p. 31).

The soul consists not only of sac-cid-ananda, but of Rasa, because the souls come from Paramesvara, whose nature is Rasa. This text of Bhagavad-gita hints at the nature of the soul:

*ascarya-vat pasyati kascid enam*  
*ascarya-vad vadati tathaiva canyah*  
*ascarya-vac cainam anyah srnoti*  
*srutvapy enam veda na caiva kascit*

**Some look on the soul as amazing, some describe the soul as amazing, some hear of the soul as amazing, whereas others cannot understand the soul even after hearing about it. (**[**2.29**](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/2.29)**)**

Philosophy is the basis of Sanskrit poetics, and poetics thus explains the philosophy. Things have come full circle.

Eventually, it dawned on me that if Sakti comes from Paramesvara, she too must have the nature of Rasa, albeit material, and if Sakti is the driving force of the universe, it too must have the nature of Rasa. And so the result of my twenty-year research in Vedanta is a comprehensive theory of Rasa which explains the driving force of everything in the universe.

The Rasa theory is the real “theory of everything”, a theory which physicists seek to formulate in order to unify the three theories of quantum mechanics, which relate to sub-atomic particles, with the theory of general relativity, which focuses on gravity. Consciousness, however, is more powerful than matter, as the saying goes: Mind over matter. For instance, the mind can alter the swing of a pendulum. As the material Rasa is the essence of the mind, so the transcendental Rasa is the essence of the soul.

The universe is much more than interactions of matter. And matter can give rise to life: Mother Nature is a living force. A galaxy, not to mention scenery on Earth, looks amazing because Mother Nature has the nature of Rasa.

In day-to-day life too, the world revolves on the carousel of Rasa. Sanskrit poetics teaches that Rasa takes one of these forms in poetry (rasa = mood) and in life (rasa = mojo): Srngara (romantic love), hasya (humor), vira (manliness), raudra (anger), karuna (lamentation), bhayanaka (fear), bibhatsa (disgust), adbhuta (only amazement), Santa (peacefulness), and bhakti (devotion to God).

Those are the foundational moods (sthayi-bhava). Subsequent poetical theorists added vatsalya (parental affection), sakhya (friendship), guru-rati (great respect for a teacher or elder), and so on. Life has an infinity of flavors to offer, all of which are meant to be included in these categories.

The concept of Rasa culminates in Lila (fun). In Vedanta, God’s purpose in creating the universe is pleasure: loka-vat tu lila-kaivalyam, “But \[God is not devoid of purpose in creating the universe: His purpose is\] only fun, like anyone in the world may want to have fun” (Vedanta- sutra [2.1.33](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/2.1.33)). The universe is the play of Paramesvara, and so Siva, identified as Him, is represented as the cosmic dancer (Nataraja).

Three elements of the Rasa theory in Sanskrit poetics are very useful in day-to-day life too, insofar as they are guidelines that help us lead a good life.

Firstly, everyone should search for ways to evoke their mojo, and should do so by seeking experiences that are new and amazing.

Secondly, Rasa in interpersonal relationships is based on appropriateness. Anandavardhana stated: anaucityam rte ndnyad rasa-bhangasya kdranam, “Other than inappropriateness, there is no cause of the disruption of Rasa” (Dhvany-aloka).

Lastly, communication is most elegant when it is indirect. For instance, Shri Krishna said: veda brahmdtma-visayds tri-kdnda-visayd ime, paroksa-vddd rsayah paroksam mama ca priyam, “The Vedas consist of three parts (rituals, worship of gods, and realization of Brahman) and explain that a soul is transcendental. The Vedic mantras are esoteric, and the Vedic seers speak in esoteric terms. I, too, prefer an indirect mode of expression” (Bhagavata Purana [11.21.35](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sb-11/21.35)). The universe communicates indirectly. Symbolism is an essential aspect of life. Everybody intuitively knows that there are signs in life. In the Rasa theory, the existence of implied meanings is in the scope of the literary function called vyanjana (Suggestiveness).

The Rasa theory best explains the mystery of life. This is another reason why Vedanta should be regarded as the best philosophy in the world. The Vedanta sun makes us see the world as it really is.
