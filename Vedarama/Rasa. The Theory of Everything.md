# Rasa. The Theory of Everything

## Charles A. Filion

Isaac Newton, Albert Einstein and other illustrious scientists have sought to explain the universe from the viewpoint of science in the search for the theory of everything, an all-encompassing theoretical framework of physics that would fully elucidate and link together all physical aspects of the universe.

The interest of both the quantum physicists and the general populace for such a theory is so great that the movie about the late Stephen Hawking’s life story is called The Theory of Everything (2014).

However, consciousness is more powerful than matter. For example, the mind can alter the swing of a pendulum. What is consciousness? What is its nature? The giants of Sanskrit literature made tremendous findings in this regard. The Rasa Theory, which ultimately explains the one thing that drives everything in the universe, is the theory of everything, insofar as the universe is much more than interactions of matter. Mother Nature is a living force.

The Upanisads and other Vedic texts expound the concept of Brahman, the omnipresent field of transcendental consciousness, from which all souls originate. An experienced yogi can cognize Brahman. Gautama Buddha and other perfected yogis are called Jivan-muktas (liberated while living) because they achieved full trance in Brahman and because in the waking state they maintain the mode of saksin (witness), also called drastr (independent observer): the soul is the witness of the mental functions and of the bodily functions. Trance in Brahman is twofold:

- samprajnata-samadhi (trance with awareness of oneself as a soul);
- asamprajnata-samadhi (trance without any awareness of individu­ality), also called nirvikalpa-samadhi (trance without any distinction).

In the Yoga system, samprajnata-samadhi is called sabija-samadhi (trance with a seed) and asamprajnata-samadhi is called nirbija- samadhi (trance without a seed).

In the state prior to samprajnata-samadhi, the soul is the witness of the mind, whereas in samprajnata-samadhi, also called turiya (the fourth state), the soul is only aware of its existence. In both instances, a soul (jivatman, or jiva for short) is called saksi-jiva (the witnessing soul). The five states of consciousness are:

1. visva, the waking state;
2. taijasa, the dreaming state;
3. prajna, deep sleep;
4. turiya (or turya), transcendence;
5. turiyatita (also called atman), the supreme form of transcendence.

In some Vedic texts, only the first four states are mentioned; in that scheme, the fourth state encompasses everything transcendental.

Those five (or four) correspond to the three kinds of existence:

- vyavaharika-satta (worldly existence), also called vyavaharika- satyam (relative existence);
- pratibhasika-satta (illusory existence: any mirage, dream, lie, etc.), also called mithya (illusion);
- paramarthika-satta (transcendental existence), also called paramarthika-satyam (absolute reality).

In the fifth state, after turiya, a soul is called pure (suddha atma) and is merged in Brahman.

Sankaracarya and other Advaita-vadins (monists), for whom a soul is unreal, say a soul thus becomes Paramatman hence a Jivan-mukta is as good as Isvara (an Avatara like Sri Krishna), whereas the Vaishnavas argue that Brahman is not the same as Paramatman; this is the substance of the aphorism that begins: jagad-vyapara-varjam, “\[A liberated soul has powers\] except the power of creating a universe” (Vedanta-sutra [4.4.17](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/4.4.17)).

Paramatman, the inner Soul, is the same as Paramesvara (God), the omnipresent, omniscient and omnipotent entity. In all living beings, Paramesvara accompanies each soul in the Heart Cakra, and is called Paramatman, who, like the jivatman (soul), is an independent observer: saksi ceta kevalo nirgunas ca, “He is the witness, makes one conscious, is absolute, and is devoid of the three modes of material nature” (Svetasvatara Upanisad [6.11](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sveta-bs/6.11)).

Paramesvara is also called Purusa (man, person) because He is the male counterpart of Mother Nature and also because, like a person, He reciprocates, and so forth. In the yogic tradition, the grace of Paramatman is needed to reach the state of nirvikalpa-samadhi. This is the origin of the concept of bhakti (devotion to God).

The main differences between Brahman and Paramesvara are that the latter has sankalpa (volition), sakti (power), and rasa (rapture). The bliss of Paramesvara is called Rasa because the bliss is based on the relation of subject and object and because it is filled with wonder. In the fourth state (turiya), if Paramatman manifests His presence to the jivatman, then the soul has the bliss called santa-rasa in its ultimate sense (rapture of saintliness). The most mystical text of Vedanta philosophy is this:

*raso vai sah.*  
*rasam hy evayam labdhvanandi bhavati.*  
*ko hy evanyat kah pranyat?*  
*yad esa akase anando na syat.*  
*esa hy evanandayati.*

**God is Rasa. Upon experiencing this Rasa, one becomes blissful. Without this bliss in the sky of the heart, who could inhale? Who could exhale? Verily, this gives bliss. (Taittiriya Upanisad** [**2.7**](https://vedarama.com/en/books/taittiriya/2.7)**)**

As a pun, this citation also humourously refers to the stoppage of breath in the state of nirvikalpa-samadhi.

In usage, the word rasa has many meanings: (1) aesthetic delight (in the context of art), (2) rapture, (3) pleasure (mojo, magical vibe) (in general language), and (4) juice (or taste; liquid). The Sanskrit poetical theorists have greatly expounded upon the concepts of aesthetic delight and rapture. Both concepts are sourced in Bharata Muni’s Natya-sastra (100 BCE). In the sense of rapture, Rasa is a technical term which signifies the combination of bliss (ananda) and astonishment (camatkara).

Firstly, in literature, a sthayi-bhava (foundational mood: romance, bravery, humor, etc.) that has become relishable is called rasa (aesthetic delight), by the definition: vibhavanubhava-vyabhicari- samyogad rasa-nispattih, “The effectuation of rasa occurs from the blend of vibhavas (subject and object, and stimuli depicted), anubhava (bodily reaction), and vyabhicari-bhava (transient emotion)” (Natya-sastra 6.32). This text is commonly known as the Rasa-sutra (aphorismic definition of aesthetic delight). It applies to a literary text and to dramaturgy. Bharata Muni stated: vibhavanubhava-vyabhicari-parivrtah sthayi bhavo rasa-nama labhate, “A sthayi-bhava surrounded by vibhavas, an anubhava and a vyabhicari is called rasa” (Natya-sastra 7.8). The sthayi-bhava is implied, by the literary function called vyanjana (Suggestiveness), by the vibhavas, anubhava, and vyabhicarin expressed in the text and is called rasa (aesthetic delight) by the derivation: rasyata iti rasah, “It is relished, and so it is called rasa” (Sahitya-darpana 1.3).

The three literary functions are: abhidha (Denotation), laksana (Indication) and vyanjana (Suggestiveness). Still, subsequent poetical theorists opined that literary ornaments (ref. Bhatta Nayaka’s literary function called bhavakatvam), fancy grammatical forms (ref. Mammata’s padamsas: Kavya-prakasa 4.43), and so on, and not necessarily an anubhava or a vyabhicarin, can raise the sthayi-bhava to the level of rasa.

Secondly, once the state of aesthetic delight has been achieved, then, if the reader or spectator very greatly relishes an implied meaning (vastu-dhvani), the soul infuses its bliss into the mind. This is called literary rapture, and is a once-in-a-lifetime epiphany.

Abhinavagupta (c. 1000 CE) explains: sa ca rasana-rupa pratitir utpadyate. vacya-vacakayos tatrabhidhadi-vivikto vyanjanatma dhvanana-vyapara eva, “The perception \[of rasa\] that originates is the form of rasana. It is the exact same function called Suggestiveness, yet it is the soul of Suggestiveness” (Locana 2.4). He expounds: sa ca rasana na pramana-vyaparo na karaka-vyaparah. svayam tu napramanikisva-samvedana-siddhatvat. rasana ca bodha- rupaiva kintu bodhantarebhyo laukikebhyo vilaksanaiva, upayanam vibhavadinam laukika-vailaksanyat. tena vibhavadi-samyogad rasana yato nispadyate ’tas tatha-vidha-rasana-gocaro lokottaro ’rtho rasa iti tatparyam sutrasya, “Rasana is a mental function that cannot be externally proven, nor is it a function that is an active force. However, it is not devoid of proof, because it can be proven by one’s experience of it. Rasana is simply a form of cognizance, but is obviously distinct from other, worldly types of cognizance, since the vibhavas and so on, the means, are distinct from worldly causes, bodily reactions, and transient emotions. Consequently the purport of Bharata Muni’s sutra is this: Since rasana has become effected from the blend of vibhavas and so on, an extraordinary meaning in the scope of rasana becomes Rasa (rapture)” (Abhinava-bharati 6.32). Bharata Muni had stated this as follows:

*yo ’rtho hrdaya-samvadi tasya bhavo rasodbhavah*  
*sariram vyapyate tena suskam kastham ivagnina*

**An emotion that relates to a meaning which speaks to the heart gives rise to Rasa. The body is pervaded by it like dry wood by fire. (Natya-sastra 7.7)**

Realizing the soul as Rasa can be achieved by taking interest in first-class poetry over many years. Sanskrit poetics teaches that rasa takes one of these forms in poetry (rasa = aesthetic delight) and in life (rasa = mojo): srngara (romantic love), hasya (humor), vira (bravery), raudra (anger), karuna (lamentation), bhayanaka (fear), bibhatsa (disgust), adbhuta (only amazement), santa (peacefulness), and bhakti (devotion to God).

Those are the foundational moods (sthayi-bhava). Subsequent poetical theorists added vatsalya (parental affection), sakhya (friendship), guru-rati (great respect for a teacher), and so on. Life has an infinity of flavors to offer, all of which are meant to be included in these categories, and so Pandita-raja Jagannatha (c. 1650 CE), for example, created these subcategories of vira-rasa (bravery) in addition to the well-known four (dana-vira, hero of giving; daya- vira, hero of compassion; yuddha-vira, heroic warrior; and dharma- vira, hero of ethics): satya-vira (hero of virtue), panditya-vira (hero of learning), ksama-vira (hero of tolerance), and bala-vira (hero of brute force). In life, a man must be a vira in one way or another.

The Sanskrit language is most conducive to the experience of rasa especially because this language facilitates the usage of puns and thereby often evokes astonishment.

Symbolism is an essential aspect of life. Everybody intuitively knows that there are signs in life. In the Rasa Theory, this concept, the existence of implied meanings, is covered by the literary function called vyanjana (Suggestiveness). In life as in poetry, the flash of insight (pratibha) in the observer arises from experience. The Rasa Theory best explains life. God, or the universe, communicates indirectly: Acausal events can be connected in meaning. (In that regard, Carl Jung (1875-1961) coined the term synchronicity.) Krishna said:

*veda brahmatma-visayas tri-kanda-visaya ime*  
*paroksa-vada rsayah paroksam mama ca priyam*

**The Vedas consist of three parts (rites; worship of gods; and realization of the Absolute) and explain that the soul is transcen­dental. The Vedic mantras are esoteric and the Vedic seers speak in esoteric terms. I too prefer an indirect mode of expression. (Bhagavatam** [**11.21.35**](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sb-11/21.35)**)**

Most Sanskrit poetical theorists were affiliated with a particular branch of Indian philosophy, and so the idea that the Rasa Theory is rooted in the ultimate reality is a major concept in the history of Sanskrit poetics. Dr. Keith writes:

... the revelation of something inherent (vyanjana) is found in the Vedanta, where all is a manifestation of the underlying reality, the Brahman or absolute.

Dr. Kane states:

When dhvani or rasa is said to be the soul of poetry (kavyasyatma dhvanir iti) it is virtually employing the language of the Upanisads about atman and ananda (anando brahmeti vyajanat).

The proponents of the Dhvani school of poetics constructed their literary theory (in regard to these topics and more: Are words real? What is the essence of poetry? How is an implied meaning cognized?) in such a way that it mirrors their philosophy, Kashmiri Saivism. For example, Anandavardhana subtly referred to the Pratyabhijna system of philosophy by stating, with the participial formpratyabhijneyau, that a great poet is one who is able to recognize which sounds and which meanings can cause the manifestation of a first-rate implied sense: yatnatah pratyabhijneyau tau sabdarthau maha-kaveh (Dhvany-aloka 1.8). Conversely, the Bhagavad-gita and other sacred books on Indian philosophy are founded on poetic expression.

The Pratyabhijna system of Abhinavagupta and of other Kashmiri Saivites propounded a philosophy of life, in contrast to Sankaracarya’s Vivarta philosophy (the Advaita branch of Vedanta), by which any genuine value of the material world is denied. Dr. Raghunath Safaya expounds:

The greatest contribution of Kashmir to Indian culture is the development of a new philosophy, more rational than other philosophies of India, and a definite improvement upon Vedanta philosophy. Unlike Vedanta which regards the physical world a trap and delusion (Maya) and creates a tendency of withdrawing from worldly life, Kashmiri Saivism accepts the reality of the phenomenal world as a manifestation of the Universal mind. It is a synthesis of the realism of the West and idealism of the East, welding the science (of the material world) and religion in a devotional monotheism. A Kashmirian could not afford to shut his eyes from the enchanting beauty of nature revealed in his homeland, and call it unreal. But instead he calls it manifestation of the divinity, or the divine energy (Sakti) which is the source of the whole movement of the universe, and Siva—Universal mind. It is this divine energy that acts as central fire, stirring each and every atom (Anu) with its sparks.

Jiva is nothing but the atom with the divine spark. Siva, Sakti and Anu are thus the three fundamental principles of Saivism. It is, therefore, named as Trika philosophy. It gave Kashmir a revelation of life as a real dynamic endowed with creative possibilities, and not as a deception or illusion. It retorted that maya of Sankara had a defeatist tone, symptomatic of disillusionment and loss to the individual and the nation.

Bhatta Nayaka, Abhinavagupta and Visvanatha Kaviraja said the bliss of relishing Rasa is like the bliss of the soul. Pandita-raja Jagannatha said the bliss of relishing Rasa is the bliss of the soul: “Transcendental consciousness (cit) (the soul’s awareness), the covering of which is shattered and which is particularized by the sthayin, is the Rasa.”

Thus Pandita-raja Jagannatha lived up to his title. Baladeva Vidya- bhйsana changed the term cit to atman (jivatman), but the general idea is the same, since a soul is sac-cid-ananda (transcendental). Baladeva clearly established the philosophical conclusion that the ultimate nature of the soul is Rasa: atmaivanandamse vibhavadibhir bhagndvaranas tad-vyanjita-raty-ady-avacchinnas tat-sannibhas ca rasah, “The soul whose covering in the aspect of bliss is shattered by the vibhavas and so on is the Rasa, which is particularized by the sthayin, implied by those ones, and which is like that bliss.” (Sahitya-kaumudi 4.10) (Kavya-mala edition, p. 31).

Dr. Shankarji Jha observes:

And, in this state, a perceiver feels raso ’ham, “I am Rasa” or “Rasa is I”, and thus identifies himself with Rasa. According to the Vedantic theory, a soul is the very person in the form of light and bliss, and such indeed is Rasa.

Furthermore, this unique experience cannot take place until and unless the cover of ignorance on the Soul is removed. \[...\] Through the combination of the trinity of Vibhava etc. the revealed state of Sthayin is Rasa.

In describing his artha-диnа (quality of the meaning) called samadhi, Vamana (c. 800 CE) said that an implied sense can be a cause of samadhi, when grasping the meaning occurs as a result of mental concentration, and so this диnа of his is called samadhi because it is a cause of samadhi (trance).

Jagannatha gives two scriptural texts to prove that Rasa is transcendental. Firstly, the highest bliss is attainable by the intelligence, in accordance with Bhagavad-gita. And secondly, since God is Rasa, by the aforementioned dictum: raso vai sah (Taittiriya Up.), it follows that the souls are specks of Rasa. This explains Krishna’s statement in the Gita :

*ascarya-vat pasyati kascid enam*  
*ascarya-vad vadati tathaiva canyah*  
*ascarya-vac cainam anyah srnoti*  
*srutvapy enam veda na caiva kascit*

**Some look on the soul as amazing, some describe the soul as amazing, some hear of the soul as amazing, whereas others cannot understand the soul even after hearing about it. (Bhagavad-gita** [**2.29**](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/2.29)**)**

Philosophy is the basis of Sanskrit poetics, and poetics thus explains the philosophy. Things have come full circle.

Vedanta philosophy states that a soul is transcendental:

- mamaivamso jiva-loke jiva-bhutah sanatanah, “In the world of living beings, My spark which is a soul is eternal” (Gita [15.7](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/15.7));
- ubhaya-vyapadesat tv ahi-kutulala-vat, “But, because of both kinds of statements (bheda and abheda), Brahman and the souls are like a snake and its coil” (Vedanta-sutra [3.2.27](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/3.2.27));
- amso nana-vyapadesad anyatha capi dasa-kitavaditvam adhiyata eke (Vedanta-sutra [2.3.41](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/2.3.41));
- vijnanam brahma ced veda, tasmac cen na pramadyati, sarire papmano hitva sarvan kaman samasnute, “If one knows that the vijnana-maya purusa (a soul) is transcendental, and if one makes no big mistake in life, one sheds off all sins upon giving up the body and fulfills all one’s desires” (Taittiriya Up. [2.5](https://vedarama.com/en/books/taittiriya/2.5)).

Abhinavagupta defines bliss as follows: anando nirvrty-atma camatkarapara-paryayah, “Bliss is the life of joy and is a synonym of astonishment” (Locana 1.1). Bliss inherently involves some degree of astonishment, but when the astonishment goes through the roof, by the force of an over-the-top implied sense, the combination of bliss and astonishment is called Rasa (rapture).

The Rasa theory is even more fundamental than Einstein’s formula: E = mc2, because Rasa is the substratum of everything. Now let us examine the relation between rasa and Rasa from a metaphysical perspective. The world revolves on rasa (mojo). The term mojo in that sense has become popular in American sports television since the beginning of the millennium. Related terms in popular culture are groove, swagger, charisma, enthusiasm, and good vibrations. Indeed, the famous scientist Nikola Tesla (1856-1943) said, “If you want to find the secrets of the universe, think in terms of energy, frequency and vibration.”

Mojo is felt everywhere to some degree, all the time. For example, the lyrics in one of the best dance songs of all time are:

Rhythm is a dancer. It’s a soul companion. You can feel it everywhere. Lift your hands and voices. Free your mind and join us. You can feel it in the air. Oh it’s a passion. Oh you can feel it. (Rhythm is a Dancer by Snap!) (1992)

People unconsciously equate rasa with passion when they say “Follow your passion” and the like. Everyone automatically and unknowingly seeks to realize that the soul is Rasa. For that purpose, those who are in the mode of tamo-gunа (lethargy) use narcotics, those in the mode of rajo-gunа (action/ passion) have intercourse, and those in the mode of sattva-gunа (goodness) practice meditation, sing devotional melodies, develop the Buddhist concept of feeling unconditional love for all beings, and so on.

For the most part, the rapture obtainable by means of poetry is srngara-rasa (romantic love) in the context of bhakti, such as Krishna’s pastimes with His women, and can be achieved by those who are in the mode of sattva-guna with a touch of rajo-guna.

The process is this: Once the pleasure (mojo) of reading poetry has occurred, the pleasure increases by reveling in the beauty of the text; the good vibes increase. Then, in a moment of epiphany, one thinks of a very astonishing implied play on words (related to srngara): one revels in the meaning, and this huge astonishment triggers the joy of the soul to flood the mind. The experience of Rasa is reminiscent of this maxim by Plutarch: “The mind is not a vessel to be filled, but a fire to be kindled.”

Rapture can also be achieved in the scope of hasya-rasa (humor) by being in an audience and listening to the comedian on stage. In an interview, the American comedian Chris Rock talked about an event one night when, while waiting backstage, he heard the people in the audience of his opening act, Martin Lawrence, laugh so hard that he thought “a riot had broke out.”

The fact that the soul is Rasa explains why we humans are who we are and why we do what we do. Music and all other forms of art, and life itself, proceed from and revolve around this truth.

Life is the astonishing paradox of being a witness and being an actor at the same time, in reference to William Shakespeare: “All the world’s a stage, And all the men and women merely players. They have their exits and their entrances. And one man in his time plays many parts, His acts being seven ages” (As You Like It).

Evoke your mojo. Children are happy by nature because their ego is not yet big enough to block the influx of the joy of the soul. Even seemingly dry dealings like business transactions between people take place because of the persons’ rasa (mojo). The world champion of public speaking in 2014, Dananjaya Hettiarachchi, won chiefly because of this line, which he often repeated and which hints at the concept that a soul is Rasa: “I see something in you, but I don’t know what it is.”

Rasa is the most fundamental principle of the universe. It is the substance of consciousness. In the highest sense, the term Rasa is a synonym of Paramesvara (God). God is transcendental Rasa; every soul is transcendental Rasa; and Sakti (Mother Nature) is the material rasa (shadow effect of Rasa): Nature is beautiful and amazing because the universe is run by Sakti, which has the nature of rasa. Sakti is the material energy of God. The mind, a product of Mother Nature, of each and every one of us has material rasa, activated by the hormone called dopamine. Moreover, this Vedic scripture confirms that the mind can be infused by the consciousness of the soul:

*vaikarikas taijasas ca tamasas cety aham tri-vrt*  
*tanmatrendriya-manasam karanam cid-acin-mayah*

**The ego has three varieties: sattvika (good), rajasika (passionate) and tamasika (lethargic). It is the cause of the sensory objects, of the senses and of the mind. It consists of both matter and spirit. (Bhagavata Purana** [**11.24.7**](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sb-11/24.7)**)**

The ego (ahankara) is inherently acit (material), but here it is called cid-acin-maya insofar as the ego is infused by the consciousness of the soul. The commentator, Sridhara Svami, explains: cid-acin-mayah, cid-abhasa-vyaptatvad ubhaya-granthi-rupa iti, “The term cid-acin-maya means the ego is a knot of both matter and spirit because the ego is pervaded by the soul” (Bhavartha-dlpika 11.24.7). The doership of the soul consists of volition. This is the substance of the aphorism: karta sastrathavattvat, “The soul is a doer, because this is as if the purpose of the scriptures” (Vedanta-sutra [2.3.33](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/2.3.33)). Sankaracarya says the author, Badarayana, is mistaken, and so he rejects this entire section (adhikarana) of Vedanta-sutra. In his commentary on: yatha ca taksobhayatah, “\[The soul is a doer\] in two ways, like a carpenter (by volition and through instruments: transcendental senses)” (Vedanta-sutra [2.3.40](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/2.3.40)), Sankaracarya writes: yat tuktam scisircirihavanvcidibhir hetubhih svabhavikam atmanah kartrtvam iti tan na, “It was stated with several reasons, beginning from sastrarthavattvat (Vedanta-sutra [2.3.33](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/2.3.33)), that the doership of the soul is inherent: That is wrong” (Sariraka-bhasya 2.3.40). Sankaracarya thinks that a soul is never a doer, but he is mistaken, by considering that there is no contradiction with this text: prakrteh knyamcmani gunaih karmrni sarvasah, ahahkara- vimudhatma kartaham iti manyate, “All actions are being done by the three modes of Nature. One who thinks “I am the doer” is bewildered by ego” (Gita [3.27](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/3.27)) because the respective scopes are different: The aphorism karta sastrathavattvat relates to spiritual activities, such as meditation, as indicated by this aphorism in the same section: samadhy-abhavac ca, “\[A soul is a doer\] also because otherwise there would be no trance” (Vedanta-sutra [2.3.39](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/2.3.39)), and to volition in general.

The material rasa and the transcendental Rasa are not mutually exclusive. Not only that, the life force in the body has a dual nature: material and spiritual. The latter is the vehicle of the soul and is dependent on the volition of the soul. It can be activated in times of grave danger and is sometimes viewed as a miraculous event. For example, a young boy once saved his father from certain death by manifesting tremendous force in jacking up a car, under which his father was trapped, but was never able to jack it up again in a normal situation. The Vedic scriptures hint at the transcendental life force as follows: sa u pra/iasya prrnah, caksusas caksuh, “God is the life force of the life force, the eye of the eye” (Kena Upanisad [1.2](https://vedarama.com/en/books/kena/1.2)).

The monists, however, say Paramesvara is nothing but Brahman, and Sakti belongs to Brahman. But this makes no sense, because they also say that in Brahman, there is no differentiation between knower, means of knowing, and object of knowledge (object of the awareness). Thus, they contradict themselves by stating that Brahman is conscious of Sakti.

The monists deny anything superior to Brahman. In the Gita, Krishna, identified as Paramesvara, says to Arjuna: brahmano hi pratisthaham, “I am the foundation of Brahman” ([14.27](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/14.27)), but the monists interpret the word brahmanah in the ablative case (from Brahman), not in the genitive. In like manner, Krishna states:

*jneyam yat tat pravaksyami yaj jnatvamrtam asnute*  
*anadi mat-param brahma na sat tan nasad ucyate*

**“Now I will tell you what should be known, knowing which one tastes the nectar of immortality. It is Brahman, which is beginningless and is inferior to Me. From the worldly perspective, Brahman is neither existing nor nonexisting.” (Gita** [**13.13**](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/13.13)**)**

The monists, however, interpret the above line “anadi mat-param brahma” (Brahman is beginningless and is inferior to Me) as “anadi-mat param brahma” (Brahman has no beginning and is the Supreme). They say that although the word anadi-mat is grammatically wrong because adding the suffix mat\[up\], which expresses possession, is redundant given that the bahuvrihi compound anadi (it has no beginning) is already expressive of possession, the usage of that suffix here is justified since it fills the meter of the verse. This interpretation is wrong because nowhere in the Vedic texts has the suffix mat\[up\] ever been used to fill the meter. Therefore the word brahmanah in verse [14.27](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/14.27) cited above must be taken in the genitive. The monists say the goal of life is to merge in Brahman. They say the soul is unreal, and deny that there can be a variety of transcendental bliss, and so they reject the notion of transcendental Rasa. The monists reject any concept of transcendental energy.

The universe is the play of Paramesvara. This is why Siva, identified as Paramesvara, is represented as the cosmic dancer (Nataraja). A statue of Nataraja is at the entrance of the CERN Institute in Switzerland. The concept of Rasa culminates in Lila (fun). God’s purpose in creating the universe is pleasure: loka-vat tu llla- kaivalyam, “But \[God is not devoid of purpose in creating the universe: His purpose is\] only fun, like anyone in the world might want to have fun” (Vedanta-sutra [2.1.33](https://vedarama.com/en/books/vs/2.1.33)).

Am I trying to prove that God exists? Not quite. Rather, I believe in miracles. For instance, my friend’s father cannot explain why, when it came down to it, he was unable to plant his hunting knife in my friend’s back when he was a kid. My take on metaphysics is a theory of paradox: There is a God and there is no God. The universe is in God (Brahman / Paramesvara), but God is not inherently in the universe. God exists outside the universe, meaning He is extrinsic to it, and so practically speaking, from our immediate perspective, He does not exist, yet He is occasionally present in the world, in the form of His transcendental potency, which makes the impossible possible, hence in that way His presence can be accounted for. God’s transcendental potency is pointed out in this text: parasya saktir vividhaiva sruyate svabhavikl jnana-bala-kriya ca, “It is heard that His transcendental potency is manifold and inherent. There is jnana-sakti, (potency of knowledge/ consciousness), bala-sakti (energy of power), and kriyd-sakti (potency of activity)” (Svetasvatara Upanisad [6.8](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sveta-bs/6.8)).

My philosophy of paradox in this regard is the sum and substance of the Vedic scriptures. For instance, Krishna said:

*maya tatam idam sarvam jagad avyakta-murtina*  
*mat-sthani sarva-bhutani na caham tesv avasthitah*  
*na ca mat-sthani bhutani pasya me yogam aisvaram*

**I pervade all this world by My unmanifest form (Brahman). All things and all beings are in Me (as Brahman), but I am not in them, nor are all of them in Me (as Paramesvara). Behold My godly might. (Gita** [**9.4-5**](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/9.4)**)**

The theory of paradox is also substantiated in the very first line of Bhagavata Purana: janmady asya yato ‘nvayad itaratas carthesu... satyam param dhimahi, “We meditate on Him from whom the creation, the continuation and the destruction of the universe take place because of His connection (anvayat) in things and because He is altogether distinct (itaratah) from them” ([1.1.1](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sb-1/1.1)). Thus, the theory that everything happens for a reason is not 100% correct. Krishna’s esoteric teachings are summarized in the fourth verse in the catuh- sloki (four seed verses) of Bhagavata Purana :

*etavad eva jijnasyam tattva-jijndsundtmanah*  
*anvaya-vyatirekabhyam yat syat sarvatra sarvada*

**The inquiring by one who seeks to know the truth about the Soul should take place to this extent that it occurs in regard to everything, at all times, both in terms of connection and in the opposite way. (Bhagavatam** [**2.9.36**](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sb-2/9.36)**)**

The term anvaya (connection) denotes the presence of transcen­dental potency in the world. In this citation also, the term anvaya and a word expressive of its opposite are used. By the affirmative process (anvaya), the Absolute is in the world in the form of transcendental energy, or else the Absolute communicates by symbols and implied meanings, by the model of symbolism expounded in Purusa- sukta. And by the negatory process, nothing in the world is the Absolute: neti neti (not this, not that) (Brhad-aranyaka Upanisad [2.3.6](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bau/2.3.6)). The concept of negation is exactly what is meant here. For instance: nisedha-seso jayatad asesah, “Glory to the Lord, who is the remainder of the negation” (Bhagavatam [8.3.24](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/3.24)). Sridhara Svami says “in the affirmative way” means God is present in the effects insofar as He is the ultimate cause, and “in the opposite way” means He, the cause, is completely distinct from the effects: anvayah karyesu karanatvenanuvrttih. karanavasthayam ca tebhyo vyatirekah (Bhavartha-dipika 2.9.36).

Thus, Vedanta philosophy is one of paradox: A soul is one with and different from God; there is fate, yet one is the master of one’s destiny; and so forth.

Moreover, nowadays too scientists claim to invent new theories, but many scientific ideas are sourced in Vedic texts. For example, Dr. Michio Kaku (b. 1947), physicist and co-founder of String Field Theory (a branch of String Theory), adheres to the aforesaid theory of paradox. He gave this lecture:

On one side, we have my esteemed colleagues who are one hundred percent certain that the universe is pointless, meaningless, and there is no God. On the other side, we have another group that is one hundred percent certain the universe has a point, has a meaning, and there is a God. One side is right, one side is wrong, right?

My personal point of view is: they’re both wrong. (Audience applauds.) What is science? Science is based on decidable statements. If I drop my cell phone, I know, it’s decidable, that it will fall, under gravity. Science is based on statements that you can test: reproducable, decidable, falsifiable. But the question of “Does God exist?”, “Does the universe have a point?”, is undecidable. It is not part of science.

Similarly, Dr. Michio Kaku states that according to the String Theory, there is a multiverse, meaning there are parallel universes. But it is well known that this concept of multiple universes has been mentioned in Vedic scriptures, such as the Puranas.

In addition, Elon Musk and others, including many speakers on TED Talks, say that the universe is a simulation: Life is pre-programmed like a video game. Again, this notion is mainstream Vedanta, as shown in the aforementioned text: prakrteh kriyamanani gufiaih karmani sarvasah, “All actions are being done by the three modes of Nature” (Gita [3.27](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/3.27)).

Furthermore, Stephen Hawking (1942-2018) dreamed of finding the theory of everything:

However, if we discover a complete theory, it should in time be understandable by everyone, not just by a few scientists. Then we shall all, philosophers, scientists and just ordinary people, be able to take part in the discussion of the question of why it is that we and the universe exist. If we find the answer to that, it would be the ultimate triumph of human reason — for then we should know the mind of God.

Commenting on this passage, the English physicist Brian Cox said:

What he was really talking about was “Nature” there. He was not a religious man, Hawking. But that idea — Einstein used to talk like this as well, that there’s a regularity and beauty underpinning our universe that we have access to. And he made the biggest step on the right track.

Albert Einstein stated his own standpoint on religion:

New York’s Rabbi Herbert S. Goldstein asked Einstein by telegram: “Do you believe in God? Stop. Answer paid 50 words.” In his response, for which Einstein needed but twenty-five (German) words, he stated his beliefs succinctly: “I believe in Spinoza’s God, Who reveals Himself in the lawful harmony of the world, not in a God Who concerns Himself with the fate and the doings of mankind.”

Einstein also wrote:

How can cosmic religious feeling be communicated from one person to another, if it can give rise to no definite notion of a God and no theology? In my view, it is the most important function of art and science to awaken this feeling and keep it alive in those who are receptive to it.

Spinoza, a Dutch philosopher (1632-1677), expounded pantheism (Nature is a form of God), though it was first outlined in the hymn of Rg-Veda called Purusa-sukta. For example: purusa evedam sarvam, “The entire universe is God” (Rg-Veda 10.90.2). A similar example is: sarvam khalv idam brahma, “The entire universe is, of course, Brahman” (Chandogya Upanisad [3.14.1](https://vedarama.com/en/books/chan-upa/3.14.1)). This is figurative usage, by the logic that nothing apart from the Absolute really exists: dvaitasyavastunah, “of duality, which is unreal” (Bhagavata Purana [11.28.4](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sb-11/28.4)). The real meaning is: “Brahman (Paramesvara) is the cause of the universe.” The figurative usage here takes place by karya-karana-bhava (the mode of cause and effect), as in the classic example: ayur ghrtam, “Ghee is longevity (is the cause of longevity)” (Kavya-prakasa 2.12). The knowledge of poetics is necessary to correctly interpret Vedic texts.

Spinoza’s teachings too coincide with Vedanta philosophy: Max Muller noted the similarities between Vedanta and the system of Spinoza: “the Brahma, as conceived in the Upanishads and defined by Sankara, is clearly the same as Spinoza’s “Substantia”.”

The 19th-century German Sanskritist Theodore Goldstucker wrote that Spinoza’s thought was... “a western system of philosophy which occupies a foremost rank amongst the philosophies of all nations and ages, and which is so exact a representation of the ideas of the Vedanta, that we might have suspected its founder to have borrowed the fundamental principles of his system from the Hindus, did his biography not satisfy us that he was wholly unacquainted with their doctrines.”

On the one hand, Vedanta is in agreement with statements such as the following by Niels Bohr (1885-1962), the Danish physicist who won the Noble prize of physics in 1922: “Everything we call real is made of things that cannot be regarded as real.” But on the other, the world also contains manifestations of transcendental vibrations. The relative unreality of the world is simply the backdrop of the game of consciousness.

Thus, let us offer our respects to Abhinavagupta, Jagannatha and other greats and consider that true happiness mostly comes from within. Life is about increasing the mojo in order to tap into the transcendental mojo, the joy of the soul. In the relative plane of existence, the best kind of bliss is the mix of both.

All in all, the universe, including the Earth, is a form of consciousness. For example, Terence McKenna (1946-2000) and other researchers into the occult talk about the Gaian Mind, meaning the Earth, Gaia, is a living being and has an ego-like consciousness, like a person. I can attest to that too. The universe is the play of Rasa. Make it epic.
