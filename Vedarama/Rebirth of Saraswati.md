# Rebirth of Saraswati

## About Book

**Aug 18, 2002 COVER STORY**

Rebirth of Saraswati

*http://www.hindunet.org/saraswati/sarasvatirebirth01.htm*

*The Vajpayee government sponsors a Himalayan effort to bring the mythical river to life from the entrails of the earth; sceptics see a lotus blooming*

**By Kartikeya Sharma**

**Chandra, the moon-god, was in distress.** Daksha Prajapati, his father-in-law, had cursed him that he would lose his shine and perish. He had loved one of his 27 wives too much and had provoked jealousy and ire, and of course, the curse. The gods came to Chandra's aid and begged forgiveness. Placated, Daksha asked him to bathe in the waters of the Saraswati and regain his shine every fortnight.

## Origin of a legend

**A sadhu at Adi Badri, supposed to be the source of Saraswati**

The Saraswati has since dried up, or become a pale shadow of her Vedic glory, if some of the stagnant pools of Haryana are her present self. Legend has it that the angry Kavasa Ailusa, excommunicated by fellow-rishis for being a dasyaputra, had his revenge by taking the Saraswati into the desert.

The Vajpayee government, with the assistance of hydrologists, geologists, archaeologists and space scientists, is trying to redeem her from the curse and, if possible, make her waters available in ponds and tanks, so that India can regain its 'ancient shine'. The project, if it succeeds, may deprive Prayag (where Saraswati of the nether world is believed to merge with the Ganga and the Yamuna) of some its holy glory, but the BJP-led government, accused of Aryanising India's history, is not bothered.

Culture and Tourism Minister Jagmohan thinks that the attempt to resurrect the river would promote interest in Indian antiquity. "It is part of the larger cultural policy for national reconstruction," he said.

Once the scientists bring up the 10,000-year-old (if not more) waters of the Saraswati, holy sites like Pehua, Kurukshetra's Brahma Sarovar and Adi Badri in Haryana would become major tourist-pilgrimage spots.

The search for the Saraswati is 150 years old, but with the advent of remote-sensing and atomic physics, stellar findings have been made. The Rig Veda gives the river a status higher than the mighty Indus or the Ganga (she is termed the mother of rivers) and many of its hymns are believed to have been composed on its banks.

Historians and archaeologists have traced her to places like Iran and Afghanistan, where there are rivers with similar-sounding names such as Harahwaiti, thus confirming the theory that Aryans migrated to India with the Rig Veda, which they had composed on the way.

In 1844, Major F. Makenson, looking for a safe route to connect Sindh with Delhi, discovered a huge riverbed, over which he wanted to build an eight-way lane. In 1869, archaeologist Alex Rogue found Himalayan alluvial deposits in the Gulf of Cambay (now Khambat) which could not have been brought there by the non-Himalayan Sabarmati or Narmada. This led him to suspect that the Saraswati must have left them there before she vanished. C.F. Oldham of the Geological Survey of India asserted in 1893 that the dry bed skirting the Rajasthan desert was that of the Saraswati.

**The Harappan civilisation thrived on the banks of the Saraswati, says Kalyan Raman (left)**

Sir Mortimer Wheeler's excavations at Mohenjo-daro and Harappa in early 20th century changed all that. Wheeler asserted that the cities were pre-Aryan and that Rig-Vedic Aryans, led by Indra the Purandara (fort-breaker), had sacked the 'Dravidian' cities. Talk was now about a pre-Vedic Indus civilisation, which in course of time became schoolbook history, though many scholars continued to doubt the Aryan invasion theory.

The doubters got a shot in the arm when Sir Aurel Stein, who surveyed the Central Asian silk route, stumbled on late Harappan sites on the dry bed of a river in Gujarat and Rajasthan. This led to the supposition that the river had dried up before the late Harappan period, since people would have built houses on a riverbed only if they were convinced that the river would not come to life again.

How did she dry up? In 1964, Robert Raikes argued that tectonic uplifts could have led to destruction of cities and a change in the course of rivers. German geologist Herbert Wilhelmy, who surveyed the drainage system of northwest India in 1969, postulated that the Saraswati had been fed by the Sutlej and the Yamuna. It was soon argued that geological changes had made the Yamuna swirl, taking away the waters of the Saraswati (thus leading to the Allahabad triveni legend).

Indian archaeologists like Dr Jagatpati Joshi, Dr R.D. Banerjee and Dr Madhusunik were soon tripping over late Harappan sites all over western India, especially on dry riverbeds. By 1980, Prof. Yashpal had claimed to have identified the Saraswati course from Adi Badri in Haryana to the Harappan site of Kalibangan (in Rajasthan), where Dr B.B. Lal had found the world's earliest trace of an earthquake.

The Landsat images that Yashpal obtained from the United States showed paleo-channels in Haryana, which he claimed to be that of the Saraswati. Yashpal did not argue that the Saraswati was a perennial river, but his discovery acted as a catalyst for those who wanted to prove that the Saraswati did flow through India. In 1985, V. Wakankar, who had excavated the paleolithic Bhimbehtka caves in Bhopal, organised a trek of 35 scientists from Adi Badri to Somnath on the Gujarat coast. That was a virtual repeat, though in the opposite direction, of Lord Krishna's half-brother Balarama's pilgrimage through the banks of Saraswati from Somnath to Adi Badri, after refusing to take sides in the Mahabharata war. Anyway, when the Mahabharata was being written, the Saraswati had dried up. Balarama was pained to see her disappear into the desert.

On the request of Wakankar's friend Moropant Pingle, who had founded the Saraswati Shodh Pratisthan in Jodhpur in 1984, the Indian Space Research Organisation's (ISRO) satellites photographed the paleo-channel. Thus was born Project Saraswati, first as a modest Rs 47 lakh research project. It was relaunched as a multidisciplinary study by the Central Groundwater Commission in 1998 with assistance from space and nuclear scientists. The commission's engineers dug 24 wells on the dry bed identified by ISRO; 23 yielded potable water.

Around the same time, a major discovery was made in the isotope division labs of the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre. S.M. Rao, who directs the division, was examining samples of water collected from underground aquifers around Pokharan, Rajasthan, to check whether radio-active material had seeped into them after the nuclear tests. Rao's team couldn't believe their eyes. The samples were of Himalayan glacial water, 8,000 to 14,000 years old.

**Water can be pumped out from the Saraswati's ground aquifers to recharge the old tanks, says Dr Bisht**

Remote-sensing and archaeological studies have indicated that a river, originating from the present Sutlej's source, had flowed through northern Rajasthan, Bahawalpur and Sindh to the southeast of the present course of the Sutlej and the Indus. Geophysical studies in Kisangarh-Tanot-Tongewala areas of Rajasthan revealed water at five out of 17 sites on the ancient channel. The wells, drilled down to 136 metres, yielded 60 to 1,000 litres of water per minute.

Those who believed that the Saraswati had actually flowed through India had thought that she had been a weak, probably rain-fed, river. She was believed to have originated in the snowless Shivalik, unlike the mighty Indus or Ganga which are swollen in summer by the melting Himalayan snow. "The Saraswati died not because of any tectonic movement," says Dr R.S. Bisht, director of excavation in the Archaeological Survey of India. "She accumulated surface water to become large. There was a great aridity all over the world between 2000 and 1800 BC when poorly nourished rivers dried up." Is this the fact behind the legend of rishis losing Vedic wisdom in a struggle for survival during a 12-year famine, and Goddess Saraswati re-educating them?

Bisht believes that the Saraswati was actually a river of lakes (Saras-wati, or the one with lakes) even during the Rig Vedic days. The ox-bow shaped lakes, filled with water during the rains, became sacred tirthas.

Folklore has a different story. On seeing the stunning Saraswati at a yagya, Brahma, who had created her, was overcome with lust. As she fled from him (vanished), she had to stop every 100 yards to catch breath (where she reappeared). That is why she has become a here-and-there river.

The Rig Vedic verses praising the Saraswati are quoted by many to argue that she had been a river as mighty as the Indus. V.M.K. Puri, former director of the Geological Survey of India, argues that the river was fed by the Bander Poonch glaciers in the Naitwar region of Garhwal Himalayas. "The Saraswati occupied the present basin of Tons river up to Poanta Sahib (Haryana) and took a westerly swing to Punjab after receiving nourishment from the Yamuna at the eastern flank," says Puri.

Puri and others say that the presence of 14,000-year-old Himalayan riverine sediments on the Ghaggar plains (part of the dry Saraswati bed) proves her Himalayan origin. The river had carved a new channel for herself because of the quake-caused changes in the lay of the land around 2450 BC. "Around Adi Badri, rocks, terraces and boulders of the Himalayan upper reaches have been recovered in large numbers which shouldn't have been there as Adi Badri is located in the Shivaliks," points out Dr Baldev Sahay, former deputy director of ISRO's Space Application Centre, Ahmedabad, who is in a committee of four appointed by Jagmohan to look into the details of the scientific tests. "It supports Puri's claims that the Saraswati was a perennial river and connected to Bander Poonch glacier in the Himalayas," he says.

According to Dr Sahay, the river got desiccated because of tectonic movement between 2500 and 1900 BC. "She was connected with Himalayan glaciers," he says. "The Harappan civilisation thrived around her." Agrees Kalyan Raman, Sahay's colleague in the committee: "There is overwhelming evidence to support the theory that Harappan civilisation existed on the banks of the Saraswati. Nearly 2,000 of the 2,600 Harappan sites which have been discovered, including the major ones like Banawali, Kalibangan, Ropar, Rakigarhi and Dholavira, are situated on the old paleo-channel of the Saraswati." Which has led many historians to rechristen the Indus Valley civilisation as Indus-Saraswati civilisation.

Not everyone is impressed. The Saraswati's yet to be discovered waters are already on fire with a counter-school of historians seeing Hindutva ghosts emerging from the long-buried river. Enthusiasts like 'American guru' **David Frawley** have been arguing that the Harappans were Aryans, and they had composed the Rig Veda on the banks of the Indus and the Saraswati, both of which are prominently mentioned in the hymns.

Frawley's main point is simple. How come the Vedic Aryans, with a highly refined literary culture, did not build any cities? And how come the Harappans, who had precise measurement for even brick-making, left no books behind? Unless both were the same people? In other words, the builders of Mohenjo-daro and Harappa were the same as the writers of the Rig Veda.

The fear of the 'far-fetched' Frawleys has prompted the counter-school to debunk the recent claims. Prof. K.M. Srimali of Delhi University points out that though a paleo-channel has been identified through satellite images, its age has not been proved. "On top of it, the attempt to rename the Harappan civilisation as Saraswati civilisation smacks of a larger political agenda of the BJP government. They want to make it a Hindu civilisation."

Sceptics like Srimali believe that the sudden interest in the Saraswati is part of an attempt to establish that the Harappan civilisation was Hindu, Brahminical and Aryan. "The common man does not know the politics behind it," says Prof. D.N. Jha of Delhi University. "They are manufacturing a new cultural geography."

Jagmohan is not discouraged by the criticism. In his view, the project offers immense economic as well as cultural potential. Plans have been chalked out to build check-dams for collecting monsoon water in Haryana and link Saraswati-associated holy sites such as Thaneswar, Vasishta Ashram, Pehua, Kapal Mochan and Brahma Sarovar near Kurukshetra.

Even while having his doubts about the might of the ancient Saraswati's currents, Dr Bisht is all for the project. "Even if the lakes have been filled up because of sedimentation, water can still be pumped out from the Saraswati's ground aquifers. This water could be used to recharge the old tanks, enabling us to revive the river," he says.

Channels below the Saraswati bed in Jaisalmer have been found to be fed by surface run-off. "New water management and harvesting policies will have to be introduced," says Kalyan Raman. "This would also include linking up the Saraswati's water from Haryana to the Indira Gandhi Canal." The canal, which irrigates large parts of Rajasthan, could be extended from Mohangarh in Jaisalmer to Rajra Road in Barmer and further into Gujarat.

Says Raman: "The Indira Gandhi Canal would be extended to Sabarmati. The Sharda's water would be diverted to the canal. The Ganga in turn would be compensated by Brahmaputra's water. The plan is to link western river systems with the eastern river systems."

In the last stage, Adi Badri, the identified source of the Saraswati on the snowless Shivaliks, will be connected to a Himalayan glacier to make it perennial again. "It will entail a detailed study of the Himalayan glaciers," admits Raman. "We have suggested opening of a glaciology department in Himachal Pradesh University for this."

Yet another controversy could crop up if, as Raman has suggested, 30 per cent of excess Sutlej water flowing into Pakistan is used for reviving the Saraswati. As it is, the 'Hindutva' government's Sindhu Darshan festival in Ladakh and the veiled threat to annul the Indus Waters Treaty are viewed with increasing suspicion by the modern-day keepers of Mohenjo-daro and Harappa.

## Everlasting appeal

*Saraswati's imprint on the Indian psyche is so deep*

**By Dr Prema Nandakumar**

**What is Saraswati? A river? A Goddess? A mere concept?** Who can unravel why Saraswati has held all Indians in its thrall down the ages? All that we know is that Saraswati has been an undeniable presence in India since the dawn of the Vedas. Once upon a time, she was obviously a free-flowing stream, a physical presence that our ancestors saw and worshipped. They called her Ambitame, the best of mothers.

The Rig Veda has much to say about the Saraswati river, referring to her by other names such as Vak (The Word) and Bharati. According to Sri Aurobindo, "Saraswati means, "she of the stream, the flowing movement, and is therefore a natural home both for a river and for the goddess of inspiration." The river must have had such a long closeness with the people that though it slowly vanished from the earth, the ambience has continued to swirl in the racial memory as myths and legends.

If the river became invisible, her symbol as an inspiration grew wings. Rishi Brihaspati insinuates in the Rig Veda that Vak (The Word) is necessary for civilised intercourse. Since human speech was vital for the growth of civilisation and hence on a par with the creation of the universe itself, Saraswati grew in importance and was seen as an equal of the creator Brahma by the time of the epics. And when in the course of time the waters of the river ceased to flow, the idea of Saraswati as "the stream of knowledge" had taken root in the Indian psyche.

The Saraswati deity as we know her today, took form and was imaged in the age of the Puranas and almost all Indian religions remain emotionally attached to the deity. The Hindus see her as knowledge and power and give her an important place. Tantra texts like Saundarya Lahari and Sri Lalitha Sahasranama give witness to the manner in which the Saraswati idea has percolated to the common man as a religious symbol.

**Creative confluence: At Adi Badri, Saraswati stream meets river Som**

Since music has been an inalienable part of Vedic religions (from the Sama Veda onwards), Saraswati (Sarada, Veenapani) has been an inspiration for composers and singers. Yet another area which has disseminated Saraswati worship has been our temple culture even though she is seen mostly as a panel decoration; one magnficient example being the sculpture of Jnana Saraswati in the Gangai Konda Cholapauram temple.

Very rarely was a temple built to consecrate her image in the main sanctum. The Koothanur temple of Saraswati in Tamil Nadu is an exception: we see her seated on a lotus, holding a garland of beads, a kamandala and a book in her three hands while the fourth is in chinmudra. Adi Sankara built temples for Saraswati in Sringeri and Kashmir. Stone, bronze, wood, painting, etchingṢ the Indian artist has never failed to bring out the intensity, gentleness and beauty that reside in the concept.

Saraswati appears as the same inspirational figure whether she belongs to a Jain or Buddhist temple. These religions too have held on to the Saraswati ideal and have not forgotten the original stream that had raised their ancestors. The Jain Agamas are called Jinavani and Saraswati, and are likened to the flow of the stream which cleanses man's body, even as the Agamas cleanse man's mind. The sculptures of Mahasaraswati as a group of 16 Vidya Devis are familiar to worshippers and are found in places like Mount Abu, Sithamur and Arani.

Saraswati is also seen with various mounts like the peacock, the swan, the boar and the cow and the Jain Agamas give noble names like Vajra Srunkhala and Jwala Malini to Saraswati indicating the particular movement through which she helps man.

Saraswati has a high place in the Buddhist pantheon as well. The Vajrayana School has hailed her as Prajna Paramita (Mother of Perfect Wisdom). The ancient Tamil epic Manimekalai describes the temple to Chinta Devi, the Budhist term for Saraswati.

So wide and deep has been Saraswati's imprint on the Indian psyche that even in our own times Ustad Allauddin Khan used to go up the hill-top temple of Sarada in Maihar (UP) and do his riaz; he said that Mother Sarada fed him with music like a mother cat!

Kumaraguruparar of Tamil Nadu is said to have acquired mastery of the Hindi language by propitiating Saraswati by the Tamil hymn "Sakalakalavalli Malai" while sojourning in Kashi. It is said that he was then able to converse with the Badshah in Delhi who gave him land in Kedar Ghat where he built the Kumaraswamy Math that has been functioning for the last four centuries.

In the last century, Telugu poet Boddupalli Purushottam's poem on Saraswati, Vaak Anu-seelanamu, poses the question, "Who is Saraswati", and answers: "She is the one who lives as a presence in the heart of the universal man as a memory and helps the individual speak out with knowledge". Bengali literature is enriched by Biharilal Chakravarti's 19th century classics, Saradamangal and Sadher Asan. While the river of Vedic and itihasic times that had become the Goddess of Knowledge and Power in the Puranas, was portrayed as a catalytic agent for achieving India's freedom by Suryakantha Tripathi \`Nirala', who wrote in a Saraswati vandana: "Player of the Lute! Fill up the new India with the nectarian mantra of dear freedom!"

## Interview/Jagmohan, minister for culture and tourism

People should know their glorious past

**You have constituted a committee of scholars to trace the course of the Saraswati river. Why the sudden interest?**

The idea is to explore and get more evidence so that we can find out whether Saraswati existed or not. The scholars in the committee have worked on the subject before and their advice will be sought once our work is completed. They will evaluate the historical, hydrological, geological and archaeological evidence regarding Saraswati. People believe that Saraswati's water is below the earth.

**Is there a move to 'resurrect' Saraswati?**

First we should be able to locate the water down below and then we have to bring it above. If we are able to get proof for our contention, we will tell people that the great river Saraswati, which was part of Indian heritage, went underground following natural disasters.

**Will the river flow again?**

We can do it through re-canalisation. We will pump out water from river beds once we ascertain that the river flowed through the area. We will make water tanks so that people can have access to the waters of Saraswati. One such place would be Adi Badri in Haryana. Work on other areas have also been commissioned.

**The Archaeological Survey of India seems to be working full time to survey the channels of Saraswati and its tributaries.**

Apart from the survey, we are also excavating Harappan sites that exist all along the Saraswati. It is believed that more than 1,500 late Harappan settlements existed on its banks. We are conducting research with an open mind.

**Is there a political agenda behind all this?**

Our idea is to combine culture and tourism. Wrong minds always find wrong reasons. We don't have any preconceived notions. There is no attempt to glorify ancient India. We only want to find out the truth. We will not force any conclusions and will put the facts before the people.

**Is the project aimed at promoting cultural and religious tourism in the domestic circuit?**

There is no single objective. Even now, thousands of people throng Adi Badri. We want to provide cleaner environment and better facilities. Culture and tourism should not be seen in isolation. We are putting them together so that the cultural policy becomes a policy for national reconstruction. People should become aware of their glorious past and should not entertain an inferiority complex.

**Will the work on Saraswati be extended to Rajasthan?**

It is not confined to Haryana. It is taking place at Kalibangan in Rajasthan and Dholiveera in Gujarat. It is believed Saraswati exhausted itself in the Rann of Kutch.

**Are you satisfied with the progress?**

I took charge of the ministry six months back. Since then we have made a lot of progress.

**Kartikeya Sharma**

## The river of imagination

**By Irfan Habib**

**In the current effervescence over new-found truths about our past,** coming in a stream from resident and non-resident oracles, the Saraswati river has come to occupy a pre-eminent perch.....

V.S. Wakankar, the oracle on archaeology, said that the upper Saraswati region forms the nucleus of human evolution. US scientist, Navaratna S. Rajaram tells us that the Saraswati river and Saraswati Civilisation are inseparable, and the latter name is the correct one to give to what has so far been known (incorrectly) as the Harappan or the Indus Civilisation...if Indus Civilisation is to be rechristened Saraswati Civilisation, name-capture, not argument, is what matters.....

In the Rig Veda, the Saraswati appears as a goddess of ritual and speech; as a river goddess; as a river. From these references a one-sided selection is presented to buttress the notion of the 'Vedic Saraswati' as a 'mighty' stream.....

The inferences drawn from the Rig Veda stand in sharp contradiction. The Sarsuti running past Thanesar is too petty a stream to fit the picture of a great river that the Rig Veda verses suggest.....

We happen to have another Saraswati between the Sutlej and the Yamuna, which does not seem to have received attention. This river rises near Kalka, the railway station of Shimla, and runs northwestwards with the Himalayan ranges on one side and the Siwaliks on the other. It is called Sirsa, which is a recognised corruption of Saraswati. If this was the Vedic Saraswati, then many of our troubles cease... We are no longer called upon to inflate a petty plains river into a veritable second Indus.....

Neither visual observation of a line of depressions or a band of different kind of alluvium brought out by Landsat imagery gives us the time when the river courses so discerned were active.....

Both Saraswati and Sindhu mean river, and, therefore, it is conceivable that in Rig Vedic times, the Indus could be designated as both Sindhu and Saraswati. Saraswati in most of the references to it in the Rig Veda is not a particular river, but the river goddess.....

The strident claims about the once 'mighty' Saraswati, has led to making a part of India (Madhyadesa) the home of the Aryans and to the taking away of Indus (Saraswati) culture from non-Aryans.....

There is no evidence in geology or geography that since the Pleistocene epoch there has been a river on the scale of the Indus running from the Himalayas through Haryana and the desert to the Rann of Kutch; or that the Yamuna and Sutlej have ever during Holocene flowed into the Thanesar stream, now identified as Saraswati. All claims built upon the greatness of River Saraswati are nothing but castles in the air, however much froth may be blown over them.

**(Excerpts from a paper presented at the Indian History Congress. The author is a noted historian)**
