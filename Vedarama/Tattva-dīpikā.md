# Tattva-dīpikā

## Cover

**Tattva-dépikä**  
**Light on the Truth**

Edited and translated from the original in Sanskrit by Demian Martins (PhD)

govinda-deva-dayitam paramartha-purnam  
vidvatsu ha jayapure jaya-bhusitam vai  
nyaya-sruti-smrti-vivada-samordhva-hinam  
vedanta-riktha-baladeva-yatim namami

*“I offer my humble obeisances unto the great sage Srila Baladeva Vidyabhusana. Being very dear to Lord Govindaji and surcharged with transcendental knowledge, he became decorated with victory in the assembly of learned scholars in Jaipur. Nobody was equal or superior to him in debates on the sruti, smrti or nyaya, for he possessed the treasure of fully knowing the conclusion of all Vedic scriptures.”*

## Preface

Although Vaisnavas do not stress logic over devotion, under the pressure of their social environment and the need to present consistent arguments in light of their theological views, many of them have given substantial contributions in the field of philosophy. From their very founder-acaryas up to the present day, the Śrī-sampradaya and the Madhva-sampradaya are distinguished for having some of the greatest scholars in history. The entire asset of their literary legacy amounts to hundreds of books concerning various branches of knowledge. Among the Śrī Vaisnavas, Śrī Vedanta Desika (1268-1369 AD), the greatest exponent of Visistadvaita after Śrī Ramanujacarya, authored more than a hundred works, including several important books on philosophy, such as the Nyaya-parisuddhi and the Nyaya-siddhanjana, in which he utilizes the dialectical approach and terminology of Nyaya to syncretize the conclusions of Vedanta, and the Sesvara-mimamsa, a Vaisnava interpretation of the Mimamsa-sutras of Jaimini. Among the Madhvas, Vyasa Tirtha (ca. 1450-1550 AD) wrote the Nyayamrta, which presents dualist thought with sophisticated logic. At the other extreme, the Advaitavadis’ philosophical exploits surpassed that of the Vaisnavas in scope. Not only did they write original treatises and commentaries on the Brahma-sutras as most Vaisnava scholars did, but they also commented upon all major traditional philosophical works such as the Nyaya-sutras, Vaisesika-sutras, Yoga-sutras, Samkhya-karika, etc. In course of time, this earned them ample recognition in scholarly circles all over India and consequentially furthered their cause to a great extent. In one sense, this may be seen as an astute preaching stratagem. The widespread and lasting outcome of such scholarly enterprises are felt even today, for while most Indian universities offer Advaita Vedanta as a major discipline, relatively few offer Vaisnava Vedanta as an option.

Śrī Caitanya Mahaprabhu appeared in a very prolific phase in the history of Indian philosophy. Navadvipa, in West Bengal, was then known as a major centre of scholarship, where youths from different parts of the country thronged for higher studies. The influence of Buddhism and Jainism had already waned centuries before, and the systems of Samkhya and Purva-mimamsa had become obsolete. Nyaya and Vedanta, however, were flourishing, and renowned scholars like Paksadhara Misra, Vasudeva Sarvabhauma, Vyasa Tirtha, Raghunatha Siromani, Vallabhacarya, Madhusudana Sarasvati, Haridasa Nyayalankara, Janakinatha Tarkacudamani, Mathuranatha Tarkavagisa, Ramabhadra Sarvabhauma, Bhavananda Siddhanta Vagisa, Harirama Tarkavagisa, Visvanatha Nyayapancanana, Jagadisa Tarkalankara and Jayarama Nyayapancanana all lived within a hundred years of Lord Caitanya’s era and compiled important philosophical treatises and commentaries, particularly on Nyaya. According to one traditional account, Mahaprabhu Himself also authored a commentary on Nyaya. It is said that when a great logician resident of Nadia saw that commentary and realized that it was far superior to any of his own writings, he became very morose, which led Mahaprabhu to throw the text into the Ganges in order to appease his distress.

Despite many hundreds of years of philosophical legacy behind them, Lord Caitanya’s main followers did not take much interest in directly discussing orthodox philosophy. Among the Six Gosvamis of Vrndavana, Śrī Jiva Gosvami (ca. 1513-1608 AD) was the only one who dealt with the views of different philosophical schools. Before going to Vrndavana, he spent over a decade studying in Varanasi, where he acquired a sound background in Samkhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vaisesika, Mimamsa and Vedanta. He reveals his command of these systems in his Sarva-samvadini, written as an auto-commentary on the Bhagavata- sandarbhas.

It was only in the eighteenth century that the Gaudiya sampradaya was blessed by a philosopher who could second Jiva Gosvami, and a third one is yet to be seen. The appearance of Śrī Baladeva Vidyabhusana (ca. 1700-1793 AD) was indeed timely, when there was a necessity to establish the legitimacy of the Gaudiya lineage and philosophy when they were being disputed. Vidyabhusana accomplished this by writing commentaries on standards scriptures such as the Brahma-sutras, Dasopanisad and Bhagavad-gita. Besides these, he also authored original treatises like the Tattva-dipika. Although the concept of a short exposition dealing with different philosophical systems had already been considerably explored previously, this work of Vidyabhusana is perhaps the second Vaisnava contribution in the genre. Until then, the only renowned Vaisnava work of the sort was the Paramata-bhanga of Vedanta Desika, a compilation of fifty-four stanzas in Manipravala (Sanskritized Tamil), in which the author briefly describes and refutes fifteen non-Vaisnava philosophies and finally establishes the superiority of Visistadvaita. In a similar fashion, at the end of the Tattva-dipika, Vidyabhusana establishes the superiority of Acintya-bhedabheda. Yet it is remarkable that, despite their fierce criticism of so many philosophies, neither of them criticized other Vaisnavas, as it has become common nowadays. This is skilfully explained by Vedanta Desika in the Paramata-bhanga (10):

*veriyar tulavudai vittagan tanmaiyin meyyarivar*  
*kuriyar nediyavar enru oru kurram pirarkkurayar*  
*ariyar tirattil arul purintu arana nanneriyal*  
*ciriyar valigal alippatum tingu kalippatarke*

**“Those who are completely devoted do not find fault with Vaisnavas who are learned and absorbed in the transcendental qualities of the Supreme Lord Krsna, Who wears fresh Tulasi garlands. On the other hand, just to protect innocent people from proponents of misleading philosophies and deliver the latter from their sinful deeds, such merciful Vaisnavas conversant with the Vedas smash their fallacious arguments.”**

Although it is unlikely that Vidyabhusana was acquainted with Desika’s work, since it was confined to a local vernacular, it is quite evident that he knew Madhava’s (14th century AD) Sarva-darsana- sangraha, a classical philosophical compendium comprising summary studies of prominent theistic and non-theistic systems, from which he seems to have borrowed not only the conceptual structure of the text but also several definitions, classifications and arguments. In this short but intense composition, Vidyabhusana displays the heights of his outstanding scholarship in very refined language and reasoning. Most of the schools of thought presented here have to their credit many traditional texts with commentaries and sub-commentaries, and for centuries they vied with each other on the validity of their concepts and conclusions. Vidyabhusana masterly refutes each of them by pointing out flaws and internal contradictions as well as by using arguments given by the opponent schools of a particular system. Therefore the readers should keep in mind that the author is not always retorting from the Vaisnava standpoint, which he keeps mostly for his grand finale. In the sections on Buddhism and Jainism, for example, he more or less follows the argumentation of Sankaracarya, who was notable for vehemently defeating Buddhism and re-establishing Vedic religion in India. It should also be mentioned that in these two sections, Vidyabhusana does not refer to primary sources. This means that he is describing these systems in the same way that they are presented by the Vedantis, which may not portray their original texts verbatim. At the end of his introduction, the author makes a sort of disclaimer - perhaps to make it clear that he never intended the atheistic systems to sound meaningful at all.

One may wonder what motivated Vidyabhusana to write this book, which, for the most part, does not directly deal with topics concerning Lord Krsna, the only thing that Vaisnavas actually hanker to hear and speak about. Although the manuscript has no dedication, since it belonged to Sawai Jai Singh II, the King of Jaipur, it is possible that Vidyabhusana may have written it for the King’s personal studies. It is very clear from available historical documentations that Sawai Jai Singh had a genuine interest in philosophy, particularly Vaisnava philosophy, and not only commissioned several treatises but also authored some of his own. Moreover, Vidyabhusana did not send copies of this text to different places as he did in the case of many of his other books, which seems to suggest that he had a particular purpose in mind. There seems to be some indications that this text may have been composed before the Govinda-bhasya, where the author again deals with many of the topics discussed here, but in a more elaborate manner. If this was the case, since the Govinda-bhasya overshadowed the Tattva-dipika, it would be natural for the author to give preference to the propagation of the former. Baladeva Vidyabhusana may also have felt the urge to dissuade innocent souls from the allurements of philosophical systems that do not lead to the ultimate goal of life, as expressed by Vedanta Desika. Furthermore, in his concluding words, he wishes that his work may bring joy to the learned. He makes a similar statement at the end of his Siddhanta-darpana, wherein Nanda Misra commented that the author intended that his book would bring relief to the devotees who were hurt by the words of fools. Vaisnavas naturally feel pain when they hear a philosophy that opposes the principles of pure bhakti (unalloyed love for Krsna). It is expected that such devotees will feel delight upon hearing the refutation of each non- Vaisnava philosophy here, just as the inhabitants of Vrndavana felt joy when Krsna killed each demon that attacked Him.

## The Manuscript and Its Authorship

Despite extensive search through many states of India, only one manuscript could be located for consultation to prepare this edition. It is part of the Khasmohor Collection, accession number 5693, preserved at the Maharaja Man Singh II Museum, Jaipur, and belonged to King Sawai Jai Singh II, who ruled from 1699 to 1743 AD. Although it is undated, there are reasons to infer that it was most probably written in the last decade of the King’s life, if not in his very last years. The name of the scribe is not mentioned, but it was unmistakably penned by Dayanidhi, who was Vidyabhusana’s main scribe for many years. We know his name from a manuscript of the Govinda-bhasya preserved in Gopiballabhpur, the Syamanandi-pitha in West Bengal, dated Samvat 1815 (1758 AD), in which he identifies himself as a brahmana, the son of the minister of Kurmacala. He not only copied most of Vidyabhusana’s works but also wrote several of his personal letters, and his copies can be found all over India. Given the fact that he was noticeably neither much skilled in Sanskrit nor a very proficient scribe, we may assume that he was a disciple of Vidyabhusana voluntarily engaged in his service. At the end of the text, we find the statement that this was a composition of Vidyabhusana, “who is totally dependent upon the lotus feet of Lord Śrī Caitanya Mahaprabhu,” and also a precatory verse that appears in his Syamananda-sataka-tika and Sahitya-kaumudi, in which he alludes to four generations of acaryas preceding him in the Syamanandi-parivara. There is also another verse that appears at the end of his Siddhanta-ratna, in which he salutes his siksa-guru, Pitambaradasa. Moreover, the style and scholarship peculiar to the other works of Vidyabhusana are distinctly seen throughout the Tattva- dipika. On the basis of all this evidence, there is no scope to doubt the authenticity of the manuscript and its authorship by Baladeva Vidyabhusana.

## Introduction -1

India has long been the land of theology and philosophy or darsana. The word darsana literally means vision, but in the present context it has a special meaning, the vision of Reality. The human mind is inquisitive from a person's very birth. As one grows, one becomes inquisitive about things around him, about life and its purpose, the causes of suffering and its solution. The innate desire of every living being is to be happy and have no suffering, sukham me syat, duhkham ḍṁ bhut. Although we want happiness and work for it, we all face some suffering in our life. The human mind has pondered over this paradox since the dawn of creation. Learned Vidura inquired about this paradox to the great sage Maitreya five thousand years ago. To know the root cause of suffering and find a permanent solution for it is one the chief aims of most of the Indian darsana schools. The Indian philosophical systems developed out of a desire to find answers to these questions. When many schools of darsana developed, it became a custom for each to try to prove its solution as the best. This was done to help its adherents have staunch faith in its principles, and gave them the ability to face their opponents. Thus a learned student of any school would be expected to know the views of other schools, and to be capable of refuting those that differed from his own.

In the beginning there was no single textbook for learning the views of other schools on prominent issues. One would learn them either by studying them directly, or get some knowledge by learning through refutations found in his own system. Every prominent school had its sutra text, commentaries on them and other independent works. Usually the commentaries and independent works would give the opponent’s view (purva-paksa) and its refutation. Good examples of this are found in the bhasyas and commentaries to Vedanta-sutra, Nyaya-sutra, Yoga-sutra and so on.

To know an opponent’s view thoroughly one needed to study that system. This could be very time consuming. In due course, therefore, a need was felt to collect the opinions of various schools in one book. Jain scholars took the lead in this field. The earliest known such work is Naya-cakra of Acarya Mallavadi (5th century AD), which gives a summary of twenty-one different schools of thought. Jains believe that Reality has many facets and thus there cannot be a single absolute vision. All schools have some truth in them. Thus Mallavadi explains the various contemporary schools without refuting any of them, his idea being that Jainism includes all of them. Not all the twenty-one schools he mentions were in fact prominent and only a few of them became widespread.

With the passage of time the concept of six systems, sad-darsana, became popular. Haribhadra Suri (750 AD), a prominent Jain scholar of his time, composed Sad-darsana-samuccaya. This is also one of the earliest known works of doxography, wherein the author collected philosophical principles scattered throughout six major darsana schools. These were not, however, the same as the six classical systems known today, viz., Nyaya, Vaisesika, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva-mimamsa and Uttara-mimamsa. The six darsanas described in his book are Bauddha, Nyaya, Samkhya, Jain, Vaisesika and Jaiminiya (i.e., Purva-mimamsa), in this order. According to those who follow the Vedas, schools of philosophy can be divided into two groups: those of the believers (astika) and the non-believers (nastika) in the Veda. Sad-darsana-samuccaya, being the work of a Jain, a nastika school, contains the views of both groups. This book, however, did not become very popular because of its brevity. It contains only a few verses devoted to each system and avoids even the slightest discussion on any debatable issue. Much later, in the 14th century, another Jain, Gunaratna Suri, wrote an elaborate commentary on it.

Haribhadra Suri also wrote another work called Sastra-varta- samuccaya. In this work he shows the defects in a particular darsana and suggests how to rectify it so that it can come into harmony with Jain darsana.

On the Hindu side, the first work of this type is called Sarva-darsana-siddhanta-sangraha or Sarva-siddhanta-sangraha in short, which is attributed to famous teacher of Advaita Vedanta, Śrī Sankaracarya (8th century AD). However, there is a controversy as to whether it was really penned by the great acarya. It contains descriptions of fourteen schools: Lokayata, Arhata (Jain), the four Buddhist philosophical systems of Madhyamika, Yogacara, Sautrantika, and Vaibhasika, Vaisesika, Nyaya; the two Purva-mimamsas of Prabhakara and Kumarila Bhatta, Samkhya, Patanjala Yoga, Vedavyasa, and Advaita Vedanta.

Madhyamika, Yogacara, Sautrantika and Vaibhasika are the four divisions of Buddhism. Prabhakara and Kumarila Bhatta are followers of Jaimini’s Mimamsa. The school of Vedavyasa included in the list refers to the teachings of Mahabharata and Puranas. This is distinguished from the school of Vedanta-sutra, which is also attributed to him.

There have been some other lesser known works of doxography such as Sarva-darsana-kaumudi by Madhava Sarasvati (14th century AD). He describes fifteen schools beginning with that of Carvaka and ending with the Advaita Vedanta of Sankaracarya. Another work by the name Sad-darsana-samuccaya, different from that of Haribhadra Suri, was written by Rajasekhara in the 14th century AD. Sarva-mata-sangraha of an unknown author from Kerala was published in the beginning of the last century. All these works are out of print and hardly known or studied by the scholars.

Madhusudana Sarasvati, the famous 16th century author of Advaita-siddhi, also wrote a work called Prasthana-bheda. This book is written to show that the purpose of all Vedic scriptures, directly or indirectly, is only in knowing Bhagavan. It does not discuss the non- Vedic schools. It considers eighteen branches of vidyas (knowledge): the four Vedas, namely Rg, Yajur, Sama and Atharva, four Upavedas, namely, Ayurveda, Dhanurveda, Gandharva-veda, and Sthapatya-veda, six limbs of the Veda (vedanga), namely Siksa, Vyakarana, Nirukta, Chandah, Jyotisa, and Kalpa, along with Purana, Nyaya, Mimamsa, and Dharma-sastra. The Upapuranas are included in the Puranas, Vaisesika in Nyaya, and Vedanta within Mimamsa. Mahabharata, Rāmāyaṇa, Samkhya, Patanjala, Pasupata and Vaisnava scriptures are all subsumed under the Dharma-sastra. Madhusudana also lists six non-Vedic schools but does not discuss them because they do not lead to his stated ultimate purpose of life.

Overall, the most popular work in this field is the 14th century Sarva-darsana-sangraha of Madhavacarya. It contains descriptions of the basic principles of all the contemporary popular and some not so popular schools. It does not only just give the description of the views of a school but also discusses them in contrast to the views of its competitors. In all, it covers the thoughts of sixteen schools. These are Carvaka (Lokayata), Buddhist (in the above four divisions), Jain, Ramanuja, Purna-prajna (Madhvacarya), Nakulisa Pasupata, Mahesvara, Pratyabhijna, Rasesvara, Vaisesika, Naiyayika, Jaimini (Purva-mimamsa), Panini, Samkhya, Patanjala (Yoga), and Sankara (Advaita Vedanta). As the author was a protagonist of Sankaracarya’s Advaita Vedanta, he discussed this philosophy as the last school, standing faultless above the others’ discredited conclusions. He follows a system of gradual improvement. At the beginning of each chapter he finds fault with the chief views of the system just discussed in the previous one. He begins with a description of the Carvaka school in the first chapter and then points out its flawed epistemology before going on to discuss the next school, Buddhism. In this way he builds up until he culminates in Advaita Vedanta as the topmost school.

As is evident from the above description, most of these works were written either by Jain scholars or by those of the Advaita Vedanta school. Siddhi-trayam (10th century AD) of Yamunacarya is an early work by a Vaisnava scholar to present the views of his Visista Advaita school on three essential topics: the self (atma), God (isvara), and consciousness (samvit). The author discusses these topics very elaborately and refutes the views of all the popular contemporary schools that do not match with his.

## Śrī Baladeva Vidyabhusana

The Tattva-dipika of Śrī Baladeva Vidyabhusana is another book in the doxographical genre written by a Vaisnava. Śrī Baladeva Vidyabhusana was a distinguished acarya and scholar in the Gaudiya school of Vaisnavism. He was a prolific writer and wrote many commentaries on original scriptures like Bhagavad-gita, Upanisads, Srimad-Bhagavata Purana, as well as on the works of previous Gaudiya acaryas such as the Sandarbhas of Śrī Jiva Gosvami and Laghu-bhagavatamrta of Śrī Rupa Gosvami. He also wrote independent works like Siddhanta-ratna, Prameya-ratnavali, Kavya-kaustubha and Siddhanta-darpana. However, he is specifically remembered and honoured for his Govinda-bhasya on Vedanta-sutra.

The immediate followers of Śrī Caitanya Mahaprabhu did not write any commentary on Vedanta-sutra but primarily wrote commentaries and independent works based upon Srimad-Bhagavata Purana, which they accepted as the explanation of the sutras written by the author of the sutras himself. Because of this lacuna, a controversy arose in Jaipur, Rajasthan, and the Gaudiya school was challenged in the king’s court to produce its bhasya on Vedanta-sutra to prove its authenticity. Were it not able to do so, the sect would not be recognized as a legitimate Vedantic sampradaya and would be barred from serving the deities of Śrī Śrī Radha Govindadeva housed in the royal palace. Since Govindadeva was the deity of Rupa Gosvami, one of the leading authorities and inspirations of the school, this was a major crisis for the Gaudiyas. Śrī Baladeva took up the challenge and produced Govinda-bhasya in a very short span of time. According to him, the bhasya was produced on the order and by the grace of the Deity Himself. That is why he named it Govinda-bhasya.

Tattva-dipika is another unique work by this esteemed scholar. Most likely taking his cue from Madhavacarya's Sarva-darsana-sangraha, the author attempts to establish the acintya-bhedabheda-vada of Śrī Caitanya as the best school of darsana, taking all other systems as its purva-paksa.

While commenting on Tattva-sandarbha (Anuccheda 1) of Śrī Jiva Gosvami, Śrī Baladeva Vidyabhusana writes, “Out of lethargy people will not read an elaborate commentary, therefore I am only writing briefly on this esoteric composition.”

He has truly followed this same principle of brevity while writing Tattva-dipika, even more so than in his Tattva-sandarbha commentary. Tattva-dipika is extremely terse, and that is probably the reason that it never became popular even among Gaudiya followers. Gaudiya Vaisnavas are more inclined to engage in nama-kirtana, nama-japa, lila-smarana and vigraha-seva than the study of philosophical texts, especially those of outside schools. Thus it is no surprise that a work like Tattva-dipika remained unknown. Its lack of expansiveness might also have prevented it from achieving widespread circulation.

## Summary of Tattva-dipika

The author begins by dividing all the schools of darsanas into the two basic groups of non-believers (nastikas) and believers (astikas). Those who do not accept the authority of the Vedas and God are the non-believers, and those who accept are the believers. Interestingly, Śrī Baladeva lumps even the believers who disagree with Vedavyasa with the non-believers. The reason for this is that if they do not accept the conclusion of the Vedas and propagate the theories spun in their own minds, their acceptance of Veda is nothing more than lip-service. It cannot, therefore, bring the ultimate welfare described in the Vedanta. Thus the author refutes their conclusions for the benefit of a serious spiritualist. Unless one is free of all doubts and very clear about the process, sadhana, one will not be inclined to follow it wholeheartedly. The specialty of Tattva-dipika is that it gives the principle of a school and its refutation simultaneously, whereas in the case of Sarva-darsana-sangraha, the refutation only comes later when the next school is described.

Usually three schools are given as those of the non-believers, viz., Carvaka, Buddhism and Jainism. Generally such compendia begin with Carvaka, since this is the most materialistic philosophy. Śrī Baladeva, however, is an exception and puts Carvaka third in the list, perhaps because none of its works are available and there were no real adherents of it during his time. He thus only mentions it later in the context of describing the soul.

Baladeva begins his work with Buddhism. He describes its five- skandha theory and the principle of four types of atoms that constitute thṛ external world. He refutes these ideas by arguing that atoms themselves cannot combine to create the world. They need a sentient agent. However, Buddhism does not accept God as the agent behind creation. If it is argued that the Buddha is the creator, then that is also not possible because he has no purpose in creating. Moreover, in Buddhism the soul is not an eternal entity but only a transient, momentary consciousness. The soul of the Buddha is no exception to this principle. Thus it is not possible for him to create. The author also invokes the standard argument of cause-effect relation used against Buddhism. He refers to the famous four-fold reflections or bhavanas of Buddhism, namely svalaksana-bhavana, ksanika-bhavana, duhkha- bhavana and sunya-bhavana. To refute these, he refers to Svetasvatara Upanisad. This seems a bit odd since the Buddhist scholars do not accept the authority of the Vedas. So refuting them on the authority of the Veda cannot be expected to make a convincing argument to the Buddhists. However, as said in the beginning of the book, the primary intention of the author is not to refute the Buddhists or other opponents but to instil faith in the adherents of Vedic knowledge so that they do not become fascinated by the non-Vedic schools. The author proves that without the acceptance of a permanent soul, distinct from the mind-body complex, the idea of getting rid of suffering is futile.

With this he introduces the Jain school, which does accept a permanent self in the body. He specifically refers to two of its principles, namely, the size of one’s soul being that of one’s physical body, and the seven-fold predication known as sapta-bhangi-nyaya. He refutes them both by logical arguments and the practical difficulty in accepting these principles.

Next, he introduces the Carvaka school, but not as superior to the Jain. Rather by analogy, he seems to put Carvaka in the same category as the Jains. Mysteriously, he does not hint at why Carvaka is third in his list. He uses very harsh words for the Carvakas, such as calling them murkha (foolish). As is seen in Yamunacarya’s Siddhi-trayam, he makes further divisions in the Carvaka school and refutes them all.

After this, he briefly introduces the Madhyamika school of Buddhism, which holds the view that the ultimate truth is the Void, suṣóṁ. Śrī Baladeva says that the Void cannot be said to exist or not exist. If the first of these is accepted, then it goes against the very definition of voidism, and if the second is accepted, then it cannot be the goal of one’s spiritual practice. No existent being aims for a non-existent object.

With this he introduces the first Vedic system, the school of Vaisesika. After speaking about the seven categories of this system he talks about the sixteen padarthas of the Nyaya school (usually referred to as a connected or complementary system of thought), and the process of liberation propounded by it. He also describes the four means of attaining valid knowledge accepted by the Nyaya school. He mentions an interesting argument given by the Nyaya school to distinguish the soul from the body. Anything that is made up of material parts is meant for other and does not exist for itself, for example, a table or a chair. The body is made up of parts, but the soul is indivisible. Thus the body is meant for the soul. The enjoyer is different from the enjoyed, hence the soul is distinct from the body. This also proves the eternality of the soul because an object without parts cannot be destroyed.

Our author agrees with the Nyaya, Vaisesika, and Prabhakara Mimamsa schools that the soul is eternal and distinct from the material body it occupies, but these schools believe that the soul is all-pervading (vibhu), which is not acceptable. Śrī Baladeva takes resort to Upanisad statements as well as to Vedanta-sutra to refute the idea of the soul being all-pervading, and establishes that it is atomic in size. He also points out that whenever the atma is stated to be vibhu in the scriptures, the reference is to the Immanent Being, Paramatma, and not to the individual self. Moreover, the above-mentioned schools aim only at cessation of suffering but not on attaining happiness. In fact, the Nyaya school counts happiness as one of the twenty-one types of suffering. Suffering, even if removed, can reappear. Śrī Baladeva also refutes the atomic theory of creation propounded by the Vaisesika school.

The next school discussed is Samkhya, which accepts two basic principles of Prakrti and Purusa, matter and consciousness, both of which are eternal. Prakrti further evolves twenty-three elements when animated by the conscious principle, Purusa. Along with this Baladeva also introduces the Yoga school of Patanjali, which is based on the system provided by Samkhya. Śrī Baladeva refutes both these schools with the argument that the combination of Prakrti and Purusa is not possible because of their opposing natures, the soul being conscious and Prakrti inert. Therefore, the famous analogy of Prakrti being a blind woman and Purusa a lame man is not applicable. The author here makes a brilliant argument against the popular principle of satkarya-vada accepted by these two schools. According to satkarya-vada, nothing is really created or destroyed. The cause and effect exist simultaneously. Effect is nothing but the cause revealed, and cause is nothing but the effect concealed. If this theory is accepted, then suffering could never come to end, but would always exist in some form or the other. Thus there is no possibility of liberation. On the other hand, however, if liberation is a transformation of suffering, then there is no difference between the two and liberation cannot be the goal of life.

Next, the author describes three different denominations of the Mimamsa school. This includes the Aikabhavika, Prabhakara, and Kumarila Bhatta schools. Aikabhavika is not a well-known system. It is mentioned in the commentary of Sankaracarya on Brahma-sutra 3.1.8. Thus it is a bit of a mystery why Śrī Baladeva bothered mentioning and refuting it in such a concise work. Since the Mimamsakas base their systems exclusively on the karma-kanda part of the Vedas, he refutes them by using the Upanisadic statements.

This is followed by a refutation of the Bhedabheda school of Bhaskara. According to this school, the soul is different (bheda) from Brahman in its conditioned state but merges into Brahman at the time of liberation. One must note that the Bhedabheda of Bhaskara is radically different from the Acintya-bhedabheda of Caitanya Mahaprabhu, which holds that the soul continues to maintain its individuality even in the liberated state. Śrī Baladeva stresses the ontological identity of the soul in both its conditioned as well as liberated states. This is the basis of bhakti.

Śrī Baladeva also briefly mentions the Pasupata and Hiranyagarbha schools. It would have been helpful for the reader if he had shed a little more light on these two systems. However, he appeared to be more interested in focusing on Advaita-vada, which he considers as his main opponent and expends maximum effort in its refutation. First he gives the basic principles of the two primary divisions of Advaita Vedanta, namely Pariccheda-vada and Pratibimba- vada. The primary defect of these schools is their misinterpretation of sruti statements and accepting imaginary concepts such as the absolute oneness of the atma and Brahman. Śrī Baladeva begins by refuting the desire for liberation in this system by logical arguments. Then with trenchant logic he severely attacks the identity between jiva and Brahman as propounded by both Pariccheda-vada as well as Pratibimba-vada. He goes on to scrutinize their concepts of the falsity of the world, and the validity of the very concept of monism. He has very beautifully and briefly culled his arguments, which need a lot of deliberation to grasp them. It is indeed a pleasure for Vaisnavas to study all of these arguments in one place.

Having briefly described the basic principles of various schools and shown their incompleteness or imperfections, Baladeva moves on to discuss the true intent of the scriptures as presented by Badarayana in Vedanta-sutra. According to Vedanta, he writes that there are three ontological categories, namely, Isvara (God), jiva (individual living being) and maya. Maya is a real energy of God and consists of three gunas called sattva, rajas and tamas. Isvara, jiva and maya are all eternal, the last two being under the control of God. Thus it is only the jiva who is influenced by maya, and never God. Śrī Baladeva gives numerous citations to prove these points. The important thing to be noted here is that these references do not need any interpretation, unlike Advaita Vedanta, where it is necessary to resort to tricks like bhaga-tyaga-laksana to prove the identity of jiva and Brahman. The distinction between jiva and Isvara continues even in the liberated state. This uproots the basic inspiration of Advaita Vedanta, which considers both jiva and Isvara to be products of maya. It also defies the theory of the Bhedabheda school of Bhaskara, which says that the difference between jiva and Brahman exists only in the conditioned state. The author also gives the true purport of the sruti statements that seem to indicate absolute oneness between the jiva and Brahman. The oneness mentioned in these statements informs us of the similarity of the two, both being inherently conscious. The statements that proclaim that there is only one Reality, Brahman (e.g., Gopala-tapani Upanisad 2.65) should not be taken literally. They mean that there is nothing superior or equal to Brahman.

The author gives a unique interpretation of the famous mahavakya, tat tvam asi - “You are That.” It means that God is the supreme object of love, just as one loves one’s own self. Thus this mahavakya teaches union or oneness in love and not ontological oneness. He further elaborates on this theme of love. He concludes that love for the Supreme, and not liberation is the ultimate goal of human life, purusartha. This is the novel contribution of Śrī Caitanya Mahaprabhu to Indian philosophy and Śrī Baladeva brings it out very crisply.

The author cites and comments on five verses of the Gita (15.16-20), which show that the above conclusions are taught by Bhagavan Krsna Himself to Arjuna. He raises arguments against the principles of distinction between jiva and Brahman and then refutes them. The author makes the point that by the grace of God one gets a spiritual form at the time of liberation and lives eternally with God in His abode. Then one is never subjected to the law of karma and thus never has to take another material birth. He supports his conclusions with copious citations from varied scriptures.

The conclusions of Śrī Baladeva Vidyabhusana can be summarized as follows:

- 1\. There are three fundamental categories of Reality, namely Isvara, jiva and maya.
- 2\. All three are eternal.
- 3\. Isvara controls both jiva as well as maya.
- 4\. Isvara is never influenced by maya.
- 5\. The bondage of jiva has no beginning.
- 6\. A jiva can never become Isvara or Brahman.
- 7\. Only by surrendering to Isvara can a jiva become free from the bondage of maya.
- 8\. At the time of liberation, the liberated jiva is granted a spiritual form suitable to serve God in His abode eternally.
- 9\. Love of God is the highest human pursuit.
- 10\. The jivas and maya, being part or energy of God, are simultaneously different and non-different from God. Such a relation is trans-rational, acintya. It can only be ascertained from the scriptures. This is called acintya-bhedabheda-vada.

## About this translation

Until now this book has remained hidden from the eyes of scholars as well as the general public. Hardly anyone even knew of its existence. There is no mention of it in the popular lists of Śrī Baladeva Vidyabhusana’s works. Dr. Demian Martins, who has been tirelessly working singlehandedly in search of various manuscripts of the works of Śrī Baladeva Vidyabhusana while travelling to different parts of India, came across a rare manuscript of Tattva-dipika. He was more than thrilled by the discovery and took upon it himself to bring the book to light. He has a done a great service to the whole Gaudiya school and to scholars in general. For the benefit of English speaking readers he worked hard to translate the book into English. There was only a single manuscript rife with scribal lapses and the translator had to struggle to make sense of them. Thus he occasionally has had to make assumptions based on the context and make corrections in the reading. Moreover, the writing of Śrī Baladeva Vidyabhusana is very terse. He assumes a lot while making an argument. Consequently, it was no easy task to grasp the author’s view and translate it into comprehensible English. On the whole the translation is very close to the original, with just some words being added for the sake of clarity.

Additionally, to help the reader to grasp the line of thinking of the author, Dr. Martins has added apt titles and subtitles which are missing in the original work. He has also given copious footnotes. I hope that in the future he will also write a lucid commentary on this work to enlighten the interested reader. This being the first edition of this work in English, it will certainly elicit the interest of scholars, and I hope that they will appreciate his sincere effort. He deserves praise and encouragement for this wonderful service to the scholarly world, and especially to the Gaudiya Sampradaya.

## Buddhism

For instance, the Buddhists think as follows: The conglomerate of elements (samudaya) is twofold - external and internal. The external conglomerate consists of land, rivers, oceans, etc., and the internal conglomerate consists of the mind and its modifications. The mind is the aggregate of consciousness (vijnana-skandha) and its modifications are the other four aggregates. The whole universe is comprised of this twofold conglomerate. Four kinds of atoms are the cause of the external conglomerate. Among them, the atoms of earth are solid, the atoms of water are liquid, the atoms of fire are hot and the atoms of air are in constant motion. By the simultaneous combination of all these elements, the external conglomerate is produced. The fivefold aggregate (skandha) is the cause of the internal conglomerate. They are the aggregate of material elements (rupa-skandha), the aggregate of consciousness (vijnana-skandha), the aggregate of sensation (vedana-skandha), the aggregate of cognition (samjna-skandha) and the aggregate of past impressions (samskara-skandha).

\[Devanagari text\]

Sound, touch, form, smell, taste, the five sense organs and the gross material elements as perceived by the mind are collectively known as rupa-skandha. The conception of “I” is called vijnana-skandha. The perception of suffering caused by this conception is called vedana- skandha. The cognition of names such as “John,” etc., comprises the samjna-skandha. The past mental impressions caused by all these are referred to as samskara-skandha. By a combination of these five aggregates, the internal conglomerate is produced. All of these categories have a transient existence. In the Buddhist view, the soul is transient consciousness (vijnana-skandha), which is different from the gross body, senses, etc. They claim that the ultimate goal for the soul is the absolute cessation of existence or to become immersed in the ‘store consciousness’ (alaya-vijnana). However, this concept is not true, for the conglomerate of atoms and the aggregates would not be possible unless there is a conscious being that mixes them.

\[Devanagari text\]

If someone claims that Buddha is the being who combines them, we say that it is not so, because since he is also a transient being and has no cognition of any desirable object to be attained by such means, he has no purpose in starting the creation, and also because it is not possible to combine the atoms, which are said to be devoid of parts. Moreover, their concept that when a subsequent moment comes forth, the previous moment ceases to exist is not logical, for the Brahma-sutra (2.2.20) states: uttarotpade ca purva-nirodhat, “If when a subsequent moment is produced, the previous one ceases to exist, then there can be no causal relation between avidya and the rest.” The reason is that there can be no combination of a material cause (upadana-karana) and its effect if the effect coming into existence in the subsequent moment of its transient cause is not uninterruptedly connected to it. A material cause is effective only when such a connection between the cause and effect exists, without which no effect can be produced.

\[Devanagari text\]

In this way, with the annihilation of the cause, the relation with its effect cannot be defined. This is the meaning of the above mentioned aphorism. Similarly, a desire for an ultimate goal which is eternal and unchanging could not possibly arise in a transient soul. Being constantly afflicted, the soul is the very embodiment of all sufferings, and in its absence, suffering or anything pertaining to the soul would cease to exist. Yet nobody desires their own destruction. Because one’s soul is by nature an object of one’s unalloyed love, a life with suffering is preferable to self-annihilation.

As for the view that the fourfold reflection - i.e. the reflection on specific characteristics (svalaksana-bhavana), the reflection on transience (ksanika-bhavana), the reflection on suffering (duhkha- bhavana), and the reflection on the void (sunya-bhavana) - are the cause for attaining the goal of life, that is also incorrect.

\[Devanagari text\]

The reason is that there is no evidence for it, and also because it contradicts the statement of the Svetasvataropanisad (3.8): tam eva viditva ’timrtyum eti nanyah pantha vidyate ’yanaya, “Only by knowing the Supreme Lord can one transcend death. There is no other way to cross over it.” Moreover, since there are factually existent beings with their respective arrangements according to name, species, etc., this fourfold reflection cannot be more than a mere mistake. By the same argument, the theory that Buddha has an omniscient continuous flow of consciousness (sarvajna-santana) is also refuted. An omniscient being’s continuous flow of consciousness free from distress, etc., is understood as a synonym of totality;

\[Devanagari text\]

and the mundane continuous flow of consciousness filled with distress, etc., is understood as a synonym of an individual. The reason is that an omniscient being would have the cognition of all existing distress and thus even in comparison with the mundane flow of consciousness, such cognition would consist of much greater suffering. Moreover, this view is inconsistent because in such an omniscient state one’s consciousness would merge with someone else’s and thus there would be no distinction between their perceptions. On the other hand, the cognition of things such as blue and white objects would also equally merge with each other, and thus the whole universe would be devoid of any duality. In this case, there being no distinction between the flow of consciousness and the conscious being, an omniscient consciousness would also be perishable, and a perishable consciousness would imply the non-permanency of the state of liberation.

\[Devanagari text\]

## Jainism

An opponent may agree with us and claim that in this case, the Jain philosophy must be correct, as they do not accept the annihilation of the soul. The Jain concept is as follows: There are two kinds of elements - the living entities and the non-living entities. The living entities are conscious, of the same measure of their bodies, and constituted by parts. The non-living entities are sixfold. One group consists of things like mountains, etc., and the other five are named asrava, samvara, nirjara, bandha and moksa. That by which the soul is impelled towards the sense objects is called asrava, or the combination with sensual objects. That which covers one’s discriminative power, etc., is called samvara, such as ignorance, etc. That by which lust, anger, etc., are completely dissolved is called nirjara, such as plucking the hairs, climbing on a hot rock, etc.

\[Devanagari text\]

The cycle of birth and death brought about by the eight kinds of activities is called bandha. The eight kinds of activities are as follows: four kinds of harmful activities in the form of sin and four kinds of harmless activities in the form of virtue. Moving continuously upwards or moving in the space beyond the universe (alokakasa) is called liberation, which a soul now entangled by the eightfold activities can attain by the means described in the Jain scriptures - austerity, one-pointed concentration on the soul, and maturity of religious observances, by which the eight kinds of bondage are destroyed. The soul then becomes fixed in happiness and acquires unobstructed knowledge. Liberation is compared to the freedom of a parrot breaking out of its cage. This freedom is the goal of life. This is the renowned Jain doctrine.

\[Devanagari text\]

Their philosophy is defined in terms of the sevenfold predication known as sapta-bhangi-nyaya, which proposes seven possible ontological views about any object: 1) syad asti - perhaps it is; 2) syan nasti - perhaps it is not; 3) syad asti nasti - perhaps it is and it is not; 4) syad asty avaktavyah - perhaps it is and it is indescribable; 5) syan nasty avaktavyah - perhaps it is not and it is indescribable; 6) syad asti nasty avaktavyah - perhaps it is, it is not and it is indescribable; and 7) syad avaktavyah - perhaps it is indescribable.

However, this explanation of the different elements starting with the souls as being arranged according to this sevenfold predication is most unlikely, for the simultaneous coexistence of contradictory attributes such as being and not-being in the very same object is not possible. Supposing the soul is of the same size of the body, if by the influence of one’s past karma a soul which is now of the size of a mosquito acquires the body of an elephant, it would not be able to experience pleasure and pain in all limbs of its new body. Similarly, if due to its past karma a soul now in a human body attains the body of a mosquito, it would not be able to fit there.

\[Devanagari text\]

Someone may argue, “The soul is constituted by unlimited parts, thus, upon attaining a small body, some parts are left, and upon attaining a large body, some parts are taken, therefore it is possible to conceive that the soul is of the same size as the body.” This is not correct, because the soul would thus be mutable, and being susceptible to change, it would be non-eternal like objects such as pitchers, etc. Besides this, one would not acquire the results of the activities that he performed and would attain the results of activities which he did not perform. In this way it would be useless to put into practice the advice of the scriptures. It is also not true that the goal of life is to continuously move upwards or in the space beyond the universe, because since it implies action, it must certainly have a cessation.

Supposing that the state of liberation is happiness, if such happiness is produced, then the previously mentioned fault is also present. If it is not produced, then there would be no difference between liberated and non-liberated souls. Therefore, in this theory there is not the slightest hope for attaining the goal of life.

\[Devanagari text\]

## Lokayata

Similarly, by accepting that the self is nothing but a combination of four elements that becomes distinguished as human beings, etc., the Lokayatas are called fools even by common villagers that cultivate land. The reason is that such a combination of elements is produced and destroyed, and by the visible distinction between living and dead bodies it is understood that they must be different from the self. Moreover, if this philosophy that the body is the self were true, then a new born baby would have no tendency to suck the mother’s breast, for he would have no knowledge of it as a means to attain something desirable. It is also impossible for the Lokayatas to explain the ability to remember one’s past life, as seen in the case of some distinct personalities, for there is no question of remembrance of something which was not experienced.

\[Devanagari text\]

Such remembrance would also not be possible due to a connection with the limbs of a previous body, for everyone’s bodies are connected with the limbs of one’s mother and father, yet nobody experiences the happiness and distress of one’s parents. Similarly, in this same school of thought, there are some who consider the senses to be the self because in their absence the body cannot function, and thus one thinks, ‘I am blind,’ ‘I am deaf.’ Others consider the life-airs to be the self because in their absence, the senses and other organs cannot function, and because they give rise to feelings such as, ‘I am hungry,’ ‘I am thirsty.’ There are still others who consider the mind to be the self because when the mind is in a sleeping state, the life-airs, senses, etc., are absent, and because it is the cause of feelings such as, ‘I wish,’ ‘I don’t wish,’ etc. By speaking thus, such persons are also cheaters, because in these propositions the self is completely absent, as respectively demonstrated by the above logical reasons, and because the senses, the life-airs and the mind are all material - hence they are perishable, the cause of suffering, and are suffering itself.

## Madhyamika Buddhism

The Madhyamikas declare that since knowledge of the truth ultimately culminates in the contemplation of void, voidness itself is liberation. They think thus by reflecting upon the state of deep sleep (susupti), as mentioned in the Chandogyopanisad (3.19.1): asad evedam agra asit, “In the beginning there was only the unmanifest (asat).” In deep sleep there is no existence of anything, therefore the natural thought of someone upon waking is that, ‘I did not exist while sleeping.’ There being no experience of sense objects in that state, they consider that the self is void and that nothing is real. What is the use of desire in such a philosophy, if the very person who desires does not exist at any time? Moreover, does such a voidist philosopher consider void to be existent or non-existent? In either case, the void theory would be ruined. Therefore, such unintelligent people are to be ignored.

## Vaisesika

Someone may argue that since here both the fruits and those who acquire them are perishable, certainly there can be no desire for liberation in the doctrines of the non-believers, and that being non-existent elsewhere, it is appropriate that there must be a desire for liberation within the philosophical systems of the believers. For example, the followers of the Vaisesika system conceive that the individual soul is all-pervading, distinct from the gross and subtle bodies, and becomes a receptacle of nine specific qualities. By realizing the true nature of the soul accompanied by factual knowledge about the similarities and dissimilarities of dravya (substance), guna (quality), karma (action), samanya (generality), visesa (particularity), and samavaya (inherence), and also by worshiping God, one attains liberation, which is the destruction of all activities of those nine specific qualities, when there is total annihilation of their prior state of non-existence (prag-abhava).

\[Devanagari text\]

## Nyaya

Moreover, the followers of the Nyaya school believe that by the enunciation, definition and examination of sixteen categories, one ascertains the twelve kinds of prameyas, by which it is then possible to realize the soul and the Supersoul. On account of such realization, accompanied by the practice of hearing, thinking and meditating, the destruction of false knowledge and all past impressions occurs. Consequentially, the by-products of false knowledge, i.e., attachment, aversion and illusion, also cease to exist. Then the by-products of attachment, aversion and illusion, namely virtuous and sinful activities, as well as the inborn tendency for such acts, all vanish.

\[Devanagari text\]

The karma previously accumulated is then enjoyed by the soul by accepting multiple bodies simultaneously. However, upon its exhaustion, there is no beginning of a new material body, and therefore one attains complete release from all the twenty-one kinds of suffering characterized by pain. This state is called liberation. Furthermore, it is not that they simply imagine this. They substantiate their philosophy with valid means, such as direct perception, inference, scriptural evidence and comparison. For instance, it is proved by these means that the soul is distinct from the gross body, senses, etc., and is eternal and all-pervading.

\[Devanagari text\]

By direct perception one feels that ‘I am the same person who in childhood perceived my parents and now in old age perceives my grandchildren.’ Although the body in childhood and old age is different, this recognition of oneself as being non-different is unconditionally proved without any contradiction, and by substantiating that there is only one person recollecting, it corroborates that the soul is permanent and different from the body. The soul’s existence does not begin at a particular point, otherwise it would not be possible for a new born baby to have the innate tendency to suck the mother’s breast, etc., for such tendencies are remembrances of the means to attain something desirable. Such remembrances are produced by past impressions caused by experience, which invariably has an individual as a receptacle.

\[Devanagari text\]

Therefore, one again obtains something similar to what was previously obtained, for all the previous experiences, impressions, remembrances, desires, etc., are common objects experienced by the very same individual upon whom they depend, and are therefore invariably related as cause and effect. As stated in the Nyaya-sutra (1.1.10): iccha- dvesa-prayatnatva-sukha-duhkha-jnanany atmano lingam iti, “Desire, aversion, effort, pleasure, pain and cognition are symptoms of the soul.” It is also true that because the combined material elements exist for the sake of something else, it is evident that unconscious enjoyable objects, such as beds, chairs, etc., are meant for an enjoyer. Similarly, because the body is not different than any other enjoyable object, it is also meant for an enjoyer. The enjoyer himself, however, is not a combination of matter, and he exists for his own sake, otherwise there would be a regressus ad infinitum.

\[Devanagari text\]

In the same way, since it has no cause, the soul’s existence does not end at a particular point, for it also cannot be destroyed. This is also corroborated in the Brhad-aranyakopanisad (4.5.14): avinasi va are ayam, “The soul is imperishable.” In this way, since the eternity of the soul is proved by direct perception, inference and scriptural evidence, and since there is no possibility of it having any opposite quality such as non-eternity, being pervaded, having parts, or having middle-sized dimensions, it is concluded that the soul has no parts. Therefore, when the question is raised whether the soul is atomic or all-pervasive, it is concluded that since atomic particles combine and produce forms that are visible everywhere, the soul must be all-pervasive. As stated in the sruti: akasavat sarva-gatas ca nityah

“The soul is eternal and all-pervasive like the sky.”

\[Devanagari text\]

Since it is equally proved by the philosophical systems of all believers that the soul is eternal, the enjoyer, distinct from the body, and experiences bondage or liberation, the followers of Vaisesika, Nyaya and Prabhakara Misra’s Mimamsa think that the nine specific qualities known as buddhi, sukha, duhkha, iccha, dvesa, prayatna, dharma, adharma, and bhavana, are produced by contact of the soul with the mind. The appearance of these nine specific qualities causes the soul to be bound in the cycle of birth and death (samsara), and their simultaneous cessation is liberation. As stated in the Nyaya-sutra (1.1.2.): duhkha-janma-pravrtti-dosa-mithya-jnananam uttarottarapaye

tad-anantarabhavad apavargah, “When suffering, birth, activity, defects and false knowledge are successively destroyed, the state of liberation follows after their annihilation.” Therefore, because these systems opine that there is a total annihilation of suffering, that the soul is indestructible and there is an attainment of the ultimate goal of life, thoughtful people may develop a desire for liberation.

\[Devanagari text\]

However, this whole concept is not very wise, because the soul cannot be all-pervading, as it would not be able to actually move to and fro, and because it contradicts the sruti (Mundakopanisad, 3.1.9): eso ‘nuratma cetasa veditavyah, “The soul is atomic and must be understood by the intellect.” Moreover, in the sruti quoted above, the word ‘vibhu’ refers to the Supersoul. Someone may object, “Such an atomic soul as conceived by you would not be able to perceive pleasure and pain in all limbs of the body.” This is not a flaw, for this description of the soul is proved by the Brahma-sutra (2.3.22, 24): avirodhas candanavat, “There is no contradiction, for the perception is felt all over the body just like a drop of sandal,” gunad valokavat, “Or it is felt due to the intelligence, which is a quality of the soul, just like a flame pervades a whole room by its light.” This is so because it is not possible to cut off inherent attributes existing in a particular object.

\[Devanagari text\]

For example, we do not see the destruction of the specific qualities of an existent pitcher as long as they remain as its attributes. As it is said in the Sambandha-vartika (56):

*atma kartadi-rupas cen ḍṁ kanksis tarhi muktatam*  
*na hi svabhavo bhavanam vyavartetausnyavad raveh*

**“If you think that the soul is by nature a doer, then do not entertain any desire for liberation at all. The inherent nature of an existent object can never be removed from it, just as the heat of the sun.”**

If it is accepted that a quality must cease to exist along with its possessor, then we have a similar case of a system which does not believe in the existence of the soul, as previously described.

\[Devanagari text\]

In his commentary to Mandana Misra’s Brahma-siddhi (1.1), Sankhapani states: “A state of liberation characterized by the cessation of specific qualities such as consciousness, etc., is non-different from the view of total annihilation. If an existent object cannot be seen, then it is no better than if it did not exist at all. Who would desire the absolute non-existence of one’s dear self? Therefore, such liberation cannot be considered the goal of life.” The reason is that there is no evidence for the existence of a soul defined as being devoid of all specific qualities, and also because if there is no one perceiving the absence of suffering, then this cannot be the goal of life. Such a state devoid of characteristics would be pointless, like a dance performance in the dark. Someone may object, “Although one may not perceive it, still such liberation is real, for the soul remains in its own constitutional position.” It is no so, for in the absence of evidence, this is not proved.

\[Devanagari text\]

The opponent may argue, “The evidence is the Chandogyopanisad (8.12.1): asariram vava santam na priyapriye sprsatah, The bodiless soul perceives neither pleasant nor unpleasant things.’” It is not so, for this passage of the sruti refers to the state in which even while embodied, the living entity is devoid of bodily identification and has no connection with the pleasures and pains of the body. Moreover, in this passage, not a single word proves the existence of a particular stage when the soul is devoid of the nine specific qualities. Furthermore, the goal of life is not the annihilation of suffering, for there is possibility that suffering may again reappear, just as at present we experience that when a particular suffering disappears, another one appears. Nor can it be said that since the cause of suffering is the prior state of non-existence (prag-abhava), once it ceases to exist, then there is no possibility of it appearing again. The reason is that no matter the cessation of innumerable prior states of non-existence, there is still a possibility of the appearance of so many others.

\[Devanagari text\]

There is also no evidence that all the prior states of non-existence can be destroyed simultaneously. Because the destruction of the prior state of non-existence cannot be accomplished by any human effort, the effort in practices such as hearing, thinking, meditating, etc., as prescribed by texts such as the Nyaya-sutra, etc., would be fruitless. Factually, such activities would just produce more suffering. Such a state of ultimate liberation in which all kinds of pleasures ceased to exist would certainly lead to abhorrence, therefore who would really wish to remain standing like a dry log, devoid of any kind of pleasure? Someone may object, “Pleasure is not acceptable, for it is like honey coated poison and is always accompanied by suffering. Therefore, only the state in which there is total annihilation of the twenty-one kinds of suffering can be accepted as the goal of life.”

\[Devanagari text\]

This view is incorrect, because the absence of suffering is also factual, even when happiness continues to exist, and because they do not consider that happiness and suffering can be conceived of as independent. There is certainly an absence of suffering at the moment of happiness and an absence of happiness also at times when there is an absence of suffering, such as in deep sleep, during universal destruction, etc. Therefore, it is also impossible that the goal of life is a state of happiness defined as the mere absence of suffering, for this would be fallacious. It is well justified to neglect a minor pleasure for the sake of a greater one, but not for the absolute absence of pleasure. This is the path of the saintly persons. Someone may argue, “Fine. Then the correct view is that there is a manifestation of eternal happiness, for this view is devoid of the previously described faults.”

\[Devanagari text\]

It is not so, for here there is no possibility of options. For instance, is happiness different or non-different from the soul? If it is different, is it produced or not? If it is produced, then it is bound to be destroyed, so how can it be the object of desire for the imperishable goal of life? One who desires liberation is indifferent even to the mundane pleasures of Brahma-loka. The cause of such indifference is that these pleasures are produced, hence they are perishable. If pleasure is not produced, then there is nothing which can be said to indicate its existence, since it is not possible to manifest itself nor be ascertained. If pleasure is non- different from the soul, then there would be no difference between liberated and non-liberated beings. In such a state of liberation there would be nothing to be perceived, since there would be no kind of connection with a means such as the mind to grasp knowledge. Thus, there would also be the same fault as previously described.

\[Devanagari text\]

Happiness which cannot be perceived cannot be called the goal of life, for it would be just as good as someone else’shappiness. If it is said that happiness in the liberated state can be apprehended by one’s senses, etc., then there would be no distinction from the state of worldly life. Similarly, their theory of cause and effect is also incorrect. For instance, in the beginning of creation, the first action amongst the atoms happens due to their contact with the souls, which are accompanied by the invisible results of their previous activities (adrsta). Then from the mutual combination of two atoms comes a dual atom; from the combination of three dual atoms comes a hexatom; from the combination of hexatoms comes a tetratom, and in this way gradually the great earth is produced. Thus, atoms are the material cause of the universe - this is the statement of the followers of Arambha-vada. This concept is incorrect. If a combination of atoms is the efficient cause of the first action, is it something visible or is it the invisible results of past activities?

It cannot be something visible, for there is no connection between the efficient cause which produces the first action in creation and efforts such as striking, which depend upon the existence of a body. It also cannot be the invisible result of past activities combined with the souls, for it has no connection with the atoms.  Nor can it be said that the relation is that of samyukta-samavaya, for there can be no connection between the atoms and the souls, both of which are devoid of parts. If there is no connection, there is no accumulation, and if there is no accumulation, there is no material cause. Moreover, the atoms have qualities such as form, dimension, etc., while the soul has opposite qualities such as eternality, minuteness, etc. Thus we have already spoken enough on this philosophy.\[Devanagari text\]

\[Devanagari text\]

## Samkhya

As for the followers of the Samkhya philosophy, they state that there are twenty-five elements, which are primarily classified as two - prakrti and purusa. Prakrti is eternal, mutable and unconscious. Purusa is eternally free from material association, all-pervading and pure consciousness. In this way, by the maxim of the lame and the blind, they explain that all material affairs come into play and become bondages due to this persistent duality from beginningless time. Because the tendency towards various material activities is not manifested simultaneously in everyone, and because, as a rule, so many different bodies are subjected to birth and death, the purusa is different in each body. Yet on account of consciousness, he is counted as a single one among the elements.

\[Devanagari text\]

Although prakrti is devoid of consciousness, with her own transformations she independently becomes the mother of the whole universe starting from the mahat, because she attains such a capacity by turning herself into a shadow over purusa's consciousness. In this way, there are twenty-five elements. As stated by Isvarakrsna in his Samkhya-karika (3):

*mula-prakrtir avikrtir mahad-adyah prakrti-vikrtayah sapta*  
*sodasakas tu vikaro na prakrtir na vikrtih purusah*

**“Prakrti (the primeval cause) itself is not a transformation. Seven elements beginning with mahat are both causes and transformations, and sixteen elements are only transformations. Purusa is neither a cause nor a transformation.”**

\[Devanagari text\]

By pondering over prakrti and purusa, when beginningless ignorance ceases to exist, prakrti has no more power over purusa, who thus has no more tendency to enjoy her again. This state in which there is absolute and everlasting annihilation of the threefold miseries is said to be the state of liberation for purusa, although he is unalloyed by his own nature.

\[Devanagari text\]

## Yoga

As for the adepts of Patanjali’s Yoga system, they follow more or less this same line of thought. They think that by attaining maturity in yoga practice and detachment by reflecting on the nature of prakrti and purusa and by performing yama, niyama, asana, pranayama, pratyahara, dharana, dhyana and samprajnata-samadhi, upon obtaining the mercy of the Supreme Lord, they are then able to restrain all the activities of the mind, after which they attain the state of asamprajnata-samadhi called dharma-megha, which leads to final liberation. They also imagine that since the soul is devoid of material association and has no attributes, suffering is taken as an attribute of the intellect. Therefore, even upon attaining the cessation of suffering, which produces the destruction of the inherent cause of suffering too, still the annihilation of the soul is not possible, for it is self-luminous - thus it is not similar to someone dancing in the dark.

\[Devanagari text\]

This view is not correct, because being pure consciousness and devoid of material association, the purusa cannot possibly combine with the pradhana. Prakrti is dull matter and purusa is conscious, therefore the analogy of the lame and the blind is inappropriate. Besides this, the transformations of prakrti are very difficult to define. For instance, the mahat cannot take form while remaining in the state in which pradhana, consisting of the three modes of nature, is equally balanced, for there cannot arise any other form from an existing object if it remains in its previous form or is destroyed. If this balanced state is destroyed, then pradhana, which is the cause of mahat, would itself be destroyed. How then could mahat be produced without having anything to depend upon?

\[Devanagari text\]

Someone may argue, “Nevertheless, the three modes of nature remain, and therefore there is no such fault as mentioned.” In this case we have to ask the following question - Do the modes of nature undergo any transformation or not? If they do not undergo transformation, they would not become the material cause of the mahat, for they would swerve from their original state. If the modes of nature do undergo transformation, their original state would be destroyed, and then how would mahat be produced without a material cause? In this way, either being destroyed one after another or remaining as they are, the elements would not be able to produce their subsequent elements. Furthermore, in the Bhagavad-gita (2.16) it is said:

*nasato vidyate bhavo nabhavo vidyate satah*

**“The non-existent has no permanence and the existent has no annihilation.”**

\[Devanagari text\]

On the basis of this statement, according to the theory that the effect exists in the cause (satkarya-vada) it would not be possible to restrain the existent suffering nor to produce its non-existent restriction, and therefore, since such a concept of liberation is not even slightly different from the bondage of the cycle of birth and death, it cannot be the goal of life. This is similar to the other systems previously described. In comparison to this philosophy, Nyaya and Vaisesika are much more logical, for in those systems it is definitely possible to restrain existent suffering and to produce its non-existent restriction. Someone may object, “Something existent can be manifest and non-manifest.” It is not so, for these two states would be similarly existent and upon pondering over one another, there would be a regressus ad infinitum. In this way it would be taken as indescribable, therefore one would fail to ascertain the real nature of anything and this would be a non-Vedic philosophy. For all these reasons, the philosophy of Samkhya and Yoga is not logical.

\[Devanagari text\]

## Aikabhavika Mimamsa

There are others, like the followers of the aikabhavika theory of Mimamsa, who say that even without knowledge of the soul, by giving up the performance of prohibited activities and activities according to one’s wish, and by performing one’s regular and occasional duties, new karma is not produced. When the results of one’s karma become exhausted after being fully enjoyed, one attains the goal of life characterized by the annihilation of all karma. This is not true, for due the lack of evidence, it cannot be testified.

\[Devanagari text\]

## Prabhakara Misra’s Mimamsa

The followers of Prabhakara Misra’s Mimamsa think that by the performance of Vedic rituals accompanied by the spiritual knowledge prescribed in the scriptures, one attains the goal of life in the form of complete eradication of the relation with the body, senses, etc., which thus causes the dissolution of all virtuous and sinful activities. This concept is not very sound, because their injunctions are not efficient for producing knowledge, and because their philosophy is also tainted by other flaws.

\[Devanagari text\]

## Kumarila Bhatta’s Mimamsa

The followers of Kumarila Bhatta’s Mimamsa say that it is only by the combined practice of both knowledge (jnana) and Vedic rites (karma) that eternal knowledge and eternal happiness can arise for the soul, which is a combination of consciousness and unconsciousness. When by eternal knowledge one becomes indifferent to any kind of object, then eternal happiness becomes fully manifest, which they call liberation. Some of them hold the view that liberation is the manifestation of eternal happiness or the absence of suffering, and is attained by mental knowledge. This view is also incorrect, because their concept that the soul is composed of consciousness and unconsciousness contradicts the sruti, according to which the soul is pure consciousness, and in the latter’s case because the view of liberation as the absence of suffering is included in Kanada’s Vaisesika theory and is therefore similarly non-Vedic.

\[Devanagari text\]

## Bhedabheda

There are also others who accept the theory that the soul and Brahman are different and non-different, and declare that by the constant practice of knowledge (jnana) and the performance of work (karma), the soul, which is a by-product of Brahman, disappears within its original cause along with all the impressions of previous material activities, thus ceasing to be a different portion of Brahman. This is what they call the goal of life. Others imagine that Brahman has two states, changeable and unchangeable, which are just like two different kinds of oceans with and without waves, and that by the combined practice of both knowledge and work, upon giving up the changeable state, one attains the unchangeable state. This concept is also incorrect, because the theory of difference and non-difference is contradicted by all kinds of evidences, and because the cessation of spiritual difference is not possible.

\[Devanagari text\]

What could possibly cause its cessation? If someone argues that knowledge of the truth is the cause, then is the object of such knowledge different and non-different or merely non-different? In the first case, there cannot be cessation of difference, for knowledge does not make its object cease to exist. In the second case, since difference would be removed by knowledge, difference would be only imagined, for as a rule, only imagined things can be removed by knowledge. If someone argues that this rule is violated when sins, etc., are removed by having the darsana of the Supreme Lord, etc., this is not true, for in this case what removes the sins is a specific prescribed activity, while in this system the intended meaning is that knowledge is particularly distinguished as the remover of something unreal, just as when one sees a city of gandharvas in a dream, mistakes a shell for silver, etc. As stated by Padmapada in the beginning of the Pancapadika, his commentary on the Brahma-sutra: “Knowledge can remove only ignorance.” If only non-difference can be proved by the Vedas, then how can difference be supported by the scriptures? If difference and non-difference can be proved by the Vedas, then even in the liberated stage such difference and non-difference would similarly exist, for there is no evidence that there is only non-difference. In the same way, the theory that Brahman is like a twofold ocean with and without waves is also incorrect on account of the lack of evidence.

\[Devanagari text\]

## Pasupata

The followers of the Pasupata philosophy imagine that by following the religious practices as enunciated in their system, one attains the presence of Lord Siva, which is the goal of life, and never comes again into the cycle of birth and death.

\[Devanagari text\]

## Hairanyagarbha

Similarly, the followers of the Hairanyagarbha philosophy believe that by several kinds of worship such as the pancagni-vidya, etc., one attains Hiranyagarbha through the path of light. This concept is also not well thought out, for in the Chandogyopanisad (1.6.8) it is stated: ye casmat paranco lokas tesam ceste deva-kamanam, “The Supreme Lord rules the worlds above the heavenly planets and fulfils the desires of the demigods.” From this we understand that God is the one who fulfils desires even in the upper planetary systems and that even the demigods’ beatitude is dependent on His authority.

\[Devanagari text\]

## Advaita Philosophy

In the same way, other modern cripple-minded people who speak their own concoctions are also mistaken. Therefore, since all these philosophies have been refuted, the philosophy which propounds that the soul is pure consciousness without any attribute is the best. For instance, Brahman is only one and the same undistinguished, unchanging consciousness which becomes divided as God and the living entity due to such a relation with indescribable nescience, which functions both as vidya (knowledge) and avidya (illusion). Or else, God and the living entity are Brahman reflected on vidya and avidya.

\[Devanagari text\]

It is just like one and the same sky can be divided by a forest or by a tree, or can be reflected on a pond or in a water-pot, and is thus designated accordingly.

The sruti also supports this explanation:

*indro mayabhih puru-rupa iyate*

**“The Supreme Soul moves through many forms by the power of His energies.” (Rg-veda, 6.47.18 and Brhad-aranyakopanisad 2.5.19)**

*eka eva hi bhutatma bhute bhute vyavasthitah, ekadha bahudha caiva drsyate jala-candravat*

**“The very same soul of all beings is situated in every living entity just like the moon reflected on water is seen as both one and many.” (Amrta-bindupanisad, 12)**

\[Devanagari text\]

By interpreting sentences like “tat tvam asi” (You are that) as bhaga-laksana, they say that having realized the original undivided pure consciousness of the soul and the Supersoul, one who desires liberation and has acquired qualities such as control of the mind and senses, etc., is able to restrain the impressions of sense objects, and by the practice of hearing, thinking and meditating, he also refrains doubts about the validity of the scriptures, doubts about the meaning of the scriptures, and unfavourable thoughts. When these four kinds of impediments are destroyed,

\[Devanagari text\]

the soul itself is liberation, being free from beginningless nescience and existing in the form of supreme bliss and consciousness. As nescience is the root cause of all suffering, upon being removed, complete cessation of all suffering takes place and supreme bliss becomes manifest. Because this theory conforms to the Vedas and it substantiates all its assertions, here there is a possibility that the desire for liberation may arise in the minds of thoughtful people. Yet this philosophy is also not well thought out, just like an elephant’s bath, for there are too many imaginative concepts and because they distort the meaning of the Vedas. For example, the aforementioned desire for liberation - if it be asked what the object of such a desire is, the reply must be ‘the soul.’ However, is this object unknown or known to oneself?

\[Devanagari text\]

It cannot be unknown, as desire for something which is not known cannot arise. It also cannot be known, for the soul is eternally perfect and has nothing to accept or reject, therefore it cannot have desire. Nor can it be said that although the soul is eternally perfect, because in the worldly stage it is covered by nescience, its qualities of self-luminosity and eternal exclusive pleasure in the self are not manifest, and that in the liberated stage when nescience ceases to exist, the soul again particularly manifests such qualities, which characterize the disappearance of nescience, and therefore desire is actually there. The reason is that it is not possible to describe the cessation of nescience. For instance, is the cessation of nescience different from the soul or is its own original constitutional position? The first option cannot be, for thus there would be duality and the non-dualism taught in the sruti would be contradicted. The second option also cannot be, for in this case even in worldly life everyone would be liberated. Someone may object, “Simply because the soul is perfect, it does not mean that it has no desire. According to you Vaisnavas, is such perfection actually existent or is something which is just known?

\[Devanagari text\]

The first option cannot be, for your argument is disproved when one recovers a forgotten necklace or when the misconception of rope for a snake disappears. Therefore, the second option also cannot be, for it shows that the soul possesses desire to attain perfection. Consequently, just like in finding a forgotten necklace or in removing the misapprehension of a rope for a snake, similarly attainment of supreme bliss and the disappearance of nescience are objects of desire, which implies that the soul appears to be imperfect, since it is covered by illusion. As it is said in the Sambandha-vartika (1.290):

*na yuktam kamana muktau pumsam nastiti bhasitum*  
*desa-kalanavacchinna-sukhady-arthatva-darsanat*

**‘It is not appropriate to say that men have no desire for liberation, for it is seen that they value happiness which is not limited by time and place.’**

\[Devanagari text\]

Even though the soul is perfect, it has not accomplished supreme happiness, therefore there is desire for it.”

This argument is not very logical, because you have rejected both the non-existence of suffering and the attainment of happiness, and because here the essential nature of liberation is wrongly presented. Therefore, such an argument cannot corroborate the theory of unqualified monism. For instance, you have accepted that Brahman is supportless, all-pervading, devoid of attributes, without a second and is unchangeable consciousness opposed to darkness. What do you mean when you speak about a relation between Brahman and darkness? Is such a relation imaginary, incidental or inherent? It cannot be imaginary, for it would not be possible to describe it. Upon being asked who imagined it, you may reply that Brahman did. However, this is not possible, for it would imply its loss of purity, for to imagine things is itself a particular attribute. If you say it was imagined by the living entity, this is also not possible, for if the living entity itself is an imagination caused by ignorance and ignorance is imagined by the living entity, then there is a flaw of mutual dependence.

\[Devanagari text\]

If you say that darkness imagined darkness, this is also impossible. Moreover, it cannot be said that such a relation is incidental, for this would imply that Brahman is situated in a particular place. It also cannot be said that such a relation is inherent, for then there would be no possibility of its disappearance. Furthermore, is such a relation between darkness and the unqualified Brahman preceded by knowledge or not? The first option cannot be, for then Brahman would not be without attributes. The second option also cannot be, for Brahman would thus be ignorant and the position accepted by you would be unproved. Someone may argue, “Darkness cannot be defined as either real or unreal, therefore its relation with Brahman also cannot be defined.” It is not so, for this is a mere definition without any evidence to corroborate it. Nor can it be said that the evidence is the Rg-veda (10.129.1): na sad asin nasad asit, “Before creation there was neither the existent nor the non-existent.” The reason is that the rest of the sentence says: tama asit, “There was darkness,” which specifies existence.

\[Devanagari text\]

Nor can it be said that such a relation with darkness is proved by the fact that otherwise the passages of the sruti which support the non-dual concept would be inconclusive, for it is by interpreting the sruti otherwise that the meaning of such passages is ascertained. Your concept that Brahman becomes divided due to nescience is also illogical, because becoming an object of division would contradict your statement that Brahman cannot be an object of anything, and because such a division is inconclusive due to its contradictory attributes such as light and darkness. The same is true regarding the theory of reflection too, since something devoid of attributes cannot be connected with any adjunct (upadhi), because something devoid of parts cannot be visible and something which is all-pervading cannot be at a distance, which is required to be reflected on a surface like that of water, etc. Someone may object, “It is not required that there must be distance for reflection (upadhi) to happen, for although the sky is in close proximity to the water, etc., still its reflection is visible.”

\[Devanagari text\]

It is not so, because the sky itself is actually invisible and the reflection is only of the luminaries situated in the sky divided by its adjuncts, which are separated from it. If the sky, which is not perceivable by the eyes, could have a visible reflection, then other elements like the wind and time would also have.

Someone may argue, “Actually the scriptures propound only monism.” In this case, we have to ask, “Is this monism described in the scriptures different from Brahman or is its own essential nature?” The first cannot be, for it would imply duality. The second also cannot be, for it would result in the fallacy of proving something which was already proved (siddha-sadhanata). If the scriptures teach monism, are they real or unreal? The first cannot be, for it would imply duality. The second also cannot be, for it would imply that the scriptures were produced and thus contradict the Brahma-sutra (1.3.29): ata eva ca nityatvam, “Therefore the eternity of the Vedas is proved.”

\[Devanagari text\]

Moreover, even an instructor of monism cannot possibly exist. Does such an instructor actually know the truth or not? The first cannot be, for knowing that there is no second soul, there would be no student for him. The second also cannot be, for it is not possible that an ignorant person may be an instructor.

In this way, since they believe that everything other than the unqualified Brahman is false, such dull-headed people should be ignored by those who desire liberation

\[Devanagari text\]

## Bhagavata Philosophy

Therefore when all concocted philosophies have been thus refuted and the darkness of other philosophies based on sophistry has been removed by the moonlight of our logical arguments, then the desire of a wise person for liberation shines forth within the philosophy that depends on the lotus feet of Lord Govinda. This philosophy is illuminated by the Srimad-Bhagavatam, the priceless jewel churned by the great sage Badarayana from the ocean of the topmost scriptures, and is spread in all traditions, just like the flow of the Ganges. In the Svetasvataropanisad (4.9-10) it is proved that there are three categories - God, the living entity and maya:

*asman mayi srjate visvam etat tasmims canyo mayaya sanniruddhah*  
*mayam tu prakrtim vidyan mayinam tu mahesvaram*

**“The controller of maya creates this material universe in which the living entity, who is different from Him, becomes entrapped by maya. One should know that maya is the material energy and that the controller of maya is the Supreme Lord.”**

\[Devanagari text\]

Because here any imagination is refuted, all three of them are eternal by nature. The sruti\[84\] further states that the Lord stands still while the living entity eats the fruits of its activities:

*dva suparna sayuja sakhaya samanam vrksam parisasvajate*  
*tayor anyah pippalam svadv atty anasnann eko bhicakasiti*

**“The soul and the Supersoul are like two friendly birds situated on the same tree. One of them eats the tasty fruits of the tree, while the other just observes without eating.”**

In the Kathopanisad (1.3.1) it is said:

*rtam pibantau sukrtasya loke guham pravistau ðṁrṁḍṛ parardhe*  
*chayatapau brahma-vido vadanti pancagnayo ye ca trinaciketah*

**“Two beings have entered the topmost place in the body, the core of the heart, and are drinking the result of sacrifice. Those who know Brahman, those who maintain the sacrificial fires, and those who perform three Naciketa sacrifices say that both are like shadow and light.”**

\[Devanagari text\]

The Brahma-sutra (1.2.11) states: guham pravistav atmanau hi tad- darsanat, “The two beings who have entered the heart are the soul and the Supersoul, for this is seen in the scriptures.” Thus, according to both the sruti and the Brahma-sutra, it is proved that God and the living entities are different on account of their distinct attributes. Similarly, the Brhad-aranyakopanisad (4.4.6) affirms: brahmaiva san brahmapyeti, “Being Brahman, one attains Brahman.” The Brahma-sutra (4.4.17) also confirms: jagad-vyapara-varjam, “Liberated souls have all powers except the potency to create the universe.”

From these two passages it is also proved that even in the liberated stage the living entities are different from God. Nor can it be said that non-difference is stated in the sruti, for this idea is only due to inattention. It is clear that one exists as Brahman itself and the other enters Brahman, because after saying that one becomes Brahman, it again says that one enters Brahman, and because Brahman is indicated as an object of the action of entering.

\[Devanagari text\]

Therefore, the passage ‘brahmaiva san’ would indicate non-difference only by transgressing the statements that the soul becomes like Brahman:

*niranjanah paramam samyam upaiti*

**“The pure soul thus attains equality with the Supreme Lord.” (Mundakopanisad 3.1.3)**

*idam jnanam upasritya mama sadharmyam agatah*  
*sarge ’pi nopajayante pralaye na vyathanti ca*

**“Taking shelter in this knowledge, the living entities attain a nature similar to Mine and thus they are neither born at the time of creation nor afflicted at the time of dissolution.” (Bhagavad-gita 14.2)**

*sarva-dainam upasita yavad vimuktir mukta hy enam upasate*

**“One should daily worship the Supreme Lord until one attains liberation. Actually the liberated souls also worship Him.” (Sauparna- sruti)**

\[Devanagari text\]

The Brahma-sutra (1.4.3) states: tad-adhinatvad arthavat, “Pradhana produces effects due to its dependence on the Supreme.” By this maxim it is proved that there is difference between the conscious Supreme Lord and its counterpart, the pradhana, which is dependent on the Supersoul and which, according to the scriptures, is inanimate. This being established, when the scriptures make statements such as ‘ekam evadvitiyam brahma,’ “There is only one Brahman without a second,” (Gopala-tapany-upanisad, 2.65), the meaning is that no one is equal to or superior to Him and that He does not require anyone’s help, for any other meaning has been rejected. Thus, the meaning of sentences like ‘tat tvam asi’ should be understood according to the precept of the Srimad-Bhagavatam (10.14.55):

*krsnam enam avehi tvam atmanam akhilatmanam*

**“You should know that this Krsna is the soul of all living entities.”**

The meaning is that the designation ‘tat’ (that) indicates that the Supersoul is the object of supreme love, just like one’s own self. Therefore, it teaches union with Him because He is the Soul of the living entities. As stated in the Brahma-sutra (1.1.14): tad-dhetu-vyapadesac ca, “Because of the statement that the Supreme Lord is the cause of happiness for the living entities.”

\[Devanagari text\]

Or else, it is understood that because the living entity is part and parcel of the Lord, if He did not exist, they would also not exist. As stated in the Bhagavad-gita (15.7):

*mamaivamso jiva-loke jiva-bhutah sanatanah*

**“The living entity in this material world is my eternal part and parcel.”**

The instruction that love for the Supreme Lord is the goal of life is given in the Srimad-Bhagavatam (3.9.41):

*purtena tapasa yajnair danair yogaih samadhina*  
*raddham nihsreyasam pumsam mat-pritis tattvavin-matam*

**“Love for Me is the highest perfection which men can attain by the performance of pious activities, austerity, sacrifices, charity, yoga and meditation. This is the opinion of those who know the truth.”**

It is further stated in the Srimad-Bhagavatam (5.5.6):

*pritir na yavan mayi vasudeve na mucyate deha-yogena tavat*

**“As long as one does not develop love for Me, Vasudeva, one cannot be freed from the connection with a material body.”**

\[Devanagari text\]

Similarly, intending to explain His own supremacy in the scripture spoken by Him, the Supreme Lord also taught that transcendental knowledge about Himself as being distinct from the living entities is the supreme goal of life. For instance:

*dvav imau purusau loke ksaras caksara eva ca*  
*ksarah sarvani bhutani kuta-stho ‘ksara ucyate*

**“There are only two kinds of existent beings, and these are known among the learned as the entangled souls and the liberated souls. All entangled souls are called perishable because of their material bodies. The liberated souls are unchangeable and therefore called imperishable.”**

*uttamah purusas tv anyah paramatmety udahrtah*  
*yo loka-trayam avisya bibharty avyaya isvarah*

**“The Supreme Person, however, is different from them and is called the Supersoul. Having entered the three worlds, this imperishable Supreme Controller supports all beings.”**

*yasmat ksṁrṁḍ atito ‘ham aksarad api cottamah*  
*ato ‘smi loke vede ca prathitah purusottamah*

**“Because I am situated beyond the entangled souls and even higher than the liberated souls, I am known in the Vedas and smrtis as the Supreme Person.”**

\[Devanagari text\]

*óî mam evam asammudho janati purusottamam*  
*sa sarva-vid bhajati mam sarva-bhavena bharata*

**“One who, without any doubt, knows me in such a way as the Supreme Person knows everything. Î descendent of Bharata, such a person worships Me by all means.”**

*iti guhyatamam sastram idam uktam mayanagha*  
*etad buddhva buddhiman syat krta-krtyas ca bharata*

**“O sinless Arjuna, I have thus spoken the most confidential scripture. Î descendent of Bharata, knowing this, one knows transcendence and becomes liberated.” (Bhagavad-gita 15.16-20)**

The explanation of these five verses is as follows. Just before speaking these verses, the Lord declared in verse 15, “I am the compiler of Vedanta and the only knower of the Vedas.” The implied meaning is, “I am the only knower of all the Vedas, therefore hear from Me as I briefly explain their essential meaning.” He then speaks verse 16 (dvav imau...). The word ‘these’ (imau) indicates that the evidence has already been proved. Two kinds of conscious beings (purusau) are known among the learned (loke). The word ‘only’ (eva) is connected with the word ‘two’ (dvau).

\[Devanagari text\]

Expecting that it might be asked who they are, the Lord mentions them - the entangled soul, subjected to several stages due to a perishable body (ksarah), and the liberated soul, who is eternally situated in one stage, since he has no material body (aksarah) and is characterized as an associate of the Lord. Then in verse 17, He explains the intention for which these two kinds of beings were described - there is a Person different (anyah) than these perishable and imperishable beings. The purport is that one should not think that the Supreme Lord is one of them. Then He says that this is the view of the Vedas, where He is called the Supersoul (paramatma). The word ‘soul’ here is a synonym for ‘conscious being.’ The reasoning is then explained - one who is maintaining (bibharti) must certainly be more accomplished than those who are being maintained. As He pervades (avisya) the three worlds (loka-trayam), one may think that if He perishes, then everything else would perish, therefore He says that the Supreme Lord is imperishable (avyayah). How is this possible? Because of His supreme power, He is the Supreme Controller (isvarah).

\[Devanagari text\]

Apprehending that someone might ask who this Supreme Lord is, He speaks verse 18 along with the evidence - He is known as the Supreme Person both in the Vedas (vede) as well as in the scriptures composed by men (loke). In the prose order here, the word 'ca’ (and) follows ‘ksaram’ (the perishable). To show the intended meaning, in verse 19 He explains the result attained by one who knows Him. ‘In such a way’ (evam) means not as someone who is just ordinarily designated so, just as one who is named ‘Asvakarna’ (Horse-ear). ‘Without any doubt’ (asammudhah) means that one knows that His supremacy is transcendental. One thus becomes the knower of everything (sarva-vit) because this is the sum and substance of all scriptures. Because this is the reason for all forms of worship, one worships the Lord by all means (sarva-bhavena). Finally, with the intention to explain that this knowledge should not be spoken to everyone and anyone, Lord Krsna calls it ‘most confidential.’

\[Devanagari text\]

Then what happens after one knows everything? He knows the transcendence (buddhiman) and becomes liberated (krta-krtyah).

The Kathopanisad (1.3.10) also affirms this same truth:

*indriyebhyah para hy artha arthebhyas ca param manah*  
*manasas tu para buddhir buddher atma mahan parah*  
*matah param avyaktam avyaktat purusah parah*  
*purusan na param kincit sa kastha sa para gatih*

**“The sense objects are higher than the senses, and the mind is higher than the sense objects. Intelligence is higher than the mind, and the great one (mahan), the soul, is higher than the intelligence. The unmanifest is higher than the soul, and the Supreme Lord is higher than the unmanifest. There is nothing higher than the Supreme Lord, for He is the summit and the ultimate goal.”**

Here the words ‘soul’ (atma) and ‘great’ (mahan) are in apposition. There is nothing beyond the Supreme Lord, for He is the summit (kastha). Why? Because He is the ultimate goal (para gatih). Goal means that which is attained.

\[Devanagari text\]

This is also stated by Srila Badarayana in the Brahma-sutra (3.3.59):

*bhumnah kratuvaj jyayastvam tatha ca darsayati*

**“The superiority of the Lord of multiple qualities is like that of the Vedic sacrifices and it is shown by the scriptures.”**

The word ‘bhumnah’ means that Lord Visnu has abundant qualities.

Someone might argue, “Because difference is something known to everyone, because the intended meaning of the sruti must be something which is not known and which may yield some good result, and because your philosophy produces no result, it is not logical.” This is not true. We are not supporting duality as it is in worldly life but difference based upon the interrelation between the Lord and the living entities. Therefore, since this difference is not known and there is a tangible result for one who worshipped the Lord and attained such realization by His mercy, why do you raise such objections, my dear?

\[Devanagari text\]

Don’t you see that even thoughtless people consider everything one? If you argue that learned scholars speak of non-dualism, are they not just repeating the nonsense which you speak, my dear? Nevertheless, there is no separation between the part and the whole as there is no difference between cause and effect, therefore everything we have spoken is faultless. In this way, as the distinction between the Lord and the living entities has been proved on the basis of many evidences, there is a possibility of the connection of darkness and the jiva who, in spite of being conscious, is covered with beginningless aversion for the Lord, just like a lamp covered by a vessel. However, if by chance one associates with the great devotees of the Lord and by their mercy attains knowledge of the truth, then, by ascertaining the original meaning of the words ‘tat’ and ‘tvam,’ one engages in the service of the Supreme Lord, Who is so powerful.

\[Devanagari text\]

When the aversion towards the Lord is destroyed by surrendering unto Him, the connection with darkness is also destroyed, just like the vessel covering a lamp when hit by a hammer. When qualities such as knowledge, bliss etc., become manifest in one’s consciousness, the Supreme Lord, the possessor of eternal and inconceivable energies, the Lord of Vrndavana Whose will is always true, reveals to him the splendour of God realization and manifests the devotee’s sac-cid-ananda form. To attain this state of supreme bliss in which there are no more birth and death is the fulfilment of human life. The evidence is as follows:

*bhayam dvitiyabhinivesatah syad isad apetasya viparyayo ‘smrtih*  
*tan-mayayato budha abhajet tam bhaktyaikayesam guru-devatatma*

**“By the influence of the illusory energy of the Lord, the living entity becomes absorbed in something different than spirit and becomes deviated from God. The result of this is fear, false identification and forgetfulness. Therefore, having accepted the guru as one’s worshipable deity, an intelligent person should worship the Supreme Lord with exclusive devotion.” (Srimad-Bhagavatam 11.2.37)**

\[Devanagari text\]

*ta ekada nimeh satram upajagmur yadrcchaya*

**“Once, by chance, the nine Yogendras happened to go to a sacrifice being performed by King Nimi.”\[89\] (Srimad-Bhagavatam 11.2.24)**

*satam prasangan mama virya-samvido*  
*bhavanti hrt-karna-rasayanah kathah*  
*taj-josanad asv apavarga-vartmani*  
*sraddha ratir bhaktir anukramisyati*

**“In the association of pure devotees, discussion of My pastimes is very pleasing to the ear and the heart. By cultivating such knowledge of My heroic activities, one quickly attains faith, attachment and devotion for Me, the personified path to liberation.” (Srimad- Bhagavatam 3.25.25)**

*daivi hy esa guna-mayi mama maya duratyaya*  
*mam eva ye prapadyante mayam etam taranti te*

**“This divine energy of Mine, consisting of the three modes of material nature, is difficult to overcome. Only those who surrender unto Me cross beyond it.” (Bhagavad-gita 7.14)**

\[Devanagari text\]

*pumstvadivat tv asya sato 'bhivyakti-yogat*

**“Knowledge always exists in the soul even in deep sleep, although it becomes manifest only in the awakening state, just like the capacity to procreate, etc., is merely latent during childhood but becomes manifest during youth.” (Brahma-sutra 2.3.29)**

*tatas tu tam pasyate niskalam dhyayamanah*

**“Only by His mercy can a pure-hearted soul see the undivided Supreme Person through meditation.” (Mundakopanisad 3.1.8)**

*api samradhane pratyaksanumanabhyam*

**“It is known from the sruti and the smrti that the Lord can be seen through worship with devotion.” (Brahma-sutra 3.2.24)**

*prayujyamane mayi tam suddham bhagavatim tanum*  
*arabdha-karma-nirvano nyapapat panca-bhautikah*

**“When I was given the pure transcendental body of an associate of the Lord, I became free from all accumulated karma, and my body, consisting of the five gross elements, was cast aside.” (Srimad- Bhagavatam 1.6.28)**

*na sa punar avartate*

**“One who attains the spiritual world never returns to this material world.” (Chandogyopanisad 8.15.1)**

\[Devanagari text\]

*yad gatva ṣṁ nivartante tad dhama paramam mama*

**“Upon reaching My supreme abode, they never return to this world.” (Bhagavad-gita 15.6)**

*raso vai sah, rasam hy evayam labdhanandi bhavati*

**“The Supreme Lord is bliss personified. One who attains Him becomes blissful.” (Taittiriyopanisad 2.7)**

In all aspects of this philosophical system, the evidence and reasoning are to be found in the original scriptures.

\[Devanagari text\]

## Concluding Words

*syamam rasikan nayanany anandayan yas cakasti sada*  
*vismapaka-damodara-lilo vatu nah sa govindah*

**Govindaji eternally shines while displaying wonderful pastimes such as the Damodara-lila, and in this way He delights Srimati Radharani, His devotees and the eyes of everyone. May He protect us.**

*vedanta-danta-hrdayai racitam mayaitat*  
*sangrhya yukti-nicayam mita-bhasitena*  
*pitambarasya karuna-varunalayasya*  
*karunyatah krtam udetu mude budhanam*

**Having collected the writings of those who control their minds by studying Vedanta, by the mercy of Śrī Pitambara dasa, who is an ocean of compassion, I have compiled this book with brief words, yet it is full of logic. May it give joy to the learned.**

*vidyabhusana-krtina prakasitah so ‘yam adbhuto dipah*  
*pratikula-vata-sange ‘py acancalo yat samullasati*

**By this composition of Vidyabhusana a wonderful lamp (dipika) has been lit, and it shines immovable even in the midst of unfavourable winds.**

Thus ends the Tattva-dipika composed by Vidyabhusana, who is totally dependent upon the lotus feet of Lord Śrī Krsna Caitanya Mahaprabhu.
