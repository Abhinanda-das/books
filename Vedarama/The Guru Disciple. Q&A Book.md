# The Guru Disciple. Q&A Book

## Introduction

For more than ten years I taught a course to new students of bhakti-yoga titled 'The Guru and Disciple Course.' I noticed that some questions kept on coming up in the classes so I wrote a book to help students go a little deeper into the subject.

It was quite an exhaustive volume at 430 pages, and it delved into the history and culture of the guru-disciple relationship, the esoteric aspects, the psychology, and the modern-day sensibilities of arranging one's life to accommodate a guru.

The questions kept coming, and were sometimes challenging, humorous, suspicious, deep or thought provoking. So in this book -small by comparison to the last - I've chosen 164 questions along with my answers. They are all real questions and I answered them either verbally or through correspondence. I hope you like them.

If you teach this subject, as I do, you'll know it can make for great discussions. I hope this book will give you much food for thought.

## What is a guru?

## What is a guru?

The word ‘guru’ is used to indicate someone who is an expert at a branch of knowledge, understanding or skill. In spiritual and religious circles, it means someone who teaches sacred wisdom, exemplifies it personally, and can inspire others to live it, too.

## So, the guru tradition is Indian?

The language is Sanskrit, which is considered by many scholars to be the mother tongue of European languages. The wisdom originates in the tradition known as the Veda , and the geographic spread of this culture centres on what we would now call India. However, this Himalayan wisdom was found as far as Persia in the west, Cambodia in the east and in parts of China and southern Russia. But in most cultures of the world there is a guru-disciple tradition, although the local language and religious references will be different.

The guru teaches the portions of the Veda that will help the student towards this goal. He teaches techniques of yoga and meditation, and he also demonstrates those spiritual practices by his personal example. The guru teaches mantras, ritual, and how to make all activities and ways of life conducive to self-realisation.

## How does a guru know what he knows?

The Vedas describe t wo ways in which knowledge can be obtained. The fi rs t is the ‘ascending pat h’ or aroha-pantha , and the second, the ‘descending path’ or avaroha-pantha . The first path is one of learning through sense perception, the second is the ‘descent’ of knowledge from a transcendent source. The first path is conditioned by the limited range of the senses, the second presumes that there is a body of information which lies above the limits of perception. This naturally fli es in the face of the modern sci entific method, because the c laim that accurate know ledge can ‘desce nd,’ either thro ugh a pro cess of revelatio n, or by learning at the feet of a realised mystic, is simply unknow n. The Vedas state t hat knowl edge rev ealed by this ‘descending p ro cess’ is that which is fr ee fr om limitation.

## But spiritual teachers always make claims of ‘higher knowledge.’ That doesn’t mean they’re right, does it?

A genuine spiritual teacher isn’t someone whose higher knowledge ‘just came to him’, or who says that he received knowledge in a dream, or by ‘revelation.’ A real guru won’t pretend to have secret teachings that noone else can see. A guru, by definition, is a person whose knowledge comes from widely available, authorised scriptures - writings that have been held sacred for thousands of years by millions of people.

## Many religions have ‘authorised’ books that are followed by millions of people. What does that prove?

The historical tradition of the Vedas is the oldest of all, pre-dating all the ‘world religions’ now in existence. Even before they became ‘books,’ or palm-leaf manuscripts, around 3,000 BC, the Vedas were passed on by word of mouth, from guru to disciple. Those verbal teachings and scriptures have proven themselves by the fact that they are still venerated and followed by millions. Many have gained the perfection of yoga by following the advice in the texts. Duration of a text tends to prove its long-term usefulness. Religions founded upon principles and values that are a mixture of divine and human tend to become unsustainable.

## If the books are always there, what is the purpose of a guru?

The tradition of the Vedas calls for both the Shastra , or scripture, and the guru who teaches and exemplifies it. They both work together. It’s not that the sacred text rules out the need for a guru, or that having a guru removes the necessity of the sacred text. Both are required. More than this, the texts say that the original guru is within the heart, and is a divine source of inner revelation who is always enlightening us. Furthermore, that the good people you share your spiritual path with, the ones who speak to you about spiritual subjects, they are known as sadhus , and together they form yet another guru to help you.

## I’ve heard of the Upanishads – what are they?

Upa-ni-shat means ‘come and sit closer,’ and it is an invitation to hear secret teachings of the real self. The great problem of life, say the Upanishads, is that we try to achieve happiness in the wrong place. For happiness to be lasting, it must be uninterrupted, yet the body and mind – and our desires – are in a constant state of flux. Enjoyment comes and goes, satisfaction is momentary, and frustration is the result. This state of perpetual anxiety is known as material life. Spiritual life begins when we look for pleasure within. This is known as the path of yoga.

## What is Vedanta?

The Upanishads are also known as Vedanta . Veda means ‘knowledge’ and anta means ‘end,’ so Vedanta means ‘the end of all knowledge.’ The great compiler of the Upanishads, known as Srila Vyasadeva, took the knowledge of the Upanishads, arranged the conclusions into a series of short arguments, and the result was the Vedanta Sutra. The Vedanta Sutra is often referred to simply as ‘Vedanta.’ Each wisdom school establishes its authority on the basis of a commentary on the Vedanta Sutra.

## What are the Puranas?

Traditionally, there are two types of Veda, sruti and smriti . Sruti means ‘that which is heard,’ meaning the original divine revelations, and Smriti means ‘that which is remembered,’ meaning the speaking of the sages. There is a multitude of incarnations of God who descend to this world, and their teachings to mankind, relayed by the sages, form the body of the Puranas . There are eighteen major Puranas, and the six highest of them are known as the sattvika-puranas . Of those the Bhagavata Purana is the chief. This 18,000-verse epic is also known as the Srimad Bhagavatam .

## Where does the Bhagavad-gita fit in?

Interestingly, the Bhagavad-gita runs from the beginning of the wisdom of the Upanishads right through to the highest conclusion of the Puranas. This is because the speaker is Krishna who, in his own words, is not only the actual compiler of Vedanta, but the knower and the goal of all the Vedas. Although only seven-hundred verses long, the Bhagavad-gita is known as one of the prasthana-trayi , or ‘three starting places’ if one wants to discuss authoritative spiritual wisdom. The other two are the Vedanta Sutra and the Upanishads.

## Does a guru teach all these books?

He must either have studied and understood a wide range of traditional texts, or at least be conversant with them. He may specialise in one, or he may teach all of them, but he would know and be able to substantiate their conclusions. Traditionally the guru gives mantras to his disciples and these would be taken from specific portions of the Vedas. Ultimately, a gur u is someone who knows t he Veda s, r eci tes th e Vedas , lives the Veda s, t ea ches th e Veda s, and whose follower s do likewis e.

## The guru’s community

## Does a guru have a guru?

Yes, his authority to teach comes from his own guru. He is the student of a guru - a disciple. He cannot be a guru for anyone else unless he has been a faithful disciple first. Yet although you may regard him as a guru, he continues to think of himself as a disciple. If the guru thinks to himself: ‘I am a guru,’ he loses his qualification to be a guru. To be a guru he must first be a disciple, and to remain a guru he must remain a disciple.

## If he always considers himself a disciple, how can he act as a guru?

He acts out of service to his own guru. Just as he received the teachings, so he passes them on to others. And then he guides others just as he was guided. He does all this in a spirit of service to his spiritual master. This receiving and giving is an age-old tradition. In fact, for someone to act as a guru he must be at the end of a long human chain of receiving and giving of knowledge.

## A guru can’t be self-appointed?

No, a genuine guru must come in a long line of gurus extending back thousands of years. The original guru in that line would have taught his disciples, who each taught others, and in this way the teachings were handed down. This ‘one-after-the-other’ chain of teachers is known as a parampara , or succession of disciples. Being a contemporary representative of such a succession conveys the guru the authority of his entire tradition, but only if he only repeats exactly what his predecessors taught. In that way he can be ‘transparent via media .’

## Transparent via media ? What’s that?

It’s an expression used by Srila Prabhupada many times. He explained the term as follows: “My spiritual master used to say, ‘ The spiritual master is the transparent via medium .’ For example, I can see the letters of this book ver y nicely through these transparent eyeglass es, without which I can not see beca use my eyes are defective. Si milarly, our senses are all defe ctive. We cannot see Go d wi th these eyes, we cannot hear Hare Krishna wi th these ears, we cannot do any thing wi thout the via med i um of the spiritual master. Just as a defective eye cannot see wi thout the via medium of spectacles, so one cannot approach the Supreme Lord wi tho ut the transparent v ia medium of the spiritual master. ‘ Transparent ’ means that the via medium must be free of contamination. If it is transparent, one ca n see through it.”

## In your succession there are the names of only thirty-two gurus. Are there historical gaps?

Yes, thirty-two names over 5,000 years would mean an average lifespan of 156 years for every guru! The answer to this conundrum is that there are indeed ‘gaps,’ sometimes of many decades. The sequence of gurus listed do not form a continuous chain but are those who were significant in their standing as philosophers, teachers, mystics or authors; those whose contribution was outstanding. Not every guru is listed, in fact there are many gurus whose names are absent. Only the prominent ones are listed. Imagine if you were to trace a finger through the history of Science: you’d have Galileo, Newton, Einstein and Hawking – the big names. Although there were hundreds of other scientists through history, they may not receive a mention if you’re just discussing the major ideas. It’s a similar principle with the line of gurus.

## But I thought there had to be a continuous chain of gurus?

It is not exactly a chain of physical bodies, more a chain of realised knowledge. Just as a river sometimes flows underground, only to reappear miles away, so the flow of gurus periodically disappears, only to reappear years later. We are concerned with the clarity and purity of teachings, not only the connectivity of the mantra-giving.

## Why are there many different Hare Krishna movements?

If each disciple of a guru initiates several disciples of his own, who each initiate several more, the result will be many branches of communities over time. Srila Prabhupada’s guru had eighteen sannyasi disciples, many of whom began their own missions. Some of those now have branches in the western world. Although ISKCON is unique in being a united mission of many branches, there are also a small number of other groups that operate independently.

## If each Hare Krishna movement is following Lord Chaitanya, why do they teach different things?

Lord Chaitanya’s teachings were given initially to his early followers, prominent amongst whom were the ‘Six Goswamis of Vrindavan.’ Each of them was entrusted with a slightly different task, and the sum total of their efforts would fulfil the Lord’s purposes. Today, the total number of people who base their religious faith on those teachings stands at around 35 million. Inevitably, after nearly five hundred years, significant variations of emphasis have arisen in the different communities. All of them, however, are united by their core teachings and their adherence to the practises.

## How does a guru teach?

## How does a guru teach?

In olden times the students would go to wherever the guru was and live with him for some years. By daily repetition of sacred verses and learning the philosophy through proposition, argument and counterargument, they’d gradually understand, remember, and become fluent. The guru would train them in Sanskrit, ritual, worship and ceremony, and he’d offer life-skills and discipline to form their character, particularly if they were young. If the guru was popular and his followers numerous, he’d employ his senior students in teaching the juniors.

In modern times, and in a movement such as ISKCON, there are many hundreds of branches where teaching is given by local senior members. The guru, who is often travelling internationally, gives the mantras and meets with his disciples every few months. Students also supplement their guru’s physical presence with his recorded talks and books, correspondence and electronic media.

## How can I learn from a guru?

Learning from the guru is not merely a matter of knowledge transmission from a teacher to a student. That may work in ordinary education, but the process of learning about the soul is radically different. So different, in fact, that we have to first learn how to learn. It is a transformational process, not merely a transmission of knowledge. Here is a story about learning:

There was once an expert piano teacher in Vienna who had two students. He taught them both for the same number of hours in his home but charged them vastly different rates. When both students compared notes, they came to the teacher and asked for an explanation. “When I asked you how much you knew about playing the piano,” he said to the first student, “you said that you knew nothing at all. So, you were easy to teach, and I charged you accordingly.” Then he turned to the second student: “But when I asked you, you played the piano for me – and you played badly. So I had to first un-teach you, then only could I begin to teach you. I charged you accordingly, too.”

A good disciple is a good student who can listen to the guru with trust. He first knows that he knows nothing and is fully prepared to set aside any attachments to knowledge already acquired that may act as an obstacle to spiritual learning. He is prepared to submit himself to a process of transformation which entails many challenges to his ego. In order to be able to relate properly to the guru, he must voluntarily enter the relationship as someone who is open to correction, criticism or even rebuke. The relationship is not a modern one of relative equality between teacher and student, but a very ancient one of deference, profound respect, and even servitude.

## ‘Deference, profound respect and servitude’ – that sounds daunting. Are they all necessary?

They sound quite severe, don’t they? And that’s the difficulty. Even though we may know about the classical guru-disciple roles, we are very reluctant, as western converts, to enter the master-servant roles in modern times. There are insufficient cultural precedents in western society to start with, indeed we have been told, from a very early age, that being anybody’s servant is not a good thing, and that equity between human beings is always the best political status quo to aim for. Neither do we want to find ourselves in a situation where we’ve succumbed to naivety and become disempowered. Srila Prabhupada once said that taking the step to becoming initiated is ‘suicide of the ego,’ since it really does involve the death of whoever we think we are. But the ego that dies is what he described as ‘false-ego’ or not the real self at all. All our identities of body and mind are transitory and have nothing to do, ultimately, with the true self. Revealing the true self is the culmination of a lot of hard work and constant re-adjustment internally, and it may take some time. So our hesitancy is based on the ego protecting the mind and body from the pain of change.

## Can’t I just read the books?

Books will give you knowledge, of course, but like any academic subject, you won’t become fully qualified unless you have a personal teacher. If you want to learn a musical instrument, for instance, you’ll need to learn from someone who can play one; if you want to become a doctor, you’ll need to attend medical school and learn from a qualified physician. The same applies in spiritual life. Through the guru’s personal example, his strong conviction and character, by his careful and compassionate discipline, and occasional correction, you’ll learn and grow far more. And faith in the guru’s abilities to guide you is a key element. Listen to this text from the Upanishads:

## I would prefer my guru to be more of a spiritual friend than a spiritual master. Is that alright?

You can have someone as a spiritual friend, guide, teacher, counsellor, therapist or priest - in fact any kind of helpful relationship with a spiritually advanced person is possible. But you’ll get far more from the relationship if you take the next step and make the person your guru. A guru is someone different from all of the above. That’s because the way you relate to a guru is different. It involves more from you and is an ongoing relationship of friendship and spiritual direction. Becoming a disciple involves elements of developing humility, engaging in deep learning and service that opens a channel of divine grace. In all other relationships that mystic component is missing. And when you make someone your guru, you’ll receive mantras and appropriate teachings.

## OK, but can I have a guru without initiation?

Having a guru and initiation go together. In the olden days, every new series of classes or period of education involved an initial ceremony to mark the beginning. It was quite possible for someone to be initiated a dozen times during their life. Of course, their original guru, who gave them the most important mantras and teachings, would be their main guru. If your guru is going to teach you anything, and if he’s going to give you any mantras to put that teaching into practise, you’ll have to be initiated. You can’t have a guru without initiation.

## What about just having a shiksha-guru?

A shiksha-guru is someone who gives teachings on a mantra you’ve already received. It’s generally the same person who gave you the mantra. You’d have such a guru after initiation. Although the term shiksha-guru is now loosely used to indicate a mentor or a guide - even a guide before initiation - the term strictly means a post-initiation teacher. The power of the mantra becomes fully available after initiation, not before.

## But I’ve already been given the maha-mantra. Why do I need to be given the mantra again?

According to sacred texts such as the Padma Purana , we gain access to the power of a mantra only through the process of initiation. Some syllables lifted from a book do not constitute a mantra; the process must be followed. Diksha is the ceremonial speaking of those sacred sounds, the recognition of a disciple’s qualification to receive them, the commitment of master and student to work together towards the ultimate goal, and the formal beginning of a new life.

However, the names of God, such as Narayana, Rama or Krishna are distinct. They are even more powerful than a Vedic mantra. The name of God is jivatmaka , the very life and soul of a mantra. The name is Krishna Himself, who does not depend on anyone or anything. He is completely independent. So if Krishna’s name is non-different from Him, and He is independently powerful, why the need for initiation at all? The answer lies in our own weaknesses. The holy name is unlimitedly powerful but its efficacy on the consciousness of the chanter can be lessened by our incorrect mental attitudes and behaviour. The guru assists the disciple by teaching how to avoid these. Jiva Goswami writes: “Indeed, one can chant the holy name without initiation but in Kali Yuga it is necessary to chant under the guidance of an authentic guru. One should receive the holy name from the guru who is a representative of Sri Hari.” (Comments on the Srimad Bhagavatam 7.5.24-25)

## But didn't Chaitanya Mahaprabhu give the mantra freely to all, without initiating them?

Traditionally, a sannyasi travels from place to place. He would not have his own students, since to train and guide them would take years and required a stable residence. Chaitanya Mahaprabhu gave no mantras formally, but he asked everyone he met to chant the names of Krishna. Whilst the giving of mantras is restricted to certain qualified persons, the holy names of God are distributed freely for the benefit of all. Everyone is qualified to chant them. Chaitanya Mahaprabhu wrote no books or manuals, but his associates did. They initiated disciples and trained them in the philosophy and practice of the Vaishnava life.

## Can you go ‘back to Godhead’ without a guru?

The Godhead we are all trying to reach is the eternal abode of God, and God has given instructions for us all to find a guru. If it wasn’t important, He wouldn’t have said it. But since He said it many times, it’s quite clear: we need a guru. Following that time-honoured process means that we can reawaken our eternal relationship with God once more. The guru helps us through careful, relevant and timely instructions, personal guidance, mantra-empowerment, coursecorrection, merciful blessings, service engagement, and by providing a living example.

## Characteristics of a guru

## From what you say, it seems I must be initiated?

The choice is yours. You should not feel pressured into having a guru. In fact, that would be the worst possible motivation. Taking a guru should never be done as a result of feeling pressure from family or friends or being urged to conform to some social convention. Neither should looking for a guru be undertaken to satisfy emotional neediness or gratify some passing religious sentiment. And you certainly shouldn’t do it as a fashion. Accepting a guru is best done when you understand that spiritual progress will give you a better life; when you’ve understood that guidance for that better life can be found in the Vedas; and when you sincerely desire to find someone expert on that path.

## I have recently seen a popular guru – should I ask him for initiation?

Popularity is no indication that someone is either a competent guru or the right one for you. There are many popular figures who have thousands of followers, but a guru cannot be ascertained by counting the number of followers. While many spiritual leaders are good people and have some knowledge, there are many who have not learned enough and who cannot offer sufficient guidance. Their usefulness will expire very quickly. A few of them are positively dangerous and, rather than helping their followers, will only relieve them of their money and precious time. Your main question at this stage should be: can this person help me?

## Can you name the bad gurus for me?

There is no such thing as a ‘bad guru.’ Either a person is a guru, or he is not. There will always be new, so-called ‘gurus’ on the horizon, so it is better to understand what the qualities and characteristics of a genuine guru are, and then you’ll be able to immediately recognise one. You’ll be able to tell the difference between a real guru and a ‘show-bottle guru.’

## What is a ‘show-bottle guru?’

In an old-fashioned chemist’s shop window, there would be at least one large glass bottle filled with brightly coloured water. Although it was meant to advertise the various medicines within the shop, the coloured water had no medicinal value whatsoever. The bottle in the window could not cure anything, it was just for show, hence ‘show-bottle.’ A show-bottle guru is one who looks good but cannot help anyone.

## Are there actually ‘good gurus’ in the world?

Yes, always. Just because there is a rise in counterfeit currency notes it doesn’t mean there is no real money. But to avoid being cheated, you must know the difference between one and the other. Counterfeit money looks like the real money until you know the difference. Similarly, by knowing the symptoms of an authentic spiritual master, you’ll be able to recognise a real guru from a pretender.

## Do I have to find a guru – or will he find me?

Although there is a popular notion that, ‘when you seek a guru, the guru will appear,’ it is not described as being instantaneous in the traditional texts. Your desire must be strong and must be demonstrated by your own actions. It requires a bit of effort to find a guru! In the famous Bhagavad-gita (4.34), for instance, Lord Krishna says: “Just try to learn the truth by approaching a spiritual master. Inquire from him submissively and render service unto him.” The first requirement is that you move towards someone in whom you have faith, then ask them relevant questions and offer them some service. Three things you must do before you can really make a connection.

## What does ‘render service’ mean?

Once you’ve satisfied yourself as to the core characteristics and competencies of the prospective guru, the tradition is that you try to please him both by your respectful questions and the offer of service. ‘Service’ simply means that you help the guru in his ability to serve his guru, whatever helpful may mean to him. If the guru is elderly, service could mean making things easier for him, for instance transport or cooking.

In ancient India, serving the guru meant bringing firewood for his sacred fire, washing the cooking pots and polishing the brass, or cutting up vegetables and fetching water. Pleasing a teacher is a universal way of learning more from him; it increases his desire to help you as a student. The offering of service is not a declaration that you have accepted the person as your guru, it just means that you are preparing the way for a future decision on their suitability.

## Why should I ask ‘respectful’ questions if the person has done nothing to earn my respect?

You can ask any type of questions you wish. But be aware that your questions will reveal a lot about yourself to the guru! The word ‘respectful’ is there to remind you that you can get the best answers from a guru if you avoid egotistic or irrelevant questions – questions where you seek to demonstrate how intelligent you are, or simply questions for your entertainment. Questions for a guru should be about ultimate issues, even if you think you already know the answers. Serious and sincere questions, based on a genuine desire to understand your spiritual nature, will allow the guru to speak directly on the basis of the Vedic texts. It will also easily allow you to understand when a teacher is only able to offer vague or speculative answers.

## What sort of person am I looking for?

As we discussed before, you are looking for someone who knows the Vedas, lives by the Vedas, teaches the Vedas, and who has realised the truth of the Vedas. The expression from the Mundaka Upanishad is that he must be srotriyam brahma-nistham , meaning that he has heard and retained Vedic knowledge, and that he is ‘fixed in Brahman,’ in spiritual consciousness. What that means is that spiritual ideas are not simply theories for him, he knows what he speaks because he has personally realised it. And he walks his talk. You are also looking for someone whose students are successful in their spiritual lives.

## So, what is the qualification of a guru?

Because guru means teacher, he must be able to demonstrate his knowledge by teaching from authorised scripture, and be able to present convincing arguments which clear away the doubts of his followers. He must also – as a result of his great learning and realisation – have understood his relationship with God and be living in that relationship daily. Here is a description of such a guru from the later chapters of the Srimad Bhagavatam (Bhagavata Purana).

*śābde pare ca niṣṇātaṁ*  
*brahmaṇy upaśamāśrayam*

**"Any perso n who is seriously desiro us of achieving real happine ss must seek out a bona fi de spiritual master and take shelter of him by initia tion. The qualif ication o f a spiritual master is that he must have realized t he conclusion of the scriptures by d eliberation and arguments and thus be a ble to convince others of these conclusions. Such great perso nalities, who have taken complete shelter of the Supreme Go dhea d, le aving aside all material consideratio ns, are to be un derstoo d as bona fi de spiritual masters. ## How knowledgeable must a guru be, and how would I know?

You won’t find a brass plaque on the guru’s door, and he may not have letters after his name, but he should be able to demonstrate his competency to refer to standard texts of the sampradaya he represents. In the line of Krishna consciousness, these are the Vaishnava texts such as the Bhagavad-gita and the Srimad Bhagavatam. He may not speak fluent Sanskrit, but he should be able to quote texts in order to support his presentations – and should certainly be able to refute opposing arguments.

For the sake of the student, he must be able to answer any questions, and to clear away any doubts or confusion. Srila Prabhupada writes that: “(The guru must) be well versed in all revealed scriptures…The Puranas are also parts of the Vedas. And histories like the Mahabharata and the Ramayana are also parts of the Vedas. The acharya…must be well acquainted with all these literatures.”

## How ‘pure’ is he supposed to be, and what does that mean?

Purity is measured by the degree to which a person’s consciousness is fixed in his eternal spiritual identity. In that condition he is free from any desires for material happiness, instead relishing inner happiness at every moment. By seeing the soul within others, he sees with equal vision, and is never disturbed by lust, greed or anger. That state of being is evidenced by a lack of interest in sensory enjoyment or other forms of material success. Srila Rupa Goswami, a great mediaeval guru, has written in his small book of teachings, the Upadeshamrita , that: “A person who can tolerate the urge to speak, the mind’s demands, the actions of anger and the urges of the tongue, belly and genitals is qualified to make disciples…”

## Does a guru need to be liberated – and what if he’s not?

Liberation is a spiritual spectrum, beginning from the consciousness of one who is working for his liberation, carefully following the principles and practises of spiritual life under the guidance of his own guru, all the way up to a person who has regained his eternal identity. In general, it is highly advisable that one become a disciple of the most liberated guru. In the absence of such a person, one can become a disciple of someone who is less qualified, but nonetheless advanced on the path of liberation. In such a situation, it is understood that one may also need to take advantage of shiksha gurus in order to complement his understanding and practise.

Here are some more qualifications of the guru from the Sri Mantra-Muktavali : "A genuine spiritual mast er is saintly, devoid of anger, learned in t he Vedas and scriptures, fai thful, fr ee of envy, a pleasing speaker, a perso n who wor ks for the welf are of all living beings, intelligent , hum ble at heart, thoughtful, aff ectionate to his disciples, and one who examines all sides of an iss ue.”

In the Aga s tya-Sa m hita it is said: "A genui ne spiritual master is a wo rs hiper of the Supreme Pers onality of Godhead, peaceful, fr ee of material desi res, aware of the spiritual truth, a teacher of the spirit ual truth, learned in t he Vedas and scriptures, is able to de liver others, able to surmount obstacles, t he best of brahmanas, expert in yantras and m antra s , one who goes to the heart of the matter, awa re of the secrets of spiritual philo sophy, austere, and truthful."

## Where to find a guru

## You’ve said I must look for a guru – where do I begin looking?

If you’ve been drawn to the path of bhakti-yoga by the writings of Srila Prabhupada, you may wish to begin your search amongst his senior followers. There are many thousands of members of ISKCON throughout the world who have found their guru by listening to the various speakers as they visit local temples and groups. After some time, they found themselves gravitating to one particular person, asked them questions and felt spiritually enlivened in their company.

Gradually, they began to regard that person as their guru. You might try that approach first.

## Do I have to find a guru within ISKCON?

There’s nothing in the Vedas that states that you must search for a guru within a particular ashram or organisation, but if an entire organisation has been set up for the purpose of helping you find a guru, it might be easier for you to take some assistance. There are various organisations other than ISKCON, but none that can offer such a selection of candidates from which you can select someone suitable.

## It seems that ISKCON has a list of ‘authorised’ gurus – do I have to choose one from that list?

From the traditional point of view anyone can select a suitable person to act as their guru, provided they fit the spiritual criteria. There is no restriction, and no list other than the list of preferred qualities and characteristics of a guru. However, membership organisations such as ISKCON, which aim for widespread spiritual engagement of the public, try to preserve the legacy of the founder's teachings by approving certain persons to act as guru. The aim is to guarantee the content of what is taught, the standard of teaching and personal example, perpetuity in the dissemination of the teachings and to effect governance and ‘quality control’ of a united, international movement.

This approach also serves to facilitate members’ choices when they wish to identify gurus who have passed the scrutiny of their peers. Yet this is sometimes found to be unsatisfactory when members choose someone who is not on the approved list of candidates, or even in the same organisation. Opinions vary as to how to make suitable adjustments. Meanwhile there is indeed a list of gurus – albeit growing - and initiation within ISKCON can currently only be given by a person on that list.

## Does it matter if the guru lives far away – or is it better to have a guru who lives locally?

There are many statements by Srila Prabhupada to the effect that a relationship with a guru, being a spiritual connection, could not be reduced by physical distance. His disciples, scattered around the world, wrote to tell him how much they missed him, and so he often wrote letters to that effect. ‘Vani is more important than vapuh ,’ he would tell them, meaning that ‘the instructions of the guru are more important than his physical form.’

It is true that a disciple prospers in the company of his guru, and yet it’s also true that God Himself reduces the perceived distance between a disciple and the guru if the disciple is situated in spiritual consciousness. In modern ISKCON, a pioneering movement where members open up frontiers in different countries, it is quite common for disciples to live some distance from their constantly touring gurus. Most of them are quite satisfied by the status quo whereby they see their guru once or twice a year. Others prefer their gurus to be more local, particularly where the guru is from the same country and hence speaks the same language as them. What works for the spiritual progress of the disciple is the main principle.

## How do I test a guru?

When Srila Prabhupada talked with musician John Lennon he told him that: “Just try to see who is addicted to Krishna.” When a man is addicted to something, he thinks about it from first thing in the morning and cannot wait until he can have it again. Similarly, when someone is spiritually advanced, he thinks about Krishna always, talks about Him, and tries to attract others to him. In the Mahabharata it is stated: ‘A man will speak what is on his mind,’ so a pure devotee of Krishna will always be speaking or singing of Krishna.

This test of a guru can easily be applied. Srila Prabhupada commented: “Whoever teaches how to know God and how to love Him – he is a spiritual master.” And on another occasion: “The definition of a genuine guru is that he is simply talking about God – that’s all. If he’s talking about some other nonsense, then he is not a guru. All we have to know is that the genuine guru is simply talking about God and trying to get people to become God’s devotees. If he does this, he is genuine.”

## What is the correct way to approach a guru?

Good question. Krishna tells Arjuna to ‘approach’ a guru, and in ancient times this would mean walking to an ashram where a guru lived, bowing down and putting some questions to him. In modern times the practical side of this idea has not changed much at all. Basically, you are trying to get near enough someone to see if they seem to be capable of inspiring you in spiritual life. You want to know if this person you’ve heard about is knowledgeable, spiritually strong, a good disciple himself and generous with his time. And please don’t forget they are supposed to be happy and friendly. These days most disciples started out by listening to recordings or reading books written by their guru. They then attended classes during his visit to their country, asked a few questions, or exchanged correspondence. A key point for many was when they wrote their guru a formal letter asking if they could regard him as their spiritual guide, with a view to possibly becoming his disciple at a later time. Despite any initial enthusiasm, there is meant to be a traditional one-year period of mutual scrutiny between guru and disciple, and only after that does the formal relationship proceed.

## How do you test someone you already respect?

You see whether his answers to your questions leave you with a greater clarity. Did he provide you with satisfactory answers, or clear up any doubts you were having? Does he inspire you to make deeper spiritual commitments? Do you feel an increased sense of purpose in your life? These are some of the questions you can ask yourself, without losing any respect for the person. Offering respect does not mean the person is already your guru, neither does it mean that you have forfeited the right to test him in many ways. Gurus know that you have this obligation, and they’ll be perfectly comfortable when you exercise it.

## If I see the guru in a dream is that a sign?

Not necessarily. There are many ‘signs’ that we look for when we become interested in making a commitment to spiritual life. Some of them are emotional or sentimental, and some the product of ‘magical thinking.’ There’s nothing wrong with emotions, or even a spot of magic, but you’ll have to exercise caution that you don’t make choices that you’ll regret later. In any relationship, your decision to move forward must be based on both heart and head. You’ll know if you tend to be too much of one or the other, and you’ll need to keep a balance.

## I don’t care how long it takes me to find my guru, I wi ll keep sear ching!

A never-ending search for a guru is the same as having no guru at all. If you’ve convinced yourself that the guru you seek is so exceptional that he hardly exists, then you’ll probably never find him. What you propose so unds very noble but is ultimately impractical. Yo u’ll run out of lif e befo re you fi nd just the ‘right’ guru. Despite all this, you may eventually find a guru. Unfortunately, if i t too k you so long to find your guru, you may again become restless after some time – be ca use you’ll realise your guru wasn’t the ‘ right’ one after all!

## When I feel I’m ready, how do I become initiated? What’s the procedure?

During the one year ‘testing period,’ or even some time before, you can attend the ISKCON Disciple Course . You’ll receive all the information you need on how to become initiated. Essentially, the course will explain about taking guidance from a senior member, joining a group for mutual support, and any other local obligations for helping you develop over this formative period. You will have to satisfy others that you’re able to keep the commitments of the life of Bhakti-yoga, and that you’re making your choice of guru in a mature manner.

## Why are others involved in the procedure?

Just as in a school the teachers are in loco parentis , or ‘in the place of the parent,’ so in the spiritual guidance system, the local Vaishnavas act in place of the guru. In general, the guru is quite some physical distance from the disciple and may see the disciple only infrequently. Some method of local training and supervision is needed. In the early days of the Hare Krishna movement, all the disciples lived communally, but that is increasingly rare these days. Only a small proportion of members live in the same vicinity. A network of groups and senior guides fills the gaps.

## If you find a guru and you have no doubts about him, but worry instead about whether you are ready, what should you do?

It may help if you analyse your insecurities by taking a step back and discussing these with another Vaishnava. Sometimes an incident may shake your confidence and you’ll become concerned about your suitability for becoming a disciple. You don’t know whether you’ll be capable of keeping to your vows throughout your life, or if you’ll end up disappointing your guru. At such times, it’s probably a good thing to reflect that most disciples feel the same way before initiation. If you’re sincere, have some enthusiasm, think you can muster some determination, and believe that Krishna Himself may help you – you will be fine.

## After initiation can I still take instruction from other devotees?

Yes, every Vaishnava within the same community can function as a shiksha-guru. Provided they speak from scripture and have a good standard of spiritual practise, they can be – and should be – a valuable resource for you. Srila Prabhupada created so many disciples and followers that they represent a rich wisdom community. A guru is only pleased when he sees his disciple learning and understanding, from whoever the instruction may come. And if you occasionally hear any philosophy you feel is questionable, something that doesn’t quite sound right, you can bring that to your guru. He will either approve it or explain why it’s incorrect. In that capacity, the guru acts as a ‘coursecorrector’ along the path.

## After I become initiated, what is my relationship with Srila Prabhupada?

Your relationship with Srila Prabhupada develops unlimitedly. Accepting a guru as your spiritual father does not diminish your spiritual connection with your spiritual grandfather. Even in ordinary life, your love for your grandfather only increases with time, even though you have a father. No-one can take your grandfather away from you. Similarly, in spiritual connection nothing is diminished. Srila Prabhupada is a very rare individual and is Krishna’s pure devotee. He came to this plane of existence for a few brief years, created a spiritual revolution, and now remains accessible for all, and especially when we are connected with him in parampara , through the process of initiation.

## Guru issues

## But did Srila Prabhupada want his disciples to become gurus after him? I hear different opinions.

Srila Prabhupada certainly spoke many times about the principle of continuing the chain of gurus on into the future. Every guru must ensure that the parampara continues after his departure, otherwise the living examples of his teachings may diminish after some years. Srila Prabhupada created something unique: an international community of Vaishnavas, with the standard teachings translated into dozens of languages. Such a vibrant community requires the dynamism of the guru-disciple relationship to perpetuate it and to preserve the Vaishnava practises.

In creating his ISKCON movement in 1966, Srila Prabhupada saw into the future, far beyond his own lifetime. He envisioned a world where there would be, as he told his followers, ‘ten million acaryas’ to teach Krishna’s instructions to a needy world. He wanted his disciples to have their own disciples, but he also wanted all of them to work collectively as a joint missionary organisation of international reach, and to do so with ‘love and trust’ for each other. In the early years of his mission he wrote as follows:

“I wish that in my absence all my disciples become the bona fide spiritual master to spread Krishna consciousness throughout the whole world.”

Letter to Madhusudana Das, 2 nd November 1967

“Our method is very simple. If one is fortunate enough to meet a bona fide spiritual master and if he acts strictly under his discipline, he also becomes within a very short time another spiritual master.”

Interview in Seattle, 24 th September 1968

“Regarding your question about the disciplic succession coming down from Arjuna, it is just like I have got my disciples, so in the future these many disciples may have many branches of disciplic succession.”

Letter from Los Angeles, 25 th January 1969

“Every one of us should become spiritual master because the world is in blazing fire... So all my students here who are feeling so much obliged...I am also obliged to them because they are helping me in this missionary work. At the same time, I shall request them all to become spiritual master. Every one of you should be spiritual master next.”

Vyasa Puja address, Hamburg, 5 th September 1969

In his strong desire to perpetuate the disciplic succession, Srila Prabhupada spoke clearly of his disciples forming the next generation of spiritual teachers. If they did, he said, it would make him happy:

“So, we have got this message from Krishna, from Caitanya Mahaprabhu, from the Six Goswamis, later on, Bhaktivinoda Thakura, Bhaktisiddhanta Thakura. And we are trying our bit also to distribute this knowledge. Now, tenth, eleventh, twelfth...my guru maharaja is tenth from Caitanya Mahaprabhu, I am eleventh, you are twelfth.”

"To become spiritual master is not very difficult thing. You'll have to become spiritual master. You, all my disciples, everyone should become spiritual master. It is not difficult...Keep trained up very rigidly and then you are bona fide guru, and you can accept disciples on the same principle. ## What about his ‘final order’? Did he choose his successors?

I think you are referring to the suggestion by some that there was a ‘final order’ that superseded all others in the matter of future initiations. This ‘order’ was that as the founder-acharya Srila Prabhupada would himself remain the sole initiating guru in perpetuity. There are, however, many discrepancies with this idea. Most importantly that there was no chronological development in his teaching on the guru, rather there was a general consistency in his instructions from 1966 when he began his movement right up to 1977 when he departed. As we’ve seen in his letters and speeches about future gurus, he did want his disciples to initiate. And all of them were to do it if possible. His only reserve was whether they were quite ready for the task.

When he was unwell in early 1977, he stopped initiating, explaining that it was not right, in his current condition, to continue. In July of that year he chose some of his senior disciples, who were already performing most of the elements of the initiation ceremonies, to begin deciding who of the new candidates to initiate, and in addition to choose their spiritual names. They would be ‘disciples of my disciples’ or ‘grand-disciples.’ Yet it was not that his directives on future initiations had been left until his final months. For a handful of senior disciples there had already been, one or two years previously, a direct verbal instruction for them to initiate in his physical absence. In October he said that he had now stopped initiating and in November he departed.

## But if he had empowered his disciples to initiate, wouldn’t they all have been successful?

No doubt you are speaking of the fact that the disciples who began initiating in 1978 were not all successful in the years that followed. Srila Prabhupada initiated 4,800 disciples, and even in his personal presence, many of them drifted from the path of bhakti. After his passing that phenomenon continued, and amongst that number were some of those chosen to continue the initiations. That gurus, the future hope of the movement, could fail in their spirituality caused despair for many, and there was widespread confusion as to how to proceed. But their eventual failures of leadership were due to many reasons and cannot be attributed to their disregard for a supposed ‘final order.’

## Why do I hear of two gurus: diksha and shiksha?

A diksha -guru is a master who gives a mantra to a student, thereby initiating him formally into the recitation of the sacred syllables. A shiksha -guru is traditionally one who gives instructions on pronunciation, grammar, phonetics, theology and techniques of the mantra. They are normally the same person, but under some circumstances may be two different gurus. Since diksha is a one-time only event, a person has only one diksha -guru. Shiksha , however, can come from different teachers, at different times, so a person may have several shiksha -gurus.

## I have heard that the shiksha -guru is more important than the diksha -guru – why is that?

From the 1600s there developed a custom of diksha being given without any of the customary and essential teachings. Disciples would not be taught any philosophy or be given ongoing guidance or support but asked to merely attend a ceremony. The guru would simply give the mantra and take some fee. This practise grew and resulted in many irresponsible gurus and many more under-educated, yet initiated people. Although a mantra has factual spiritual potency, for it to be activated the student must also adopt disciplines to purify the consciousness.

In addition, to sustain strong spiritual practise throughout life the disciple must also understand the techniques, obstacles, rewards, and the ultimate goal of mantra recitation. All this is included within the ‘relevant instructions’ or shiksha . For this reason, reforming acharyas began teaching that God’s grace was achieved much more by shiksha , rather than mere diksha . Of course, both are essential, but the mistakes of history had produced a disparity of the importance of diksha and shiksha gurus, and it needed to be rectified. Nevertheless, ancient tradition describes the guru as a mantra-giver, philosophy teacher and moral guide – all in one.

## Did Srila Prabhupada want his disciples to become shiksha or diksha gurus?

As mentioned before, the guru is usually the person who both gives the mantra (diksha ) and the explanatory teachings about the mantra (shiksha ). Most gurus play both roles. Only in 17th and 18th century India was there a stark separation between the two roles which led to a community of largely ineffective, yet official, diksha gurus. Srila Prabhupada’s guru taught that shiksha , or pure teaching, was the more important element, but insisted that the guru’s role was to do both. Srila Prabhupada performed both roles for his disciples, and it can be reasonably concluded that he also wanted his disciples to do both. In his general usage of the word ‘guru’ or ‘spiritual master,’ both in his talks and books, he customarily did not bring up the two terms as distinct types of gurus. His general explanation of a ‘regular guru’ was that he gave the disciple mantras and taught the theology and philosophy underpinning the mantras.

## How is it that some gurus do not stay spiritually strong?

The guru is also a disciple. In fact, he is commanded never to think of himself as a guru, despite being addressed as such by his followers. So, the real question becomes ‘how is it that some disciples do not stay spiritually strong?’ The answers are numerous, but the metaphor given is of the full moon, shining brightly, which becomes gradually darkened, night by night, almost imperceptibly slowly. Similarly, even a brilliant Vaishnava, with a face like the moon, can become eclipsed by aparadha , or offenses, chief of which is enmity and criticism of his fellow Vaishnavas. Certainly, when someone addressed as a guru forgets that he is a disciple, his position as a humble servant at the feet of his spiritual master will gradually be forgotten.

## Why are some gurus initiating in the presence of their spiritual master?

Traditionally, Srila Prabhupada told us, the disciple brings all prospective disciples to his own guru while he is alive. In his absence the disciple then becomes the guru and initiates. This is the etiquette. However, it can also be observed from Vaishnava history that when the guru orders his disciple to initiate due to sickness, old age or vast distances, the faithful disciple complies. In our modern setting, it has often been found that both geographical distance and language can be barriers to a disciple making a firm connection with a guru. So in ISKCON a few disciples have begun initiating while their gurus are still present.

## Why are there so many different opinions about what Srila Prabhupada wanted?

Each of us is an individual, with our own level of knowledge, and experience. With knowledge and experience comes realisation and understanding. But still, not everyone will come to the same conclusions. Added to this mix is the vast wealth of teaching given by the acharya , and perhaps his personal instructions to different disciples. Many of us hold one selection of teachings to be all-important, and others have their own choices. In an international organisation such as ISKCON, with members from a hundred countries, it is inevitable that there will be different opinions. What is remarkable is that ISKCON has remained a united mission despite so many varieties of understanding.

Since by this time, most of the initiation ceremonies conducted around the world were done by others on his behalf, it is true that those celebrants were actually ritviks . Whilst the strict meaning of the term ritvik would suggest that it was a temporary arrangement, there arose a suggestion that the term ritvik-acharya be used in perpetuity to describe the future gurus. While it is true that every person who performs the function of guru does so on behalf of Srila Prabhupada, it is also true that in his physical absence they cannot be properly referred to as ritvik-acharyas . ## Can’t Srila Prabhupada be my guru? After all, he is the acharya and therefore my main shiksha guru. Is that correct?

This was proposed back in the 1970s. A group of young men, very interested in yoga, declared to other devotees that ‘Srila Prabhupada is our guru.’ Yet none of them came forward to follow the rules and take formal initiation. When he heard about this the founder-acharya stated: “I thank them for their sentiment, but they are not my disciples.” Accepting a guru involves both the aspirations of the would-be disciple and the acceptance of the prospective guru. The disciple may regard himself a follower, but it must be reciprocated by the guru for initiation to take place. Since Srila Prabhupada’s physical presence is no longer available, his acceptance of a candidate is no longer possible, and therefore initiation by him is not available.

## But all his teachings are available, in books and recordings. What more is required?

This was also proposed to Srila Prabhupada. His reply was that the guru must also be ‘concrete,’ and that the ‘guru appears before the physical senses of the disciple.’ When someone suggested that the books of the spiritual master were non-different from the guru, he laughed and reached forward and grasped a disciple’s ear and pulled it: “But the book cannot do this!”

## Can’t I just surrender within my heart?

On a morning walk in Australia in 1975 this was also suggested. Since accepting a guru is a spiritual act, and since the guru is a representative of Krishna within the heart, can’t a guru be within the heart also? And initiation take place there? Srila Prabhupada replied: “You do not know where to find out Krishna in the heart.” Another person then asked:

“Srila Prabhupada is it possible for a person to return to the spiritual world without taking initiation from a bona fide spiritual master?

Srila Prabhupada replied: “No, that is not possible. Therefore Rupa Gosvami said, a dau gurv- asrayam . First business is to accept spiritual master. A dau gurv- asrayam . Who will train him? He is already fool, rascal. He must be trained up. So he must be trained up by the representative of Krishna.”

## But if Krishna is the ‘guru in the heart’ what is the need of any other guru?

The very next day in Australia, Srila Prabhupada kept the conversation on the subject going: “By the mercy of Krishna one comes in touch with the bona fide spiritual master, and by the mercy of spiritual master, one gets Krishna. So Krishna is in everyone's heart. Isvara? sarva -bhutanam (BG 18.61). Krishna can understand what we want. So when we sincerely want Krishna then Krishna sends His representative, guru. Guru is outside representative of Krishna. So to the sincere student, Krishna teaches from inside and outside. That is the way, so that he becomes quickly fit for going back to home, back to Godhead.”

## Qualifications of a disciple

## So now I know something of the guru’s qualifications – what about mine?

Well, the first qualification of an aspirant disciple is that you should know what the qualification of a guru is. Otherwise you can be cheated quite easily, not only by an incompetent guru, but by your own impulsive nature. For this reason, Srila Prabhupada spoke as follows: “But even before making inquiries (about a guru) we should have some qualification. If we go to a store to purchase some gold or jewellery and we know nothing about jewels or gold, we are likely to be cheated. If we go to a jeweller and say, “Can you give me a diamond?” he will understand that this is a fool. He could charge us any price for anything. That kind of searching will not do at all. We first have to become a little intelligent, for it is not possible to make spiritual progress otherwise.”

## Those are high qualifications, aren’t they?

They are the classical qualifications found in scripture, but a disciple is supposed to follow them even today. Adding together all Srila Prabhupada’s instructions we find that he said much the same. In summary he asked that a disciple should be inquisitive with the guru, should listen carefully when he speaks, should be obedient to the guru, strictly follow his guru’s instructions, have faith in him, be respectful and commit no offenses against him. ## What if I make mistakes?

They say that ‘To err is human.’ It’s natural to make mistakes in the beginning. When a toddler is learning to walk, she may take a few steps, wobble, and then fall. The parent doesn’t criticise her but helps her stand up again and take the next few steps. In a short time, with parental help and patience, the child has learned to walk. Similarly, in the beginning of spiritual life, we take a few steps forward and may wobble and fall. But Krishna is very patient, especially if we’re trying hard. Sometimes the guru is stern on the Lord’s behalf, but mostly he understands the difficulties the young disciple goes through and will always try to help with relevant and compassionate instructions.

## What if I change my mind about which guru I would like?

Better that you fully understand the criteria of accepting a guru and carefully make your choice in the beginning. Remember that there’s a one-year period of mutual scrutiny which commences after you’ve made your choice of guru, and that this period begins again if you change your mind. Your choice should not be made on an impetuous or whimsical basis because you may waste time. You’ll end up with the guru of your choice, it just may take extra time.

## What if I choose a guru, become initiated, and then change my mind?

That is not recommended. You should not change your mind but bring it under the control of your clear intelligence. The sacred texts inform us that once having accepted a guru, the guru should not be abandoned. The problem is that once you’ve accepted a guru, your mind does indeed rebel under the influence of the ego, which immediately seeks to protect you from all the transformation you now must do. You’ll need to resist that temptation. You cannot change gurus as often as you change your mind. Rather, the guru is there to change you. But don’t worry: the guru will give you permission to take teachings and guidance from several other inspiring individuals – provided you bring anything you don’t understand back to him for further clarification.

## Principles of spiritual life

## I’ve heard that devotees follow many rules after they become initiated. What’s the idea?

The general idea is that when you set yourself a goal, you give up or reduce some of your short-term pleasures to gain a higher enjoyment. For instance, when an Olympic athlete wants to go for a gold medal, he or she must abandon fast-food and wake up early for training. There’s no question of going to parties. The thought of achieving that medal puts all the other pleasures of life into perspective. With the goal in mind, each day can be moulded to physical fitness and training for the specific sport. Similarly, devotees of Krishna mould their life into one of spiritual training, where the idea of the soul’s awakening is the highest attainment. Their life is then regulated accordingly.

4\. No intoxication.

## What happens if you take on the vows and then find it difficult to follow them?

In order that your words become solemn vows, they can only be spoken publicly when you feel you have a realistic chance of keeping to them. That’s why you have one year to see whether you’re OK with them. Perhaps it’s much easier to make lifetime promises when you’re young and single – when you’re older you know that major changes in life such as marriage, building a career and becoming a parent inevitably alter the way you keep promises.

But here is the good news: by making vows you develop the determination to follow them, and by leading a spiritual life you develop the strength to overcome any future challenges. There may indeed be times when you’ll find it difficult to follow every vow you make, but if you fix your mind on your ultimate goal, arrange your life appropriately, and make sure you keep good company, you’ll find it much easier.

## Initiation

## What is initiation?

Initiation is the formal beginning of the relationship between guru and disciple. Known in Sanskrit as diksha , it is the time when the candidate takes refuge in the guru and receives a mantra together with instruction on its theology, techniques of practise, ritual and moral conduct. The guru will also teach the history of the mantra and its effects on the chanter.

## I’ve heard there are two initiations, first and second, could you explain what the difference is?

The stages and ceremonies of initiation are described in ancient worship manuals known as the Pancharatra . Full reformation is described as having five elements. These are known collectively as Pancha Samskara . In order they are as follows:

1\. Tapah – Voluntary discomfort for spiritual purification.

2\. Pundram – Clay markings given by the guru (tilak)

3\. Nama – Spiritual name ending in ‘Dasa’ or Dasi.’

4\. Mantra – Mantras whispered by the guru into the ear.

5\. Yaga – the process of deity worship is explained.

In ISKCON the first initiation involves the giving of the universal mantra and steps 1-3. The second initiation involves steps 4 and 5. So Pancha Samskara is completed in two stages, one or two years apart.

## What is the ceremony?

While the diksha ceremony may vary according to the community, the following elements are common: (1) Ritual sanctification of the place where the ceremony is to be conducted. (2) Inviting the Deity as a witness. (3) Worship with fruits, flowers, incense and lamps. (4) Sipping of water with purifying mantras. (5) Kindling of a sacred fire and offering grains into the flames. (6) Disciple making his symbolic refuge by lying prostrate at the feet of the guru. (7) Giving of the mantra by murmuring it into the ear of the disciple. (8) Guru blessing the disciple. (9) Presentation of gifts to the guru. (10) Disciple breaking his fast with a meal of the guru’s remnants.

## What happens at the ceremony in ISKCON?

For initiation in ISKCON the candidate is required to have demonstrated an initial taste for spiritual life by chanting, choosing to associate with other devotees, engaging in devotional service and giving up bad habits. At the ceremony of first initiation, the candidate must be freshly bathed and dressed in clean cloth. After sipping water, three strands of Tulasi neck-beads will be given to him. The guru gives a lecture, often on the ten offenses to the names of Krishna, a list taken from the Padma Purana .

The candidate is then called forward and offers pranama (respect) by bowing down to the guru. He or she is then asked to recite the ‘four regulative principles,’ four restrictions that are to be followed thereafter. Then the guru will give the disciple’s new name, a name that often begins with the first letter of the old name. After this the guru hands the disciple a string of wooden japa beads upon which he has chanted. A fire sacrifice follows, after which is kirtan. The new disciple offers the guru a small dakshina (donation) of money as a gift.

## What happens at a second initiation ceremony?

Second initiation follows at an unspecified interval, although Srila Prabhupada said that it was not universally necessary for all his disciples, and that discretion should be applied as to who should be given it: ‘Only those who are serious.’ Whereas the maha-mantra is public, the diksha mantras are secret, given by the guru in a private place. Beginning with Srila Prabhupada and ISKCON, female disciples have also been given the Vedic Gayatri mantras, although the sacred thread is reserved for males.

## Does the guru take my karma at initiation?

You mean karma-phala , the ‘fruit of action.’ Every action has a reaction and the result of bad action, or ‘sin’ is an accumulation of latent material desire that will again cause us to stray from the spiritual path. Sin is an old English word, an archery term to indicate our missing the target. When we’ve missed the target of human life, we’ve sinned. Krishna is the remover of all sinful reaction, and He says as much in the famous verse from the Bhagavad-gita: “I will deliver you from all sinful reaction.” (18.66). So the Lord is the cause and the guru is the instrument. Nevertheless, Srila Prabhupada said to one disciple: ‘The guru takes the karma, but Krishna protects the guru.’ After initiation the disciple must commit no more sinful activities.

## I feel anxious that the guru is taking on such a responsibility for me.

Hopefully the responsibilities will be balanced. You are both taking on responsibilities. You are taking on a guru for life, and the guru is taking you on as a disciple for life. Just as he is taking on a responsibility so are you. It is not that after initiation he will do all the responsible, hard work of teaching and guiding, and you will relax. You’ll have to make an effort too, and you’ll have to come up with determination and perseverance. But since nothing in life comes without an effort, your mutual efforts as guru and disciple will pay off in the form of progress. Meanwhile, don’t be anxious. Your future is brilliant.

## Is this my illusion, of not really understanding the status of a guru?

The status of a guru is precisely the status you give him. Nothing more or less. He has no independent status. Whether hundreds of others adore him should not concern you. The guru is not interested in gaining a sense of status by counting how many followers he has, and it’s best for you if you don’t do that either. The only status he has is if you accept him as Krishna’s representative in this world. You’ll accept him as that if he brings you closer to Krishna by his teaching and personal example. When you voluntarily accept him as your guru, you give him status by having chosen him. Illusion for a disciple is if he forgets what the guru has done for him and becomes guru-druha , ungrateful or neglectful of the guru.

## How, if at all, does life change after initiation? Can I prepare for this?

Life before initiation is characterised by hope, ambition, striving – and the expectancy that you’ll have a different life afterwards. After initiation you’ll be inspired, and you’ll learn more; your confidence will grow along with your skills in practising bhakti-yoga. However, as time passes, it is sometimes seen that even the most enthusiastic of initiates can gradually lose their former dynamism and become mechanical practitioners. To preserve spiritual vitality, we need to be making a genuine spiritual connection in our meditation, study, prayer and service. The great saint Bhaktivinode Thakur said that he examined his conscience every two weeks, on the Holy Day of Ekadashi, to see how he had improved.

His understanding was that his spiritual advancement should be noticeable. Spiritual life is not a competition, and we should never compare ourselves to others, but we can be true to ourselves, and strive to improve on our behaviour of the previous month. A good spiritual director or guide, supportive friends, and inspirational and regular community kirtans and classes are all essential.

## How is my new name chosen?

There are several traditions in a guru choosing the new name of the disciple. The first is that he chooses a name whose first letter or syllable is the same as the first letters of the disciple’s existing name. Thus, Peter may become Peetambara, a name of Krishna, and Anna may become Anapayini, a name of Lakshmi. Then, since many disciples with Indian heritage may already have Hindu names, the guru may augment their existing name by adding a few extra syllables to make it more specifically dedicated to Krishna. Ravi might become Ravi Lochana or Shambhu might become Shyama Sundara.

The third tradition is to not make any of these amendments, in which case someone named John could easily become Vishnu. All names are then suffixed with ‘Dasa’ or ‘Dasi’ meaning ‘servant.’ The guru is meant to choose standard names which appear in the sacred texts, names that will inspire us and those who speak to us.

## Do I have to change my name?

That is the tradition mentioned in the Padma Purana . Srila Prabhupada writes:

“After initiation, the disciple’s name must be changed to indicate that he is a servant of Lord Vishnu. The disciple should also immediately begin marking his body with tilaka (urdhva-pu??ra ), especially his forehead. These are spiritual marks, symptoms of a perfect Vai??ava. If a person does not change his name after initiation, it is to be understood that he will continue in his bodily conception of life.”

From this, it seems that certain external changes are indeed important. In practise, most initiates seem happy receiving a name to indicate their new beginning.

## Will initiation affect my relationship with my husband or family?

In life, nothing ever remains the same. Everything and everyone are in a constant state of flux. Relationships are always affected by these changes, and when one person in a relationship, or one member of a family, chooses to take spiritual life seriously some things will inevitably change. Spiritual life is not about challenging relationships or families, rather it can vastly improve them. Life in Krishna consciousness is all about discovering happiness through divine service, and that can greatly increase the happiness within a relationship or a family. Happiness is contagious, after all, and everyone loves a person who laughs easily.

The difficulty seems to lie when the changes involved in becoming a devotee of Krishna have not been discussed with others. For instance, devotees choose to be vegetarian, and this may not sit well with other family members. That can create tension at mealtimes if not discussed well beforehand. Parties and celebrations can also be difficult if other members of the family drink alcohol and expect the devotee to join in the fun. Everything can be negotiated though, and a family should be able to talk about how the changes will affect them all. That conversation comes before, not after, becoming a devotee.

## How do you explain to friends about the changes that will happen when you're initiated, but that it’s nothing to be frightened of?

You ask as if you’ll be breaking bad news or telling them you’re just about to undergo major surgery! Most genuine friends these days do not flinch if you tell them you’re taking up meditation or yoga, or that you’re going on a meat-free diet. Years ago, it might have been a bit of a shock, but not now. If they’re genuine friends they’ll stay with you despite your changes. But still, you should make it easy on them. A word of advice is to let them in on your new life choices earlier rather than later, and gradually rather than all at once. As with families, the difficulties come when your ideas of having fun are now different, and you’re no longer as interested in the night life as you once were.

Sometimes this may mean that you and a few old friends will drift apart, and that can hurt, but if you can make new friends it will soften the blow.

## Practicalities...should wife and husband take initiation together?

That would be ideal, but it very rarely happens. Spiritual life is a personal journey and it’s not very often that it happens to two people at the same time. Most of us seem to make the choice to get serious about spiritual matters and then our partners, parents, siblings and other relatives are all left wondering why we’ve changed.

But it does sometimes happen that a husband and wife become interested in bhakti at around the same time – perhaps within two or three years of each other. What happens then is that the most interested partner’s enthusiasm gently influences the other, and if they’re both very patient with each other it may be that they’re ready for initiation at the same time. However, there are many happy couples where one partner is a devotee and the other is not.

## Should they have the same guru if possible?

Sometimes it can contribute to family harmony if both husband and wife have the same guru. They’ll both have the same focus and influence, and a mutual understanding of how to carry out their spiritual lives together. And there have been disagreements between a husband and wife when each has a different guru who says things slightly differently. So for an extra insurance for domestic bliss, it might be said that the same guru is an advantage.

Having said that, there are many couples who have individually chosen gurus who inspire them, and they use the differences between the gurus to develop each other’s strengths – thereby enhancing family harmony in a refreshing way. Where there’s already peace and love in a relationship, two vastly different gurus will be no problem; where there’s already tension in the relationship, even having the same guru may not help the couple.

## What rules do I have to follow regarding prasadam?

There are just five rules:

(1) Everything you eat should be shuddha , or pure. No foods from impure or dubious origins, and no foods that are too rajasic or tamasic . For this reason, vaishnavas don’t eat eggs. Onions and garlic are also off the menu because they destroy the ability to meditate with focus. No wine is to be used in preparing food. Many devotees will not eat foods prepared in a factory, preferring instead to only home-cook.

(2) All foods must be ahimsa , or non-violent in their origin and preparation, so a Vaishnava diet does not include meat, poultry or fish, or anything derived from these.

(3) Everything must be first offered to Sri Krishna. While shopping and preparing food, the ingredients are known as bhoga , when the offering is made to Krishna it is known as naivedya, and after the plate is removed from the altar it is known as prasada or ‘mercy.’ Care must be taken that nothing is offered twice. Purity also demands that the food is not tasted or smelled during cooking, and the mind of the cook is focused on Krishna.

(4) On the holy day of ekadashi , the 11 th days of both the waxing an waning moon, devotees eat no grains, beans, peas or anything made with their products such as bread, chapatti, breakfast cereals, pastries or biscuits.

(5) Some days on the Vaishnava calendar are designated as ‘fasting’ days. On these days the devotees take no food at all, until the designated time such as sunset, twilight or moonrise. Krishna’s birthday, known as Janmashtami, is a full day fast until midnight. Everyone over the age of 12 is meant to follow these fasting days.

## What is the relationship between a guru and a disciple?

## What should I expect from my guru?

The minimum you should expect from your guru is that he will be friendly, affectionate, accessible and concerned for your welfare. A guru is a teacher, both by word and action, so you should expect him to be interested in how you’re learning and in your successes and challenges in spiritual practise. He would make himself available to you, see that you are learning the standard scriptures and understanding their deeper meanings, ask about your spiritual practise generally, and see that you are supported in your spiritual life by a strong circle of peers, wherever you live. When you write a letter to him, he should normally respond within two weeks with relevant advice suitable to your personal situation. At least once or twice a year, if not more, the guru would be expected to personally visit your country, if not the region where you live.

## What shouldn’t I expect from my guru?

What you should not expect is for the guru to be keenly interested in every aspect of your family life, or every event of your day. He might be, of course, but you should not expect it. The guru is neither a psychologist nor a therapist, a doctor, financial consultant, or a marriage and relationships expert. He is not a careers guidance officer. He may give some general advice on the importance of having a peaceful family life, staying healthy, or in always spending money within your means, but do not expect him to be a counsellor on all subjects. You should find others for that.

## What would a guru expect of me?

A guru would expect you to be a good student, which means a good listener, a good questioner, and enthusiastic to understand the subject at hand. He’d expect to hear from you (once a month is not too much) and to reply to his letters. Naturally, if you’ve formally promised the guru to follow the regulative principles of Vaishnava life, to study, and to chant sixteen rounds of maha-mantra japa daily, he’d reasonably expect you to meet that vow. If there were any challenges to that ongoing commitment, he’d expect to hear from you and to discuss any obstacles you might be facing. He would also want to hear from you before you made major changes in your life such as moving home, changing jobs, or if you went through a period of sickness.

## What do I have to do for my guru? I've heard I have to cook and take care of his laundry?

Well, this type of thing falls into the category of personal service, and it is normally reserved for those who accompany the guru when he travels, or those younger disciples who live in his ashram or monastery with him. If the guru visits your country once or twice a year, and there are a handful of other disciples living there, you might be asked to do the guru’s laundry once every two years. Not a burden, I think! Generally speaking, though, the disciples compete for service rather than seeing it as a chore, as this is one way of pleasing the guru who functions as the representative of Srila Prabhupada.

## What does it mean to ‘follow the guru’s instructions?’

A guru gives two kinds of instructions: upanyasa (spiritual discourses) and upadesh (pertinent instructions). The first is meant for all who wish to listen, the second only for the individual student. Typically, the guru is expert at giving timely instructions for the disciple’s next step in spiritual life. They will be for the disciple, not for others, and will also include any encouragement and clarification required. The guru neither flatters the disciple, nor gives him instructions that are beyond his grasp. Occasionally the guru instructs the disciple to undertake a task of practical service, such as a new project, but again, he will supply all relevant support.

## Do you have examples of the kind of instructions a guru may give a disciple?

This would be very individual to the person. For instance, the guru might ask one disciple to ‘chant sixteen rounds of japa every day,’ and ask another to ‘learn how to cook for Krishna.’ To another he might say ‘please write articles about the philosophy,’ and to another, ‘please carefully study the Bhagavad-gita.’ To still another, ‘Go and start a magazine,’ and to another, ‘Build a temple of Krishna in your town.’ Srila Prabhupada gave many instructions to his 5,000 disciples, and together they shaped his worldwide mission in just twelve years.

## I've heard that you are meant to do everything your guru says. Is that true?

Gurus in general do not make unnecessary or improper demands. Neither will they push their disciples to do something which is not in keeping with their moral or spiritual development, or to do anything which would threaten their education, career or family life. A guru always speaks and acts in accordance with the teachings and personal example of his own guru, so if Srila Prabhupada did not ask for it, then the guru won’t. Sometimes, because the guru’s job is to challenge our ego and elevate us beyond our self-imposed limitations, he may cajole us out of our comfort-zone in order to help us see ourselves and our capabilities in a new light. Srila Prabhupada did this many times with his disciples; ordering them to go and open a centre in a new country or produce a book. The disciple might have had no previous experience of living in the country or of book publication, but by the guru’s order and through Krishna’s grace, the disciple rose to the task and a new horizon was crossed.

But after time, if the guru has proven to be irretrievably entangled in sensual enjoyment or philosophical deviance, the disciple can legitimately abandon the relationship, always remaining thankful of what good he gained while in it.

## When you've made a commitment to a guru, what happens if your needs, or their needs, change?

With any other kind of teacher or therapist, you can relinquish your relationship if your needs change. For instance, no-one is expected to keep the same schoolteacher or dentist throughout their life. With a guru, there’s an expectation that your basic need for guidance in spiritual life will not change, so your guru remains your guru. And normally, the guru will never give up on the disciple. That may sound daunting, but the relationship is a happy one, and you shouldn’t think that you’ll get ‘stuck,’ unable to move on in your life. In practice, there’s a great deal of flexibility, and your guru, sensing you need a change, will either suggest one of his peers, or endorse your selection of a guide. The only rule is that you bring the teachings you’ve heard from the new guide back to your guru for approval.

## Previously you told me there are no ‘bad gurus,’ now you tell me that sometimes the guru does turn bad. Which is it?

Sometimes a good person temporarily becomes ensnared by the material energy. It does happen. For this reason, Krishna states that ultimately His devotee returns to him and never perishes. However, our tradition does describe that there are others, posing as gurus, who are not qualified but who may appear to be so to the untrained eye. Their cursory knowledge of scripture, their wit or charisma, and perhaps the number of followers, all persuade us they are worthy of our trust. Such a confusing person is described in the Tattva Sagara :

"A perso n who eats vo raciously, wears opulent clo thing, is greedy after sense pleasures, loves to argue, is a fo llow er of the atheist philo so phers, is wicked, speaks what should not be spoken, has many vices , and although fi nancially well situated, is greedy fo r mor e and bigger donations, is the kind of spiritual master that destroys the goo d fo rtune of his disciples. ## Well, I had a guru once before. He was a bad guru. I’m not sure if I’m ready to trust anyone again.

I can quite understand. You were justifiably disappointed. But it would be wrong to assume, even in the face of so many ‘bogus gurus,’ that there is no-one genuine who can be trusted. And it would be completely incorrect for you to draw the conclusion that a much safer path is to not have a guru at all. In India there is a saying:

“Once, you burned your mouth on a cup of hot milk.

Now, when you hold a cup of cold yogurt you blow on it.”

Your previous experience has taught you an important lesson, but it would be a great pity to regard all spiritual figures as charlatans.

## I agree, but I don’t want to get hurt again. I’d rather not be anyone’s disciple. A friend, maybe.

Your hesitancy is based on the common knowledge – and your painful experience - that not all teachers are what they say they are. Over the past fifty years in the western world there has been a steadily growing backlash against charlatan spiritual teachers, abusive priests and mischievous ‘masters.’

We know that there is a body of sacred wisdom that can help us, but the flimsy representatives of that wisdom are seldom truly wise and sometimes downright pernicious. We recognise that we need spiritual guidance but also that self-proclaimed teachers may disappoint us. The response is that we keep our guard up even as we try to surrender. Like going skydiving but holding on to the edge of the aeroplane door at the same time, we become stuck between adventure and safety.

And that’s the difficulty. The full effect of having a guru is only experienced by those who place themselves as disciples. Disciple infers discipline, and that’s a relationship different from being a student, pupil or friend. You can relate to a guru as a friend or guide, but you can get so much more from him if you relate to him as a disciple.

## How do I know when I’m ready?

Once you’ve satisfied yourself as to the qualifications of the guru, you should think: “Am I ready to learn from him? Am I ready to be guided and, if necessary, corrected? Do I feel that this person is someone who can occupy the place of guru in my life? Is this a relationship that I can maintain for life? Am I convinced of the philosophy, theology, morals and ethics such that I can follow them for life? If I have lingering misconceptions or deep-seated attachments, am I prepared to have them challenged? Do I have any lingering doubts that need attention and am I prepared to expose them?”

## What else should I consider?

Well, you could also change places with the guru for a few moments. Instead of thinking whether the guru will meet your expectations, and subjecting the guru to your exacting scrutiny, you can instead think of yourself from the guru’s own perspective: “What about me? Am I good enough to become his disciple? Will I be able to do justice to the teaching and help that my guru will offer me? Will I be a disappointment to him or a cause for his unhappiness? Will I be a credit to him and assist him in his service to his guru?”

## How do I know if I’d be a good disciple?

You don’t become a disciple until it feels right for you. If you’re able to function in the guru-disciple relationship for an entire year before becoming a disciple, you’re probably going to be OK afterwards. Some things may change for you as the years go by, but it will usually be something good for you, something that will help you to grow spiritually. Otherwise you will be pretty much the same sort of person throughout your life, just a more awakened version. So if you’ve already come to that tipping-point in your life where you know that spiritual life is for you, you like what you’ve heard already, you’re enjoying spiritual practice, you should have no doubts that you’ll be a good disciple.

## What do I do if I find some things my guru says too ‘black and white’ for me to accept?

Sometimes your guru will say things that you consider too ‘traditional.’ Perhaps he quotes from scriptures in a way that you find ‘literal’ or not sufficiently adjusted for contemporary life. In such circumstances you may have to adjust how you understand him. If it is during a public talk, he may be speaking to a certain section of the audience, and not necessarily to you. If speaking to you directly, he may be asking you to consider a particular point.

Sometimes the very point that concerns you is the one you’re meant to examine further. All of us have material attachments, so occasionally the guru speaks to those attachments, and that can be uncomfortable. But it doesn’t mean he’s wrong. On other occasions a guru may be found at fault for being ‘too liberal,’ or not sufficiently endorsing of traditional culture. That will also have to be understood in context.

## Spiritual practise

## I have heard of sadhana , what does it mean?

Literally, the word sadhana is translated as ‘the means to reach the goal.’ The first objective in making any kind of progress is to establish your goal; the second is to focus your determination to reach it; and the third is to identify and then begin the incremental steps by which the goal can be reached. This third objective is known as sadhana .

Sadhana is made up of two elements: the positive actions you do as muc h as you can, an d the negative and obstructive actions y ou try to avoid. Both the positive, goal-affirming actions and the negative, goalrestricting actions are identified and a rule for life created from them. Avoidance of the negative is known as yama , and the positive is known as niyama . These two go hand in hand just as dietary restricti o ns go very well wi th medicine fo r a sick perso n. If we accept t he prim ary truth – that we are infected wi th the spiritual disease of m aya – then we’ll resign ourselves to get well soon.

## Is there a specific time for sadhana, or do people do it all day?

When you take a medicine your prescription normally directs you to take it regularly throughout the day. The same goes for sadhana – it is an all-hours regimen. The main medicine is the recitation of the Hare Krishna maha-mantra and that can be done at any time of day, and in all situations. But because our taste for doing it later in the day is greatly affected by what we do in the first few hours of the day, it is best to chant japa during the first two hours after waking up. Typically, a practising Vaishnava will rise from sleep, shower, and immediately begin the day’s rounds of the maha-mantra. Since this is a golden time for meditation, particularly if it is before sunrise, there is to be no distraction of any kind during this time.
