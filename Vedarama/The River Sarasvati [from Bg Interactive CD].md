# The River Sarasvati \[from Bg Interactive CD\]

## The River Sarasvati \[from Bg Interactive CD\]

Satallite technology has contributed to a major discovery about India's past. It has established the existence of the bed of the dried-up Sarasvati River.

Old Vedic texts mention an extraordinary wide river named Sarasvati that originated in the Himalayas and flowed to the Arabian Sea. Vedic sages convened metaphysical dialogues on its banks and visited places of pilgrimage along its course. Where is that river, mentioned so often in the Puranas, in the Mahabharata and in the Rg-Veda (fifty references in forty-six hymns)? We don't see it today.

Scanners used from space have become a tool for archeology, a trend that started after 1972, when NASA, the American government's space program, launched Landsat satellites to study agricultural areas. (Because vegetation reflects infrared rays, scientists could estimate crop yields.) By the late 1970's geologists, geographers and archaelogists had all followed suit. A few researchers employed Landsat imagery to pinpoint the location of Mayan ruins in the Yucatan jungles and prehistoric structures in former Mesopotamia. With space images, scientists detected two generations of the Great Wall of China.

In 1981, the space shuttle Columbia aimed an imaging system at the earth's major deserts. The images revealed ancient riverbeds in the Sahara, buried three to five meters below the sands.

In 1986, the French introduced a breakthrough in satellite remote sensing. Their National Center for Space Study designed a new technology, named SPOT — though not offering the fine detail of aerial photography — covered much wider areas.

Now an Indian and French archaelogical team on the ground, coordinating its work with a French SPOT satellite, has ascertained that the Sarasvati River described in the Vedas is fact, not myth. Vividly exposing the signatures of old rivers and their branches, data from SPOT shows that the Sarasvati existed long before 3000 B.C. and it evidently dried up completely around 2000 B.C., at the latest.

The satellite sensors and pointable optics photographed the dried bed of a river seven kilometers wide in the region of India west of New Delhi. In Punjab, the bed of this river, located where we would expect the Sarasvati, was twenty kilometers wide, according to the satellite imagery.

From Space, researchers detected that the Sarasvati had had many tributaries, which had watered an immense area of fertile soil. Traces of artificial canals were also discovered.

After India's first archaelogical breakthrough at Harappa in 1921, the findings there were accepted as evidence for what was called the Harappan culture. Then, in the same decade, another major site surfaced, at Mohenjodaro. The two cities, lying along the Indus River, were renamed "the Indus civilization."

Since the 1950s, more major sites have been located. But the distinctive feature of this new breed — Lothal, Rupar, Dholavira and Banawali — is that they all lie on or near the banks of the newly discovered river Sarasvati.

In 1992, Rafiq Mughal, a Pakistani archaelogist, found a site on the dried riverbed of the Sarasvati at Guneriwala that was apparently as large as Mohenjo-daro.

The discovery of the original 1,600 kilometer-long Sarasvati has cast in a new light the approximately one hundred excavated cities and towns of the so-called Indus culture. For a much larger concentration of sites — more than one thousand — has been detected along the old Sarasvati riverbed, buried under the sands of the Thar Desert.

The archaelogical progress in northern India so far has prompted many scholars to rename the Indus Valley phenomenon the Sarasvati-Indus civilization.

Some scholars object. They downplay the importance of the Sarasvati discovery, saying that the artifacts retrieved at the Sarasvati sites do not yet compare in significance to those of the Harrapan sites.

But those abreast with Vedic scholarship know that the cultural milieu described in the Vedic literature covered a far greater geographical area than archaelogists originally thought.

Running from the Himalayas to the Arabian Sea, the full course of the rediscovered Sarasvati roughly parallels the river Indus.

This remarkable find was intuited in the latter half of the nineteenth century by the Sanskritist Max Mueller. In *Sacred Books of the East* (xxxii. 60), he recorded this observation: "Though it may not be possible to determine, by geological evidence, the time of the changes which modified the southern areas of the Punjab and caused the Sarasvati to disappear in the desert, still the fact remains that the loss of the Sarasvati is later than the Vedic age, and that, at that time, the waters of the Sarasvati reached the sea."

Current technology can ascertain when geological changes forced the Sarasvati to disappear. Still, time is needed to fine tune the date; satellites along cannot clinch the geochronology. High-tech work on the ground is needed to confirm the findings.

Many of the sites discovered on the Sarasvati allow for radiocarbon dating. So far, sites near the river's mouth give approximate dates of 3000 B.C. Further upstream, towards the Himalayas, the dates of the sites become more recent, ranging to 1300 B.C.

The Indo-French satellite-and-field study revealed that parts of the river began to dry up as early as 3000 B.C. The complete disappearance of the Sarasvati is dated no later than 2000 B.C. Other studies date the complete disappearance anywhere from 2500 to 1700 B.C.

The archaelogist Rafiq Mughal says the river flowed as a perennial in the fourth and early third millennium B.C., and then disappeared at the end of the second millennium.

Significantly, many archaelogical sites are found directly on the old riverbed. All researchers agree this indicates that parts of the river must have dried up long before the people constructed dwellings there.

The proto-Harrapan sites found on the riverbed have been dated back to 3000 B.C. Paul-Henri Francfort, leading the French team, said in the journal *Eastern Anthropologist* (1992) that at the time of these first Harrapan sites on the riverbed itself, "no large perennial river had flowed there for a long time."

Vedic literature describes the flow of the ocean-bound perennial river. Lovers of the epic *Mahabharata,* which includes the *Bhagavad-gita,* will recall passages describing Sri Balarama's pilgrimage-tour inland, along the Sarasvati. For example: "The procession moved swiftly to the pilgrimage sites at the time of the war of the Kurus; going upstream along the Sarasvati, from the ocean onwards." (MB 9.34.15-18). Then we have the stanza in which Sri Balarama's upstream tour reaches the river's source (MB 9.53.11). Other Vedic histories, the  *Puranas,* also echo this geography.

The Vedic texts obviously describe the geography of India as it existed in 3000 B.C. or earlier, when the Sarasvati emanated from the Himalayas and flowed into the Arabian Sea. This means the Vedic people and culture coexisted with the mysterious Indus Valley tribes. Thus we can sagely contemplate that in some form — oral or otherwise — the Vedas did exist around 3000 B.C. And we can be even more confident that Vedic culture, with its religion and philosophy, is the oldest living culture in the world.
