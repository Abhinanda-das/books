**Prabhupāda’s Commentary on Rape**

In the purport to Śrīmad Bhāgavatam [4.25.41](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sb-4/25.41), Prabhupāda makes the following remarks about rape that have led to much controversy: In this regard, the word vikhyātam is very significant. A man is always famous for his aggression toward a beautiful woman, and such aggression is sometimes considered rape. Although rape is not legally allowed, it is a fact that a woman likes a man who is very expert at rape.

Much has been written about this commentary since rape is a word seeped in negative emotion. Women who have been raped are scarred for life by that dehumanizing and disempowering experience. And women who have not experienced it fear it. So even a vague suggestion that a woman might like a man who would rape is reprehensible to them. There are thus valid reasons to explain the comments.

However, in another sense, the attacks and defenses seem wholly superfluous because Prabhupāda explicitly forbade illicit sex, defining it as sex outside marriage meant for any purpose other than the procreation of good children. He repeatedly spoke about Bhagavad-Gita [7.11](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/7.11), where Lord Kṛṣṇa says dharmāviruddho bhūteṣu kāmo ’smi bharatarṣabha, which means, “I am lust (or sex) that is not opposed to dharma (or religious principles)”. In his organization—ISKCON—those proven to indulge in illicit sex (with or without consent) have been stripped of their position of authority. Gurus found to have been involved in sexual relationships outside marriage (consensual or non-consensual) have been deposed. Even those in a high position secretly watching porn have been stripped of their status. If there are exceptions to these rules, they are not because Prabhupāda in any way endorsed such behaviors.

And yet, those accusing Prabhupāda of endorsing rape ignore the high moral standards that he set for everyone. The rational and natural conclusion that one is led to—if we take all the facts into account—is that he meant something different when he used the word “rape” and certainly not the emotionally charged criminal act of rape—i.e., unwanted violence on a woman—being enjoyed by that woman. This article is devoted to discussing such a meaning.

**Alternative Understanding of the Word**

To summarize the main point quickly, a man’s sexual aggression toward a woman is not always unwanted. A woman wants aggression from a man who she is attracted to. She dislikes aggression from all other men. To determine whether aggression from a man is rape, we have to consider the woman’s mental state—if she wanted aggression from a man then his aggression is not rape, but if she did not want aggression from a man then his aggression is rape.

[4.25.41](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sb-4/25.41) and [4.25.42](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sb-4/25.42) reveal that the woman in the story is attracted to the man and praises his aggression toward her (displayed by proactively approaching her): O my dear hero, who in this world will not accept a husband like you? You are so famous, so magnanimous, so beautiful and so easily gotten. O mighty-armed, who in this world will not be attracted by your arms, which are just like the bodies of serpents? Actually, you relieve the distress of husbandless women like us by your attractive smile and your aggressive mercy. We think that you are traveling on the surface of the earth just to benefit us only.

The woman in question has been propositioned by the man who is attracted to her. The woman is also attracted to the man in return. They are talking to each other as prospective husband and wife. The woman is admiring the man’s physique, fame, and magnanimity. The woman is talking about her distress living without a husband, and how this distress is being relieved by the man’s attractive smile and aggressive mercy (ghṛṇā-uddhata). It is not unwanted aggression. It is aggressive mercy. It is what the woman wants and she considers the man doing what she wants a merciful act.

To decontextualize the word rape from its true context of wanted aggression and contextualize it against the imagery of unwanted aggression is the factual inversion of the intended meaning. It could happen only if persons commenting on the word rape have either not read the text or have malicious intent. Taking a word in isolation separate from the context or contextualizing it differently is not a sign of sincere people. It rather indicates a mind preoccupied with the imagery of sexual violence. It is this mental state, rather than what the book states, that lies at the root of all misunderstanding.

**The Problem of Sanskrit Translations**

Most English speakers don’t realize that non-native English speakers think in their native language and translate it into English. Most English-speaking people know one language—English. They have not dealt with the problem of thinking in one language and translating it into another. They don’t understand how translations from one language to another are poor if there isn’t cultural overlap between the speakers of two languages. Such is the fate of all Sanskrit-to-English translations that don’t share culture.

Some time ago, I wrote about the problem of Sanskrit to English translations, using examples of prakṛti, graha, kāraṇa, dharma, and śraddhā, which are translated into English as “nature”, “planet”, “cause”, “religion”, and “faith”, showing how each of these translations is so deeply flawed that confusions about the meaning become absolutely inevitable. Prakṛti is not “nature” because nature excludes the senses, mind, intellect, ego, and morality, which prakṛti includes. A graha is a house in which the living entity is trapped and not a “planet” from which he can easily escape. A kāraṇa is not a “cause” because causation in modern science means forces; love can’t be a cause in science; kāraṇa means a choice that acts upon a karaṇa or passive instrument that is activated by the choice; love is a choice and hence it can be a kāraṇa. Dharma isn’t “religion” because dharma is different for different classes, genders, and ages, while religion is common across these differences. The word śraddhā doesn’t mean “faith”. It means respect. The final rite performed by a son for his father is called śrāddha, for example.

Somewhat recently, I wrote about the problem of Sanskrit to English translations, using the example of manas, buddhi, and ahaṃkāra, which are translated as “mind”, “intellect”, and “ego”, showing how each of these translations is so deeply flawed that using them makes confusion inescapable and inevitable.

For example, there are five traits of intelligence (correct apprehension, incorrect apprehension, memory, sleep, and doubt), of which doubt is most fundamental. Every part of our sensual and mental apparatus is driven by absence. Something is missing. When that absence is experienced, a presence is created from the absence, which unites with the absence and destroys it. This is the basic model of change.

Intellectual change comes through the agency of doubt. An idea is generated from the doubt, unites with it, and destroys it. If we keep doubting bad ideas, we will eventually come to the good ideas, that will unite with the doubt, destroy it, and never produce new doubts. A person has to doubt to be called intelligent. Then they have to carefully destroy that self-doubt to establish certainty. This process is intelligence. Women doubt themselves much less than men, and they want to be with doubt-free men. Since women rarely have doubts, and doubt is the basis of buddhi, hence, women are less intelligent if buddhi is equated to intelligence. This, of course, offends people who don’t know what buddhi is.

When bhūmī, apah, anala, vāyu, and khaṃ are translated as “earth”, “water”, “fire”, “air” and “ether”, people think of solids, liquids, plasma, gases, and space, rather than smell, taste, sight, touch, and sound. All Sanskrit-to-English dictionaries are deeply flawed. If we can do that, then all problems can be easily resolved. Without it, none of the problems will be addressed.

Since there are two distinct meanings of the same word, one of which is accepted in English while the other is rejected, the problem is created by the English translation.

**Meaning of Har, Hari, Harā, and Haraṇ**

Let’s suppose that a father brings a gift for his son, and tells the son that he has a gift for him, but hides it behind his back, and wants the son to grab it from him. As the son tries to take the gift from behind the father’s back, the father raises his hands and puts the gift out of the son’s reach. The son jumps to grab the gift, but the father again moves his hand away. This game goes on for a while, to increase the son’s excitement and anticipation, after which the father relents and voluntarily gives the gift to the son. Even as the father tries to keep the gift out of the son’s reach, he actually intends to give it to him. Even as the son appears to fight with the father for the gift, he is assured that he will eventually receive the gift.

This paradoxical concept is Har. It is poorly translated as “stealing” because the translation implies immorality, forced use of will, and absence of the desire to give what is being forcibly taken. The embodiment of the pleasure that takes forcibly is Hari. The embodiment of the pleasure that wants to be taken forcibly although resists being taken in this way is Harā. The interaction between Hari and Harā is that of (a) wanting to take by force, (b) resisting being taken by force although wanting it, and (c) ultimately yielding to the forced taking. The process of wanting, resisting, and yielding is Haraṇ. Of these three, resisting and yielding are visible to the eyes but the wanting is not. Therefore, if we restrict ourselves to the naked vision, then Haraṇ will look like a resistance followed by defeat. It will then seem immoral. The mental state is the crucial distinction between an immoral and divine experience.

This divine process of “stealing” what we want to be “stolen” is seen repeatedly in Kṛṣṇa’s pastimes. The elder gopis want their butter to be stolen but hang it high on the roof, so that Kṛṣṇa and His friends will have to struggle to get it. The younger gopis want Kṛṣṇa’s romantic attention but pretend that they are disinterested, and Kṛṣṇa steals their clothes and makes them come naked before Him. Kṛṣṇa then plays the flute in the night to invite the gopis to dance but upon their arrival lectures them about chastity.

**The Divine Masculine and Feminine**

The divine reality is non-dualistic. It means that everything comes with its opposites. Men are protectors but they like being aggressive. Women want protection from men, but also aggression from them.

This divine non-dualistic reality is understood through the interplay between Puruṣa and Śakti. The Puruṣa is will and the Śakti is power. Their sexual union is the union of will and power. Through this union, no will is left unfulfilled and no power is left unutilized. The will is sometimes aggressive and sometimes passive—it is aggressive when it demands something from power and passive when it accepts the supply of power. The power is sometimes aggressive and sometimes passive—it is aggressive when it demands to be used by the will and passive when it fulfills the demands of the will.

The Supreme Truth is the combination of Puruṣa and Śakti and exists in a non-separated primordial state when the will and power are inactive. The activation of will and power is the separation of Puruṣa and Śakti. A will with power can do everything for itself. But it is more pleasing if some other power fulfills the will. A power with will can do everything itself. But it is more pleasing if the power is used to fulfill some other will. Puruṣa and Śakti separate to enjoy—the will and power are separated because life is more enjoyable if some power fulfills some other will and some will utilizes some other power.

The Puruṣa is the divine masculine and the Śakti is the divine feminine. They are originally one person. But they separate for enjoyment. The Puruṣa becomes the masculine will and the Śakti becomes feminine power. The power instigates the will—use me. The will instigates the power—do this for me. Thus, in different places, the Puruṣa and the Śakti are called mutually superior to each other, mutually inferior to each other, mutually inseparable from each other, and mutually united with each other. Those who cannot understand these varied descriptions can think about will and power to understand them.

**Divine and Mundane Meanings of Words**

All words originate in the divine non-dualistic reality. That is, their original meaning is non-dualistic. Har means stealing what we want to be stolen. Balātkāra means acting aggressively with a person who desires that aggression. In the material world, however, non-duality becomes duality. The new meaning of the word is either-or, devoid of the simultaneous presence of the opposites. Now, Har means stealing what we don’t want to be stolen. Balātkāra means acting aggressively with a person who doesn’t want that aggression. The bodily reality remains the same but their meanings and intentions are inverted.

The dualistic reality is the inversion of the non-dualistic reality due to the separation of opposites. Action with aggression is flawed because it is an action against someone’s will. Action without aggression is flawed because it is powerless against adversity. Stealing is flawed because it leads to violence. Charity is flawed because it leads to laziness. You cannot find a perfect situation under duality. Every case is potentially problematic in a different way.

To avoid these problems, we have to make a choice. In some cases, aggression is good. In other cases, aggression is flawed. In some cases, stealing is bad. In other cases, charity is bad. Duty or dharma can never be universalized due to duality. One has to think about the potential outcomes of any action before acting. As the time, place, situation, and person change, the action changes in the pursuit of dharma because dharma can never be universalized due to duality.

**Dualistic and Non-Dualistic Use of Words**

The words we use to describe reality are the most preliminary form of bodily reality called śabda or sound. Touch, sight, taste, and smell are more advanced forms of bodily reality. Under dualistic reality, a sound has only half the meanings while under non-dualistic reality the sound has opposite meanings. If we use different words for dualistic and non-dualistic reality, then nobody will understand the non-dualistic reality in the dualistic world because the non-dualistic words have no corresponding reality in the dualistic world. If we use the same word to describe the dualistic and non-dualistic realities, then everyone will understand it half-well.

Understanding half-well is better than not understanding. Hence, the same words are used for both dualistic and non-dualistic reality, creating confusion. The alternative is using different words, making it impossible for people to understand non-dualistic reality.

The use of the same words to describe duality and non-duality is a necessary evil. The evil has to be overcome by saying that the words understood in a dualistic sense are only half-true. There is another meaning of the same word that is not understood if the word is interpreted dualistically. For example, the dualistic meaning of Har is stealing without consent but the non-dualistic meaning of Har is stealing what we want to be stolen. The dualistic meaning of Balātkāra is unwanted aggression while the non-dualistic meaning of Balātkāra is acting aggressively with a person who wants such aggression.

**Dualistic and Non-Dualistic Minds**

Therefore, the dualistic mind finds problems in every statement (and everything) while a non-dualistic mind sees no problem in any statement (and anything). Enjoyment is good for the dualistic mind and suffering is bad. But for the non-dualistic mind, suffering causes detachment so it is good, and enjoyment causes attachment so it is bad. The non-dualistic mind therefore rejects both mundane enjoyment and suffering. Instead, he finds that suffering when working for Kṛṣṇa is pleasure, and enjoying while working for Kṛṣṇa is also pleasure. When the words are connected to Kṛṣṇa, then the opposition between words (such as enjoyment and suffering) disappears. The opposition appears due to a disconnect from Kṛṣṇa.

When a non-dualistic mind speaks, the dualistic mind infers one-half of the meaning and discards the other half because in his interpretation opposites cannot be simultaneously true. When a non-dualistic mind speaks, the dualistic mind assumes that one-half of the meaning must be meant even when both halves may be rejected. This is because dualistic thinking operates under the assumptions of binary logic governed by the principles of identity and the laws of non-contradiction and excluded middle.

This is why all the words spoken by non-dualistic minds are problematic, flawed, and incomprehensible to the dualistic mind because one-half of the meaning is not comprehended and without that, everything is open to criticism because one-half of the meaning always suffers from some dualistic problem. We cannot say that suffering is good because then the pursuit of liberation would be rejected. We cannot say that enjoyment is good because then detachment leading to liberation would be rejected.

This is the reason why those who have studied non-duality seriously often say that language is incapable of explaining non-duality because every word is interpreted dualistically under the material condition and hence, we must maintain silence forever. The counterargument against this silence is that if opposite things (under the dualistic interpretation of words) are accepted as true assuming non-duality, then over time the mind will renounce duality and become non-dualistic. We can explain how opposites are at once true. For example, we can explain the non-dualistic meanings of Har and Balātkāra. There is no contradiction although it is seemingly created due to the dualistic interpretation of words.

**The Problems of Aristotelian Logic**

Binary logic, or the three rules of identity, non-contradiction, and excluded middle, apply to objects and not to persons. Of course, nothing is an object; everything is a person. However, they can be persons under duality or non-duality. The person under duality models the world by binary logic, approximating it to an object. However, he can never complete this modeling because a person is not an object.

A simple example of the failure of object modeling in the case of a person is that men want to protect women and men want to be aggressive toward women. Similarly, women want to be protected by men and they desire aggression from men. Since protection and aggression are mutually opposed, therefore, using binary logic, we cannot model men or women. If logic is forcibly applied to model men or women, then we will have to discard one-half of what it means to be a man or woman. That is, men should protect and not be aggressive and women should desire protection and not aggression. Alternately, men should be aggressive and not protective and women should desire aggression and not protection.

The mind that has been contaminated by binary logic and object modeling of reality cannot understand persons because personhood involves the simultaneous existence of opposites that binary logic forbids. Under this binary logical thinking, both men and women have to stop being men and women and have to become monolithic objects. If this monolithic objectivity is universalized in the form of man-woman equality, then both men and women have to suppress their masculine and feminine tendencies because universalism says that both are equal so how can there be gender differences between them?

Even as the material world is dualistic and comprises mutual opposites, it is not binary logic, because the opposites are mutually defined, mutually coexistent, and simultaneously true. But they are not true of the same person. Unlike Puruṣa and Śakti who are alternately passive and aggressive, the dualistic person is either always aggressive or passive. Thus, some men are good providers and bad romantics while other men are good romantics but bad providers. Some women are good mothers but bad romantics while other women are good romantics but bad mothers.

Under dualism, we cannot get perfection in either man or woman in the material world. That perfection is attained under spiritual non-duality. But even under material duality, it is possible to get compatibility between man and woman where each partner prioritizes one thing over another and sacrifices the expectation of their opposites. Mutual compatibility involves the simultaneous co-existence of opposites, although in different persons.

**Prabhupāda Spoke Unvarnished Truths**

Prabhupāda spoke the simple unvarnished truths. His commentary on masculine aggression is also simple and true. A man is always famous for his aggression toward a beautiful woman, and such aggression is sometimes considered rape. Although rape is not legally allowed, it is a fact that a woman likes a man who is very expert at rape. The only problem in this statement is that he was thinking of wanted aggression and most people reading it are thinking of unwanted aggression. The verses in Śrīmad Bhāgavatam under which these comments are made are talking about wanted (rather than unwanted) aggression. But most people can’t think of wanted aggression. They have collectively forgotten what wanted aggression feels like, how both men and women want it, and how this desire builds emotionally satisfying marriages. They have collectively assimilated the idea that all aggression is unwanted.

When the mind is conditioned by the abundance of violent sexual imagery then it cannot understand romantic aggression. It seeks that aggression through violent sexual sadomasochism. It cannot grasp the meaning of “activity employing force” and translates it into “rape” which has a horrible denotation and connotation. This is not a flaw that anyone can fix without changing the Sanskrit-English dictionaries that mistranslated words. After all, how could anyone think anything that Europeans had never thought of? The colonial mistakes are not over. Those who want to fix those mistakes have to do what colonialists never tried.
