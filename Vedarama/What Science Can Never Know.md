# What Science Can Never Know

## Introduction

We have grown up under the great influence of science. It is the light that illuminates our modern world. Science has been lauded for us as the highest achievement of mankind. We have been educated by science programs and classes and told the glory of its marvelous discoveries. We have seen the tremendous power of science and our daily lives function through its massive and complex technology. We await the latest scientific discoveries and explorations, seeing science as our doorway to the mysteries of the universe. Many of us feel that someday, perhaps soon, science will uncover the key to life and unlock all the secrets of the cosmos. We are even using science on a genetic level to try to alter life. We are also using science to understand the ways of thought and feeling, even to measure meditation.

We are largely the product of the scientific mind, and its way of thinking underlies our thoughts. For many of us, science is the closest thing we have to religion; it is the repository of our faith. We regard science as the force of enlightenment and the leading edge of the great march of civilization. We do not trust anything to be real unless it has first been proved in scientific terms. Modern culture is thus essentially the culture of science, and in its wake art, philosophy, and religion have been reduced to shadows or satellites of the scientific approach.

Science, particularly modern physics and its great magnitudes in time and space, has taken upon itself the mystique of the spiritual, the eternal and the infinite. Our innate spiritual urge to transcend the limited domain of the mind and senses has become confused with the scientific endeavor to extend our horizons within it. The same glory and wonder that people formerly recognized in nature or in meditation, we find in the latest technology and its new information. Through science we seek the unlimited in the endless pursuit of the limited, striving for more factual knowledge and outer control, to provide us greater energy and novelty for our external seeking.

Science certainly has shown us many of the greater glories of the universe beyond our normal sensory range. It has given us the image of distant galaxies and uncovered the minute workings of the atom, extending our sense of both the large and the small to a superlative degree. Yet it has not found God or absolute truth or solved any of the ultimate issues of life and death. Moreover, that same beauty and glory of life is evident in all things, from the flower to the daily rising of the sun. Science has not created this glory, and in revealing it in the distance it may have caused us to miss seeing it in our daily lives. However far science takes us it cannot provide us knowledge of our own hearts.

We have also witnessed the great danger of science, the terrible forces it has unleashed, the monstrous weapons created through it, its alliance with commercial and militaristic powers, the destruction of nature, and often the turning of human beings into machines, which follows a mechanical-mathematical idea of the universe. We are taught to emulate the computer as the model of intelligence and to seek our fulfillment in life in being passive spectators of the latest mass-media events produced by our ever-developing technology. We try to understand human behavior in terms of statistics and are attempting to produce life in a test tube. Hence many of us turn to gadgets and drugs for our fulfillment in life as we are taught to seek ever-greater external stimulation. In all this we may just be following the way of external seeking that the scientific view of life encourages.

Even those of us who look beyond science are still often enamored of it. We may be influenced by its way of thinking and even against our deeper insight we may employ its methods in the spiritual realm, where they do not belong. Many of us would like to make some peace between science and the spiritual or possibly integrate them into some great new modern culture. While this may be possible at least to some degree, it requires subordinating science to the spiritual, not putting them both on the same level.

Certainly many of the discoveries of science have been helpful to humanity, and we do rely on them for our practical functioning. Improvements in sanitation, transportation, and communication are beneficial. Yet much that is artificial has also come through science and replaced something originally more wholesome. Our natural and healthy environment is being replaced by an artificial and technological world of metal, plastic, and machinery.

We should not confuse what brings us practical benefits with what brings us truth. Truth may not be according to our convenience, nor will it *necessarily* make our lives easier and more stimulating. It may not give us more information or more things to seek and to want. It may require that we question ourselves and our need for ever more power.

## Science and mysticism

We also see in the encounter between Eastern mysticism— particularly the yoga traditions—and Western science an attempt to link their major insights, which do have common ground. Both talk of the age of the universe in terms of billions of years and speak of the relativity of time and space. Some have seen this encounter as a great linking between East and West, the point of transformation for society. Certainly the vast universe revealed by science is closer to the views of Eastern religion than to the limited scope of traditional Western religious views. Moreover the open mind and practical experimental approach to the outer world in science has its counterpart in the freedom of thought, meditation, and the many yogic techniques from the spiritual traditions of the East.

In this encounter there is much that is interesting. However, science is not and can never be spiritual. Its field of knowledge is the outer, practical, and mundane, the apparent and the manipulatable. It has nothing to say about the inner and the spiritual. This is not to denigrate it but to put it in its place. We may need our car in order to travel, but our car cannot tell us who we are, where to go, or what is truth.

Spiritual knowledge is of an entirely different dimension than that of science. To find it, we must make our mind silent in meditation. There is nothing we can do in a laboratory to bring it about. Spiritual knowledge requires seeing the inherent limitations of science and the intellect, their utter incapacity to know reality. Though scientists have questioned the solidity of matter, they have not yet seriously questioned the outgoing mind and its ability to find truth.

This encounter between science and mysticism has not just served to give some scientific acknowledgement to the spiritual; it has, perhaps even more so, served to grant a yet more spiritual aura to science. We have the physicist as guru phenomenon, the doctor as priest. We are even looking the scientists, particularly those who have some openness to the spiritual realm, to become our spiritual guides. While such individuals may have something to offer us in the spiritual realm, it is not by reason of their scientific knowledge. To give a scientist or doctor credibility in the inner realm is like the credibility we give to movie stars or athletes to tell us what is the best product to buy; it is a false transference. There is no equation between scientific and spiritual knowledge.

Science cannot solve for us the great and ultimate issues of life and death, the soul and immortality. These are not issues that rest on theories, statistics, experiments, calculations, or equipment. They can only be solved by understanding the nature of our own consciousness. No material formula or agency can do this for us.

## High-tech yogas

One of the most evident forms of this connection between science and the spiritual is the development of new high-tech yogas. Such approaches use subliminal tapes, guided meditations, and special videos to try to attune the chakras through various sounds or instruments. They work through machines or media approaches and use a scientific type of terminology.

If there is a sound to which we can attune our chakras it must be one that we make with our own voice, as in chanting. It cannot be pre-made for us and we cannot just listen passively to it. There is no machine that can balance our internal or psychic energies. Similarly, if we want to gain the power of visualization, we must gain control of our own mind and imagination. We only loose control by passively following external suggestions. This is not to say that tapes or videos on spiritual teachings are not useful or that other devices may not be interesting or fun to play with, but that the real work is on ourselves when all external stimuli are put to rest.

There is no technological shortcut to enlightenment. There is nothing that we can just take, listen to, watch, or plug into and find truth. Better equipment is not going to advance our spiritual life. Entertainment is the way of escape and illusion, and it cannot bring us to reality.

## Employing the right means of knowledge

The means of knowledge must be appropriate to the object known. If we want to learn about microorganisms, we must use a microscope to find them. A telescope, however well made, will not find them, even though we may search the entire sky; similarly, we cannot find out what is the nature of our consciousness by looking on the outside.

If the spiritual exists, it is something infinite and eternal. It therefore can never be known by finite and transient instruments. That such instruments fail to find it is not surprising, as it is not in their field: to employ science to find truth or God is to employ the wrong means of knowledge. Science looks outwardly. Even when it "examines consciousness," it is through the outer body and brain and its chemical processes. The truth—the infinite and the eternal—is inherently outside the field of scientific knowledge, however far we extend it.

## Numbers and truth/ The measurable and the immeasurable

The basis of science is mathematics. Take away the measurements and what is left of science? Nothing. Yet what part of reality is the measurable? Is what is truly essential, meaningful, and beautiful in life capable of being measured? Can the most important things in life—love, beauty, and truth—be reduced to mumbers and equations? The measurable, however extensive, is only the surface appearance, not the real essence. Can the measurable dimensions of a flower substitute for its fragrance?

However large the numbers we discover, they remain finite; and compared to the infinite, the finite, however vast, is always nothing. However far in time we are able to project our knowledge, however many billions of years, it is still transient and only an instant compared to the eternal. However much we know of the external world, we still do not know who we really are.

Even one life is beyond price, beyond measure; how much so is all life? Any one human being is for more than any name, number, or image of them; so too are all beings. To put a number on something is not to understand it but rather to destroy its sacred presence and leave us only with its shadow. It is for this reason that science can be used by the commercial mentality, with nature or human beings numbered and priced, turned into commodities, and carelessly exploited or consumed.

However we extend our systems of measurement—which are theoretically endless—we can never get out of the domain of numbers. Life is not in numbers, however—great or small—nor is truth calculatable or computable. It is in both the totality and the individual, the whole and the indivisible. It is not the object of measurement but the subject of true awareness whose being is everywhere.

The outgoing mind can only deal with the part. It sees quantums or isolated bits, not the pervasive field. The more we focus on the part, the more we lose track of the whole. To understand the whole we must forget the part. To know the ocean we must stop focusing exclusively on the waves.

## Reality and systems

Every system of measurement creates its own illusion. We invent a system of measurement to determine reality, but each system is limited, for to measure is to limit. Though it may reveal certain things, it will obscure others. We give ourselves an illusion because what is not measurable is always more significant than what can be measured.

We fixate our minds on a system of measurement and its refinement and lose direct contact with life, which is ever-new. We try to reduce the immeasurable to the measurable, trying to limit life to a predictable pattern. We gain knowledge of names, forms, and numbers, but the living being, which is always more than these, is obscured. All systems of measurement are only approximate. They can only measure one side of things. We can bring more detail into them but the variability of life will always remain.

Unfortunately the mind often becomes so attached to its systems of measurement that the system becomes more important than the reality. We try to force reality into the mold of our equations. We reduce people and nature to numbers, prices, cogs in a machine, or parts of a formula. This is the basis of much of the destruction that is going on in the world. Yet when the mind's system is set aside, reality naturally restructures the mind in the power of consciousness.

## Quantity and quality

Science deals through the measurable with the domain of quantity. It can tell us about the quantitative side of life, but it has no place in determining qualitative issues, like those of the spiritual and religious domains. It can tell us how to get from here to there on a mundane level, but it cannot explain to us why we are alive or who we really are.

In each thing there is both a quantitative and a qualitative dimension. The quantitative dimension is the measurable, the known. The qualitative dimension is the presence, the beauty, the meaning and the sacred mystery. Science can only give us more quantities. Hence it produces a mass culture, but it cannot give us quality, which only comes through profound inner searching.

What do we really know of the universe? Science can tell us what kind of chemical reactions are going on in stars, but what does that really mean? Do we not feel through our intuitive perception the glory of the stars? Is that not evidence of something more that chemistry? Can any equation produce that?

## Experiment and observation

Another great cornerstone of science is experiment. It recognizes only the validity of those things which can be proved through experiment. This gives it the appearance of objectivity. But experiment, by nature, can only deal with external, material, manipulatable phenomena. How can we find out anything about what is internal, immaterial, spiritual by this process of materialistic experimentation, even if we extend it to the brain? Are God and the soul objects we can dissect upon a table or put into a laboratory? Experiment is limited, like numbers, to outward or measurable things. It can tell us nothing about the sacred.

According to yoga, even the brain is external to our true consciousness; even the body is not ours. Experiment by its very materialistic nature leaves out the real being, the true consciousness, which cannot be manipulated as a thing or a process.

To experiment with things is to manipulate them, to put them in an artificial circumstance. It is to take them apart and try to get to the knowledge of their workings. But neither man nor nature is a machine. The whole is greater than the sum of its parts and through division we cannot arrive at knowledge of the whole. It is only through observation and spiritual guidance that we can learn the truth of what we are and what the world is. This is not a matter of arranging experiments but ceasing to manipulate things from the outside.

## The yogic versus the scientific method

Modern science is based upon the scientific method. This says that first you invent a theory about how things work and then you look for data to prove it. Once you have enough data the theory can be said to be a law. Yoga says that this approach is not objective or scientific at all. The reason is that any theory or preconception will color our perception and thus create data to justify it. It is the theory that determines what the facts are, as it establishes what we are looking for.

It is like a man putting spotted glasses on and then trying to find data to prove that the world is spotted. Naturally, there is much in this vast and variegated world that may tend to give some sort of justification to any number of theories.

The yogic method is more scientific than the scientific method. It says that first we must empty our minds of all assumptions and preconceptions. We must approach the object without any predetermined ideas or judgements, with a mind that is completely still and open, like a mirror: then the mind can objectively reflect the nature of that object. We must not have any barrier of opinions between ourselves and the object but should unite with it completely in the silence of mind. Then its nature will be communicated to us. This is the "yogic science of direct perception." According to this spiritual science it is only in the unity of the observer and the observed that truth can be known. This is only possible through consciousness, not via any mechanical method. All instruments and ideas serve as barriers that prevent this direct knowing.

Science approaches reality in a measured and mediated manner, from the outside via equipment. It reduces reality to an equation, to a system, which then becomes more important than the thing itself. It only gives us an image of reality that blocks our direct experience of it. The reality of things can only be experienced but never really understood in terms of a mental pattern; hence there is no real truth in any theory. Truth can only come into being when the mind is one with the object of its perception, and all theories are barriers to that.

According to the ancient yogis, all outer knowledge—knowledge consisting of material name, form, number, and appearance—is *maya* or illusion. It is this knowledge which is ignorance, because it only sees the surface and misses the real thing altogether. It is like a man following out the patterns revealed in a kaleidoscope, not realizing that they are produced by his own turning of the instrument.

## The danger of thinking that we know

A few years ago I went to hear the meditation teacher J. Krishnamurti speak at the Los Alamos Laboratory. The topic was "Creativity in Science." His answer was very simple: "There is no creativity in science. Science deals with the known and creativity deals with the unknown." The creative dimension of life is not a matter of number or quantity. It proceeds through the unique, the ever-new, the non-repeatable. What we think we know is our ignorance and arrogance. True knowledge is not an activity of the mind but revealed by divine descent. It occurs when we realize the utter incapacity of our mind to know the truth and recognize the sacred mystery of all that is.

The Upanishads also say, "Into blinding darkness enter those who follow ignorance. Into even greater darkness fall those who are attached to knowledge." Outer knowledge, if we take it to be true knowledge, is worse than ignorance. It makes us think we know something real when we only know trivial facts and superficial appearances.

## The knower and the known

Science has extended our knowledge of the known, but has it given us the knowledge of the knower? The great yogic quest, as portrayed in the Upanishads, is "By what can the knower be known?" Even if we know everything, if we fail to know the knower, we have missed out on the main thing. The knower cannot be known through the instruments of knowledge. Modern science cannot tell us who the knower is. The knower can only be known through spiritual culture. The great quest of true knowledge is to seek self knowledge and love of God. Science cannot give us this. If we know everything else but not our spiritual selves and our relationship to the Absolute, we are like the man who has gained the entire world but lost his soul.

Modern science, hopefully, is beginning to realize its limitations. Some scientists are beginning to see that the outer world and the externally directed mind can only take us so far—that however far we go, we end up with something unknown or transcendent; that even the influences of time and space as we know them eventually are found to disappear. A true scientist knows that science and the spiritual are as distinct as the spiritual and plumbing. We don't go to our plumber to find out the nature of truth or reality or how to organize our society; but that we don't go to him for such knowledge is not to denigrate his work or its practical usefulness.

## Science and fundamentalist religion

Perhaps the most positive thing in modern science is its sense of free inquiry and examination. Compared to it the restrictive mentality of organized and fundamentalist religions appears quite narrow and ignorant. Science can give us a greater sense of the universe and thereby take us out of the narrow boundaries of our identification with class, nation, race, or divisive religious identities, and thereby encourage a global consciousness. In this respect many scientists are much more open and humane than a number of religious leaders. Such scientists are right in pointing out the danger of fundamentalist religious influences and their bigotry and dogma. Yet these fundamentalists do not represent the true spirit of religion, which is oneness in purpose with the divine will. It is not only the scientists that they have attacked and oppressed, it is also the mystics and yogis they have tried to eliminate.

On the other hand, science with its spiritual void can make us vulnerable in time to such distorted religious influences because it has nothing to feed the soul, and the starved soul may eventually reach out for anything that appears spiritual. Science and fundamentalist religion are the two opposite sides of the same coin. They represent the two sides of the imbalance within human culture today. Each represents an extreme that holds in bondage one side of truth. Science holds in bondage freedom of mind. Fundamentalist religion imprisons and confuses our need to connect with the Divine. If we criticize science without recognizing the limitations of fundamentalist and organized religion, we may only serve to give power to the latter.

Moreover, the free spirit of science is not as free or wide as it appears. Freedom to pursue the outer is not true freedom. It is a freedom within the field of bondage—inside the prison, as it were— not the freedom to go beyond it. A really open mind questions thought and the ego, not just the things of the outer world.

Science has a certain humaneness to it that can appear enlightened. It often seeks material welfare and the expansion of outer knowledge for all people. Yet to promote the welfare of our outer nature without regard for the inner is to nourish the body and starve the soul. To open the field of outer expansion can be to close the door to inner transcendence. It is a freedom to pursue transient pleasure, which is the loss of inner contentment and eternal happiness. It is the freedom to seek sorrow.

Science may not be the advanced stage in the evolution of intelligent life that we think it is. It is still in the adolescence of material seeking, not in the maturity of inner knowing. The truly advanced civilizations in the universe are not those of super-technologies warping time and space to produce more power for their outer seeking, but those of great yogis contemplating the infinite within the heart. To confuse scientific knowledge with true knowledge is perhaps the greatest error of our age. By its materialistic and outward nature science may lead us towards destruction if we do not recognize its limitations. Spiritual knowledge is the knowledge that takes us beyond death. It is not the knowledge that merely prolongs our physical life or gives us more capacity within it, but that which goes beyond the outer realm of time and space altogether. Science cannot give us this knowledge.

The problem with scientific thinking, its skepticism and inquiry, is that it has not gone far enough. We have yet to doubt thought and the intellect as being capable of knowing reality. We have yet to question our whole means of knowledge. We have yet to question the whole field of outer knowledge based upon measurement and equipment. Above all we have not yet questioned our ego, our separate selfish identity in life. Until we do this, whatever we pursue must result in confusion and illusion. Our knowledge will only reflect and justify the preconceptions and prejudices of our ego.

All life is full of supreme wonder and beauty when we free ourselves from the "known," when we cease to project the patterns of our acquisitive mind upon the face of God. The infinite is closer to you than your own aorta. It is ultimately either directly or indirectly behind the blinking of your eyes, within your breath, and behind your voice. It is the light through which you see and the curiosity by which you think. Take pity on the poor people deluded by endless data and numbers. Do not let your mind rule you. Better to be ruled by divine love than scientific law.
