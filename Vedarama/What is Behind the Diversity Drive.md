# What is Behind the Diversity Drive?

## What is Behind the Diversity Drive?

DECEMBER 15, 2023

Reading Time: 6 minutes

When you give people comfort, they become lazy and demand more comfort. Technology creates more comfort. Thereby, it also creates lazy and demanding people. The growing laziness initially reduces the benefits of technology—people can do more with technology, but since they are becoming lazier, therefore, they do less with technology. Over time, as laziness increases, it offsets all the benefits of technology.

Very few people know that despite rapid advances in technology, organizational productivity has been decelerating for several decades now. Each new technological innovation creates the possibility to do more and cheaper, makes people a little bit lazier, and the growing laziness cancels the benefits of new technology. Meanwhile, people demand new technology, the technology makers have to sell technology, the new technology is more expensive than the stable technology, and purchasing new technology requires higher salaries. The workers demand higher salaries to buy new technology and their employers give it to them to ensure that their newly produced technology is purchased by consumers. Thereby, new technology leads to higher compensation and greater laziness.

We can summarize all these facts in one statement—(a) new technology is created, (b) people buy that technology, (c) growing comfort makes them lazier, (d) productivity declines, and (e) the employers have to pay more for the same level of productivity. Since money is spent, but the benefits are not accrued proportionately, all this extra spending for declining returns accumulates as debt to be repaid in the future.

The above chart shows how productivity growth has come down to near zero in G7 countries from a peak of 8-10% in the 1950s. All the extra money spent on creating technological innovation is not increasing productivity, which means that the return on investment is negative. More and more money has to be poured in to maintain the same level of productivity, which just contributes to rising prices and debt.

When corporations began struggling with decelerating productivity in the early 2000s, human resources doctors prescribed a new medicine—diversity increases productivity. If you put people with different skin colors in the same project, you will magically grow productivity.

There is truth in this assertion. Every problem has many facets. If you solve one facet of the problem in isolation, you make the solution of other facets harder. Ideally, you want all the facets of the problem to be solved simultaneously. People from diverse cultures can look at different facets of the problem better than people from one culture. If all those facets are highlighted while solving a problem, the problem would be solved more comprehensively.

However, this premise generally fails because it is very difficult to solve all the facets of a problem simultaneously. Solutions to complex problems evolve over time. Products and technologies are introduced with minimum functionality (every new product or technology begins in the minimum viable stage). Over time, they get new features. The different facets of a single problem are prioritized and sequenced for a solution. While focusing on one facet of the problem, the other facets are ignored. Solutions to the previously ignored facets often require modifications to previously solved facets. A comprehensive solution involves iterating over the different facets sequentially and cyclically.

When we put people with diverse cultures together, and emphasize different facets of the problem, the process of building a solution stagnates because it is very hard to solve all the facets of the problem simultaneously. Diverse voices talking about different facets of the problem become distractions from the task of solving one facet of the problem and getting the product or technology to market. Trying to solve all facets at once throws a group of people into internal conflict, and delays the development of technology.

Therefore, the human resources solution of diversity is theoretically good and practically bad. It is theoretically good because every problem has many facets that have to be solved collectively to produce a good solution. It is practically bad because all those facets are very hard to solve at once. The sequencing of those facets requires some voices to maintain silence. After that, the previously silent voice can speak again, bringing focus to another facet of the problem, while the previously vocal voices go silent. Everyone trying to solve different facets of the problem at once is like many people saying many things in a room with no one being heard. They have to speak one by one, while the others maintain silence, for everyone to be heard.

If diversity is used to improve productivity, it produces negative results because a diverse group cannot be simultaneously productive. As a result, when a group becomes diverse, it stops producing anything useful. All the time is spent hopping from meeting to meeting, producing meeting notes, while getting nothing done. A diverse group spends most of its time resolving internal conflicts. Only the most aggressive voices, if not silenced, can get something done. Under such aggression, most people go quiet and become disengaged from the process of solving problems. They don’t complain vocally but they vote with their feet. They often renounce responsibility and let someone else take the heat. All this eventually affects productivity negatively.

The human resources doctors cannot accept that their solution is worse than the problem of declining productivity. They besmirch the opponents of diversity as ones advocating racism, homophobia, patriarchy, and sexism. To avoid the accusations by human resources or fellow diversity champions, the people who were previously engaged in trying to solve problems stop their efforts. The net result is an even greater and faster productivity decline.

Meanwhile, to align costs with productivity, the human resources doctors have to either reduce salaries or lay people off. In either case, the overall consumption drops, the profits decline, while productivity continues to decline. That has to then invite a new round of layoffs, decreasing salaries, or both. The laid-off people or people with declining salaries become a burden on society either through social security, or through rising crime, or both. What cannot be obtained by working is now obtained through crime or government dole-outs.

Human resources doctors have for decades claimed that they make the workplace better. To their utter shock and dismay, they found that at the end of the COVID pandemic, people did not want to return to offices. People preferred to work from home. They don’t like the colleagues they are working with. They don’t want to see them face to face. They want to maintain distance. Working from home helps them do that. Of course, they rationalize their preference through various socially acceptable arguments. They don’t say that meeting people at the workplace has already become a source of pain and that they have become so used to comfort that they cannot be bothered to work harder for it.

Working hard for comfort is the essential contradiction of industrialization. If you get comfort, then you will not work hard. You will accept hard work only when comfort is missing. As comfort rises, people stop working hard and demand more comfort, the demand fuels technological innovation but the consumers of this innovation are not going to work hard to pay for it. The net result is rising debt and prices.

Long-lived prosperous societies made people work hard by teaching them about duty and responsibility. They instilled a sense of meaning and purpose in life. With a higher purpose, diverse people can cooperate because goals are at the center of the work. Each person’s ego was not at the center of their work. The anchor of work was in a common purpose. With a sense of duty, people can work harder because they are taught about obligation toward others. Industrialization wiped away the sense of duty, meaning, purpose, and responsibility. It taught people to work hard for their comfort, not recognizing the inherent contradiction in that proposition. The result is declining productivity. Myriad attempts to fix this problem—such as through greater diversity—simply worsen the problem because the solution lies elsewhere. But until the system of working hard for comfort collapses through continuously declining productivity, people will not consider the alternative.
