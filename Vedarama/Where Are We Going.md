# Where Are We Going?

## Chapter 1. What Comes After Death?

There is probably not a single person who has not thought at some time in life about what happens when death comes.

The problem of death is universal and, thanks to the media, it is present among us every day and at all hours. It is evident that death does not distinguish between poor and rich, ugly and pretty, or fool and genius. Even so, we prefer to close our eyes, relegating this unpleasant prospect to oblivion.

We may think, especially in youth, that death is something that happens “to the elderly”, or to people who have “bad luck” and suffer a fatal accident, or to the unfortunate who fall victim to a terrorist attack or a crime, or who are overpowered by natural disasters like floods, fires, earthquakes or hurricanes. We see death so far away that we do not even consider that we are already, invariably, as mortals, on the list of the future deceased.

People, as soon as the subject of death arises, prefer not to talk about it, because it causes uncertainty and fear, since the complexity of the matter leaves us without finding answers, and, above all, because nobody wants to die.

## Death and the question of immortality

What happens at the time of death?

Is there life after death?

What awaits us in the afterlife?

These are questions of all men and women of all ages of humanity; questions that haunt us especially when we have to attend the funeral of a loved one, when we suffer from a severe illness, or when, inexorably, we realize that our youth is fading.

This feeling usually appears around the age of 45, in the middle of life; but today, in a time when youth is worshiped like a goddess, we often notice it already at 30. This feeling is imposed when we realize that from now on we will no longer continue “climbing the mountain”, that from now on we are going down—at an accelerating speed. We are aware that we will not be able to stop the decay of the body, neither with weight loss cures nor with exhausting gymnastics, or rejuvenating creams. More and more people are spending considerable amounts of money on beauty creams and cosmetic surgery clinics, in a vain attempt to stop what is impossible to stop. Suffering from aging and the loss of the body is due to identification with it. Although we feel young inside, we believe that the aging body is us. This conviction, this material vision of reality, leads to the first response.

## Response 1: After death we will cease to exist

The materialist, whether humanist, atheist, or scientist, claims that all personality and consciousness are totally and permanently extinguished when the body dies. When the body disappears, the person disappears with it. In other words, for most of us, the person is the body.

Because the body is made of material elements, such as earth, water, fire and air, and death signals the end of the visible temporal structure, the disappearance of the body signifies the end of existence. Even if we accept the law of the conservation of energy, which says that energy is never lost but only transformed, it is undeniable that the aggregation of this energy in the form of a living being undergoes such a drastic change with death that it stops manifesting what we call life. Therefore, if we identify the vital force with the material body, the extinction of the body implies the extinction of life.

For the materialist, human life with its thoughts, feelings, and desires, including intangible phenomena such as “love,” “beauty,” “goodness,” or “justice,” is nothing more than a combination of biochemicals, atoms, molecules and different energies. The same applies to animals, plants and everything that exists in nature. Everything is constantly transforming, adopting new forms and creations, and everything is destined to disappear when the aggregation of material elements disintegrates.

The materialist thinks that when a child is born, a new person begins to exist who then grows up, lives his life, and finally dies. At that moment, that person has ceased to exist. In the best of cases, creative works and thoughts remain alive in the memory of people and generations to come, but there is no such thing as an immortal being.

The materialist does not conceive of anything beyond a single substance, and this substance is physical, empirical, and material. For him, all human existence, all thoughts and all feelings arise from the nonliving and inorganic matrix of physical nature, and it all disappears at the time of death like a bubble that bursts and vanishes into thin air.

## Response 2: After death we will be resurrected

The Christian tradition teaches that the human being, composed of spirit, soul and body, receives from God one life on Earth. God creates a new soul during conception, and the life of this soul is unique and decisive because the soul has the freedom to approach God or move away from Him.

Death entered human life as a consequence of sin. Christ, by emerging victorious from sin, also overcomes death and clears the path for those who have faith in Him. Resurrection at the end of time will bring to its culmination the project of salvation carried out by God for the whole of humanity throughout history. In the resurrection of the flesh of all the dead on the day of final judgment, God will accomplish for all men what he accomplished on Easter morning with His son Jesus. The chosen ones, even in their bodies, will be conformed to the endless life of the risen Christ.

Although there are variations and different details regarding the doctrines expressed above, in essence, all the main branches of the Christian faith profess the same idea about what happens after death.

## Response 3: After death we will transmigrate

According to the sages of ancient India, the present life is not the only one, but a single link in a long chain of births and deaths. We are not the body, but the person, the soul, within it. We are eternal travelers who go from body to body, appearing in different disguises as actors on a stage: sometimes as a celebrity, other times as a despised fool; sometimes as a king or queen, other times as a beggar. Sometimes we play the role of a man, other times that of a woman. And with each change, we forget our previous life.

The change of body implies the transmigration of the soul. At the moment of death, the life force, the soul, leaves the body and enters a new body that is being formed according to its past desires and actions. When this new body deteriorates and becomes useless, whether due to old age, illness or other causes, the soul leaves it and enters another womb to build a new body vehicle. This cyclical process is also known as samsara, the cycle of repeated birth and death.

The concept of the cycle agrees with what we observe around us. Everything in the universe is cyclical. Nothing ends at a point of “no return.” We just have to observe nature and realize how everything around us works “around the clock”. Always after winter comes spring, and after the night dawns a new day.

In addition, the doctrine of reincarnation offers a rational and logical explanation about the differences (physical, psychic, social, cultural, economic, etc.) that are found between human beings, showing that there is neither luck nor undeserved bad luck, nor arbitrary fate or blind chance, nor an unjust Creator or partial God.

If we come to understand the mechanism of reincarnation and the law behind it, karma, we can gain a clear understanding of the phenomena of life and death, and what comes after death – knowledge that will leave no open questions or loose ends.

One who knows the laws of karma and samsara, and shapes his life according to these guidelines, has the possibility of coping more easily with the small and large problems of life. In this way, the human being can free himself from the deep-seated fear of death and truly become the architect of his fortune, positively influencing the preparation of his future.

## Chapter 2. What is Reincarnation?

The term “reincarnate” comes from Latin re (again) and incarnare (to enter the flesh) and literally means “to re-enter a body of flesh and blood”, or “to take again a bodily form.” It is not the same as “resurrection” which proposes that the person be resurrected in the same and only body that he had when he died.

The thesis of this book is that the reincarnation process obeys specific laws. It is a scientific process, not a question of faith. If the transmigration of the soul is a fact, if it is true that we have lived before this existence and that we continue to live in another body after the physical death of our present body, then it is valid in all places and at all the times, also in the West and in the 21st century, and also for those who do not accept it. However, if the doctrine of reincarnation is not true, it cannot be a fact anywhere, neither in the East, specifically in India, nor in the West because it has never been a fact under any circumstances, not even for those who believe in it.

The subject of the present work is the systematic investigation of the subtle laws of reincarnation and its consequences for our lives. We will address, among others, the following questions:

\- What exactly happens at the moment of death?

\- Why don’t we remember our past lives?

\- Do our actions, thoughts and desires influence our future lives?

\-    Can animals incarnate in human bodies?

\-    And can humans transmigrate to animal or even plant species?

\-    What is karma?

\-    Can we, and should we, get out of the cycle of repeated births and deaths? If yes, how?

\- After liberation, what awaits us; where does the soul go, and what does it do?

## The Vedas, sources of wisdom

The most comprehensive sources on reincarnation are the Vedas, the revealed scriptures of India. Their origin dates back to over 5,000 years, which means that they are the oldest writings in the history of mankind.

The word veda comes from Sanskrit (the original language of India) and means “knowledge.” The Vedas contain knowledge on a wide range of subjects, both spiritual and material. Some examples are the Ayurveda (medicine), Jyotirveda (astronomy and astrology), Sthapatyaveda (architecture and engineering), Gandharvaveda (music and art), and Dhanurveda (tools and weapons).

Because of the complexity of Sanskrit, the Vedic scriptures remained for centuries beyond the reach of Westerners. The first translations appeared in the 19th century when the German Indologist Max Muller (1823-1900) dedicated himself to translating the most prominent Vedic texts into English and German.

In more recent times, Om Visnupada 108 Sri Srimad A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada (1896-1977), who founded the International Society for Krishna Consciousness (ISKCON), translated over 70 volumes of ancient Sanskrit texts into English. His books, which are already available in all the major languages, are recognized as standard works in 80% of the world’s universities, where they are used as textbooks.

Among all the Vedic texts, the two most famous ones are the Bhagavad-gita (lit. the song of God) and the Srimad-Bhagavatam. The Gita is part of the monumental epic Mahabharata, which has the distinction of being, with 100,000 verses, the longest text in world literature. Although the Bhagavad-gita comprises only 700 verses, it is the most celebrated scripture because it is spoken by Krsna, the Supreme Person, who explains to Arjuna, His friend and devotee, the essence of all the fundamental aspects of Vedic knowledge. Krsna describes the different yoga systems, and among the principal topics, we find the teachings on the soul, death and reincarnation. Therefore, we will often refer to this text to explain the process of the transmigration of the soul and related topics.

## The mystery of life

Modern science defines life as “a living organism of one or more cells that grow and develop, reproduce, respond to stimuli, and have a metabolism.” But what causes a cell to grow and develop? There are also dead cells that no longer grow or reproduce. What is missing? If life is nothing more than the result of complex chemical reactions, then why has no scientist succeeded in adding the missing ingredients to a dead cell and bring it back to life?

The molecular biologist and English Nobel Prize winner Francis Crick (19162004), who discovered DNA in 1953, proclaimed in 1966 that science will be able, in the near future, to generate a living organism synthetically, based on the combination of amino acids. However, despite continuous attempts, and increasingly advanced technology and trillion-dollar budgets, no one has been able to produce life in the laboratory. In fact, Sri Srimad A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada challenged the scientific community in 1973. He said: “I am not even asking them to generate a human being, but only to produce something as simple as an ant or a blade of grass. But that is not possible, because life does not come from matter–life comes from life.

The Bhagavad-gita explains that living organisms show symptoms of life because they are animated by the vital force which is by constitution a particle of immaterial energy. This particle is called in Sanskrit atma or jiva and is identical with what is understood in the West as soul. The jiva is infinitesimal and is beyond the perception of our senses. The Svetasvatara Upanisad (5.9) confirms this as follows:

*balagra-sata-bhagasya satadha kalpitasya ca*  
*bhago jivah sa vijneyah sa canantyaya kalpate*

**“When the upper point of a hair is divided into one hundred parts and again each of such parts is further divided into one hundred parts, each such part is the measurement of the dimension of the spirit soul.”**

Being of another nature, the spiritual spark that gives life to the material body cannot be detected, even with the most powerful microscope. And it is not imaginable by mental conjecture either. In fact, even scientists admit that life eludes scientific investigation and remains a mystery.

The aforementioned Francis Crick acknowledged this perplexity in his book Life Itself—Its Origin and Nature:

“What we have discovered is that even at this very basic level there are complex structures which occur in many identical copies—that is, which have organized complexity— and which cannot have arisen by pure chance. Life, from this point of view, is an infinitely rare event, and yet we see it teeming all around us. How can such rare things be so common?”

In the same way that light is the symptom of the sun, consciousness is the symptom of the soul, the jiva. We may not see the sun because the clouds cover our view, but thanks to daylight we can understand that the sun is in the sky and illuminates everything with its rays. Similarly, although we do not have the ability to see the spirit soul with our material eyes, we can deduce its presence from the signs of consciousness.

Because the soul belongs to the immaterial dimension, its qualities and characteristics cannot be observed, even by the most advanced material technology. Danish physicist and Nobel laureate Niels Bohr (1885-1962) recognized:

‘We have to admit that we cannot find anything in physics or chemistry that has a relationship, even remotely, with consciousness. However, we know that there is such a thing as consciousness because the simple fact is that we possess it ourselves. Therefore, consciousness has to be part of nature or, rather, a part of reality. But that means that we must assume that, far from the physical and chemical laws, as explained by quantum theory, there are other laws of a completely different nature.

## The gross and subtle bodies

The spiritual soul develops material bodies according to its level of consciousness, and this consciousness evolves and changes according to the interaction of the soul with the body. The material body comprises two principal parts: the gross body and the subtle body. Krsna explains in the Bhagavad-gita (7.4) that material nature is composed of eight basic elements—five gross elements and three subtle elements—which provide the ingredients for the formation of the two bodies.

The elements are:

1. earth
2. water
3. fire
4. air
5. ether
6. mind
7. intelligence
8. ego

The five dense elements correspond, in the terminology of modern science, to all kinds of solid matter (earth), all kinds of liquids (water), all kinds of radiant energy (fire), all kinds of gases (air) and the omnipresent space (ether).

All the phenomena that we can perceive in this world consist of a combination of these five main elements. Our eyes see certain shapes, our ears perceive all kinds of sounds, with the nose we perceive different smells, with the tongue we savor certain flavors, and with the skin we can perceive different structures. In this way, we jivas come in contact with the outside world through the bodily senses.

The three subtle elements (mind, intelligence, and ego) cannot be seen, heard, smelled, tasted, or touched. But we know that they exist because they can be perceived by their symptoms. The presence of the mind is noted by its powers of thinking, feeling and willing. The symptom of intelligence is the faculty of discrimination, and the ego is perceived by the sense of identity that each of us has. Because this identity is usually linked to the temporary material body, this sense of ego is called in Sanskrit ahaìkara, false ego, because our true identity is spiritual. We will delve into this topic in more detail later.

## The spiritual spark

Coming back to the spirit soul, let’s see what its characteristics are. After qualifying the eight material elements as apara-prakrti, inferior energy, Krsna goes on to explain: “Apart from this inferior energy, there is another higher energy, para-prakriti, the living entities who use the resources of the universe.” (Bhagavad-gita, 7.5).

The spirit soul is superior to matter and has the power to manipulate and exploit it to satisfy its desires. The interaction of the jiva with its gross and subtle bodies results in an extremely complex network of reactions that are beyond the scope of nuclear physics, chemistry, and biology. For this very reason, it is so difficult for science to define the exact difference between a living body and a dead body. Obviously, something is missing, but no one knows what it is exactly.

The Bhagavad-gita explains the difference between a living body and a dead body with the presence or absence of the soul. As soon as the living force leaves the body, the body is considered “dead.” The characteristics of the soul are explained in detail in the Second Chapter of the Bhagavad-gita (Texts 20, 23-25):

“For the soul, there is neither birth nor death at any time. He has not come into being, does not come into being, and will not come into being. He is unborn, eternal, ever-existing and primeval. He is not slain when the body is slain.

“The soul can never be cut to pieces by any weapon, nor burned by fire, nor moistened by water, nor withered by the wind.

“This individual soul is unbreakable and insoluble and can be neither burned nor dried. He is everlasting, present everywhere, unchangeable, immovable and eternally the same.

One may wonder, if the soul is eternally the same, how do the different species of life come into being? Krsna explains in the Bhagavad-gita (13.22) that these are due to the soul’s contact with the material qualities or modes, the gunas: karanam guna-saìgo ‘sya sad-asad-yoni-janmasu. “Due to his contact with material nature, the living entity meets good and bad in different species.”

## The three modes of material nature

According to the Vedic natural sciences, the variety of the different species is based on the combination and gradation of three elemental modes of material energy called gunas: 1. sattva-guna (goodness), 2. raja- guna (passion), and 3. tama-guna (ignorance).

The three gunas can be compared to the three primary colors — yellow, red, and blue — that form the basis for a great variety of hues.

If we mix the 3 primary colors, we will get 6 secondary colors, then 6 tertiary colors, etc. Similarly, the three modes of nature can form numerous combinations to offer bodies and minds according to every type of consciousness imaginable. Scientific discoveries about the structure of DNA support this view, as each DNA has been shown to be different, allowing an individual to be identified by analyzing their DNA.

The different bodies of the living entities can be compared to different types of vehicles or houses, each different in size, shape and color. The inhabitant of each body, the soul, is conditioned by the three gunas that largely determine its freedom of movement and action, its possibilities for enjoyment and its abilities to manipulate the environment.

A tree, for example, cannot move; a tortoise can move only slowly, while a horse can run and jump freely. There is an immense variety of living beings, each with its peculiarities and differences that are based on the three modes of nature.

Although the mode of ignorance predominates in plants and animals, there are also gradations between these species. Among animals, the donkey is the typical animal in ignorance, the tiger and other predators are dominated by passion, while in the cow and the bee, who only work for the benefit of others, goodness prevails.

Even among the same species, we find these differences. Among birds, the swan symbolizes goodness, the eagle passion, and the crow ignorance. Although there is a biological similarity between the different representatives of the same species, the consciousness and actions of different beings show different expressions that are due to the influence of the modes of nature.

Human beings are no exception. People who are primarily influenced by kindness show qualities such as compassion, honesty, charity, and loyalty. Those who act mainly under passion are dominated by selfishness, envy, greed, lust, lust for power, lust for fame, and other intense desires. In other people, who live in ignorance, inertia, stupidity, drunkenness, illusion, etc. predominate.

Thus, there is a great variety of material bodies, where the living entity can take a seat to play its role according to the three gunas that influence its consciousness.

The three modes are also compared to three types of strings that bind the soul to material existence. As long as the consciousness remains absorbed in the qualities of these three gunas, the soul is drawn from one material body to another. The process of bhakti-yoga, which we will explain in the last chapter, aims to put an end to this miserable situation, overcome the three gunas and connect our consciousness with Transcendence. Krsna says in the Bhagavad-gita (14.26):

*mam ca yo ‘vyabhicarena bhakti-yogena sevate*  
*sa gunan samatityaitan brahma-bhuyaya kalpate*

**“One who engages in full devotional service, unfailing in all circumstances, at once transcends the modes of material nature and thus comes to the level of Brahman \[Transcendence\].”**

Later we will look more closely at Transcendence and its different aspects.

## The change of body in the present life

If we look at our own existence, we realize that our body has changed in many ways since we were born. Still, we feel the same person. We remember when we were young, and we recognize ourselves in photos, although now our bodies are different, our mentality has changed and our feelings are not the same. This transmigration of the soul through a single body during a single life could be described as “internal reincarnation.” Based on this universal experience of the human being, the Bhagavad-gita (2.13) offers a key analogy to explain the phenomenon of reincarnation: “As the embodied soul continuously passes, in this body, from boyhood to youth to old age, the soul similarly passes into another body at death. A sober person is not bewildered by such a change.”

## The change of body at the time of death

With this analogy, Krsna establishes the foundation of spiritual knowledge. The first lesson is to understand the difference between matter and spirit. Aham brahmasmi, I am spirit soul, is the primordial and essential realization of the transcendentalist. Krsna also answers the question: “What determines the afterlife, on what does the type of body that we receive after death depend?” In Chapter Eight, Verse 6, he says: “Whatever state of being a person remembers when he quits his body, that state will attain without fail.” And later (Bg. 15.8): “The living entity in the material world carries his different conceptions of life from one body to another as the air carries aromas. Thus, he takes one kind of body and again quits it to take another.

## Memories of past lives

Why don’t we remember our lives in previous bodies? First of all, our memory, even in the present body, is limited. We can hardly remember what we were doing a few days ago, what to speak of weeks, months or years. Secondly, let us imagine that we could remember not only everything we have done in this life but also everything we have done in previous lives. Our mental system would collapse or be in a state of constant anxiety and overload. From this point of view, it can be considered an act of divine mercy that our memory is relatively short. As Krsna explains in the Bhagavad-gita (15.15): sarvasya caham hrdi sannivisöo mattah smrtir jnanam apohanam ca. “I am seated in everyone’s heart, and from Me come remembrance, knowledge and forgetfulness. By all the Vedas, I am to be known. Indeed, I am the compiler of Vedanta, and I am the knower of the Vedas.”

Thus, it is an act of divine grace that we can forget the past and live in the present without the burden of “terrible memories.” However, those who have the desire to know about their past can resort to techniques such as regression therapy, which can bring back memories of previous lives. But why waste time with such memories? The past will not come back and cannot be changed. What we can and should change is the present and thus prepare for the future.

What happens at the moment of death is the crucial point in life. We will leave our gross body and will be carried by our subtle body to our next destination. It is, therefore, the subtle body with its thoughts, feelings and desires that determines, to a large extent, the future gross body. But our thoughts, feelings and desires are not the only factors that decide our future. There is another determining factor involved: karma.

## Chapter 3. The Law of Cause and Effect

Without the law of karma (action and reaction), reincarnation would be an accidental event and thus not very meaningful. Karma literally means “action, or deed,” but these words do not convey the full meaning. Since there is no translation that conveys the true meaning, we will use the Sanskrit term, which has become an accepted notion in many Western languages. To understand how karma works, it will be helpful to understand the mechanism that governs the law behind it.

## Karma, the law of action and reaction

The term karma first appeared in the West in the 19th century, when Madame Blavatsky introduced it into her system of theosophy:

Karma is the basic law of the cosmos that links effect with cause in the physical, mental and psychic world. Because no cause can exist without its corresponding effect--from the greatest to the smallest, from the cosmic cataclysm to the movement of your hand--and because something similar produces something similar, karma is the invisible law and recognized that links in a wise, fair and considerate way each effect to its cause, relating the result to its cause.

The Austrian philosopher Rudolf Steiner compared the law of karma with the natural law of balance between cause and effect, or, as Newton expressed it in his third axiom of classical mechanics: “To every action, there is always opposed an equal reaction.” Even in the Bible, there are indications of such a law; for example, in Revelation (22:12): “Look, I am coming soon! My reward is with me, and I will give to each person according to what they have done.”

And in the words of the Apostle Saint Paul (II Corinthians 9:6): “Remember this: Whoever sows sparingly will also reap sparingly, and whoever sows generously will also reap generously.”

This understanding was commonly accepted during the first centuries of Christianity, until the year 553 of our era, when at the celebration of the second council of Constantinople, reincarnation was considered anathema, and the dogma of the resurrection was imposed. But, the laws of nature do not stop acting because we ignore them. If someone puts his hand in the fire, he will get burned. Fire does not forgive even a small child who is ignorant of the scorching quality of fire.

If each action has its corresponding reaction, we can imagine the complexity of the law of karma that takes into account each action of each human being and provides him with the corresponding reaction, whether in the near or distant future. But a law is not an impersonal or blind force. Behind every law, there is a legislator. So how does this law work? Who watches that it is applied fairly? Krsna says in the Bhagavad-gita (18.61):

*isvarah sarva-bhutanam hrd-dese ‘rjuna tisöhati*  
*bhramayan sarva-bhutani yantraruòhani mayaya*

**“The Supreme Lord is situated in everyone’s heart, O Arjuna, and is directing the wanderings of all living entities, who are seated as on a machine, made of the material energy.”**

Srila Prabhupada comments on this verse:

Krsna, as the localized Supersoul, sits in the heart directing the living being. After changing bodies, the living entity forgets his past deeds, but the Supersoul, as the knower of the past, present and future, remains the witness of all his activities. Therefore all the activities of living entities are directed by this Supersoul. The living entity gets what he deserves and is carried by the material body, which is created in the material energy under the direction of the Supersoul. As soon as a living entity is placed in a particular type of body, he has to work under the spell of that bodily situation. A person seated in a highspeed motorcar goes faster than one seated in a slower car, though the living entities, the drivers, may be the same. Similarly, by the order of the Supreme Soul, material nature fashions a particular type of body to a particular type of living entity so that he may work according to his past desires.

## Free will and predestination

Because the law of karma is universal, infallible and extremely rigorous, we may wonder whether we living entities have any freedom to act. The answer is yes. Free will is an inseparable quality of the soul. Because the individual soul is a tiny particle of the Supreme Soul, and the Supreme Soul possesses free will in fullness, free will is an intrinsic quality of each individual. It is never lost, but it may be limited due to our decisions.

For example, if someone violates the State laws, he risks being arrested and sent to jail. Once in jail, his freedom will be curtailed—but not nullified. The criminal cannot get on an airplane and fly to the Caribbean and lie on a beach, but he can lie on the bed in his cell. He has some freedom of action within the space granted to him by the prison authorities, and if he cooperates with the authorities, he is likely to be granted soon parole for good behavior. If he continues to break the rules, he is “predestined” to suffer the consequences and perhaps end up in solitary confinement, where his possibilities for action will be further reduced.

Free will allows us a certain radius of action, although always within the restrictions dictated by the laws of nature. Our freedom is never unlimited. We are obliged “to play by the rules of the game” which means within the framework determined by material nature. If we violate the rules, we have to suffer the consequences, the reactions to our actions. For example, a soup without salt is bland, but with too much salt it becomes inedible; There is a small margin regarding the amount of salt within which the flavor of the soup is acceptable, and this margin depends on the type of body. What is pleasant for one body is not necessarily pleasant for another.

Each body has its powers and limitations. Perhaps we would like to fly like a bird, but if we jump off a cliff, even if we open our arms like wings, we will not fly, but we will break our bones. We completely depend on the laws of nature, which are experienced in different ways depending on the body we inhabit. The human body cannot float in the air or survive underwater, but there are other bodies that offer these facilities: those of birds and fish. If we observe the different species, we can appreciate how each body has certain powers and abilities that distinguish it from another.

What distinguishes humans from other beings is their intelligence, but if a human being uses that intelligence to imitate the skills of birds and fish, building airplanes and submarines, he is ultimately not much more than a “sophisticated animal.” Still, his inventions are merely crude imitations of the originals provided by nature, acting on the Supreme Designer’s blueprint that provides designs infinitely more perfect than the most advanced inventions of man.

In fact, in the animal kingdom, we find many species that possess material abilities that are far superior to ours. Some animals see better than us, such as eagles and hawks, or bees that even perceive the ultraviolet spectrum. Others have much more acute ears, like bats or dolphins, which perceive ultrasonic waves. The smell of dogs is so sensitive that it can detect concentrations of a substance as low as one part per billion; bears can sleep for months, and elephants can eat hundreds of kilos of food a day. The great advantage of the human body is its developed consciousness and higher intelligence. If human beings do not use these capacities for spiritual cultivation, but to progress in those activities that they have in common with the animals—eating, sleeping, mating and defending—then it is inevitable that, due to their desires and corresponding actions (karma), they will have to accept lower life forms again.

A Vedic axiom says that the body that we have now is the result of our desires and actions in the past, while the body that we will have in the future will be the result of our wishes and actions in the present.

The following analogy illustrates this thesis. When a young man enters the university to study medicine, it is understood that, in the past, he successfully completed his studies at primary school and high school. If he didn’t have enough knowledge, he wouldn’t be admitted to medical school. In the present, his years in college will determine his future. If he dedicates himself to studies and passes all the exams, he will get his diploma or license that will allow him to practice as a doctor. If he spends his time just having fun, he will have to accept a job that requires less qualification and will be less paid. But even such a less favorable situation is not final or unalterable. At any time, he can take the initiative to expand his knowledge and improve his position in life.

The conclusion is that we have, in any circumstance, a margin of independence that allows us to decide how to act, but we must always remember that we live under the strict laws of material nature. Thinking that we can bypass those laws will only produce misery.

Decisions are made in the subtle body, specifically in the three sections of the mind: thinking, feeling and willing. It is the way we think, feel and desire that determines our way of acting. And our way of acting will determine our dense body and its environment.

Therefore, every yoga system has as its objective the purification of the subtle body, first of all, the mind, to elevate us to a higher state of existence.

## The law of karma from the point of view of action

In the Vedas, we find a detailed description of different actions and their results. The karma-kanòa section recommends: if we want to be rich, we must act in this way; if we want to be famous, we must act that way; if we want health, beauty or strength, we have to act like this, etc.

Therefore, when we see that some persons are rich, famous, beautiful, or strong, we can conclude that they have acted in the past in a specific way (magnanimous, charitable, pious, etc.) that allows them to reap now the fruits of their actions.

However, not determined is how they will use these advantages in their present lives. That depends on how they use their free will. Consequently, a rich, famous, or beautiful person is often not necessarily a “good person.”

The same principle applies to the undesirable. The Vedas recommend avoiding certain activities that would lead to illness, failure, or other misfortune. Following these recommendations will reward us with corresponding results.

## The law of karma from the point of view of reaction

If we look at the law of karma from the other side, it is logical to think that everything that happens to us in this life — be it pleasant or unpleasant—is a reaction to our actions in the past, either in this same life or in a previous lifetime. Therefore, it is not bad luck, blind chance or the will of cruel fate that plays tricks on us, but rather we are reaping the fruits of our own decisions.

For this reason, we sometimes meet people who, despite leading a healthy and pious life, receive severe blows from fate. But those who possess real knowledge and transcendental vision know that the root of their misery is found in their own heart. Therefore, they do not blame anyone, least of all God, for their situation. With the appropriate angle of vision, we can understand that everything that happens to us has its reason for being. It also contains a lesson and, properly accepted, serves to purify our existence and raise our consciousness.

## Evolution and devolution of consciousness

What is recommended in the Vedic literature is precisely the evolution of consciousness, since there is also the possibility of going in the opposite direction and descending to a lower level of life.

Two questions that repeatedly arise concerning reincarnation are 1) whether animals also obey the law of reincarnation and can become humans, and 2) whether the soul can pass from a human body to an animal body or even that of a plant.

Before answering these questions, let us remember the Vedic definition of “life”: “Living organisms show symptoms of life because they are animated by the vital force which is a particle of spiritual energy.” From this statement, it follows that the spiritual spark can dwell in any organism suitable for life, be it human, animal or plant. The soul is present even in the simplest forms, such as microbes or amoebas. In the Bhagavad-gita (2.24) it is stated, nityah sarva-gatah sthanur: “The soul is eternal and is present everywhere.” The only difference between humans and animals lies in the body they have, both subtle and gross, because according to the structure of the body, the soul exhibits its qualities. If a human being behaves in the present life like an animal, then it is only reasonable that nature will offer in the next life a body more appropriate to his level of consciousness which, in one sense, is not a punishment, but a favor.

The human form of life is a crossroads. It is the only type of body that offers the facility to make use of one’s free will and choose one path or another. All the others species do not have this power of choice; they simply follow the path of evolution that culminates eventually at the human level. In the human form, the consciousness of the soul is considered “in full bloom” because it has developed sufficiently to be able to distinguish between good and evil. An animal only follows his instinct, it is not involved by his acts in new karmic reactions. In the lower forms of life, such as those of animals, the conditioned soul rather pays the karmic debts incurred in the human form. Once its debts are cleared, the soul receives again a human body and with it a new opportunity to elevate itself—or degrade itself.

Srila Prabhupada explains in the Srimad-Bhagavatam (4.29.4):

Originally, the living entity is a spiritual being, but when he actually desires to enjoy this material world, he comes down. From this verse, we can understand that the living entity first accepts a body that is human in form, but gradually, because of his degraded activities, he falls into lower forms of life—into the animal, plant and aquatic forms. By the gradual process of evolution, the living entity again attains the body of a human being and is given another chance to get out of the process of transmigration. If he again misses his chance in the human form to understand his position, he is again placed in the cycle of birth and death in various types of bodies.

The human body enjoys a unique position among all forms of life because it gives the soul the power to determine its future. In the forms of plants and animals, this freedom does not exist. The human being does have the choice to act in accordance with the laws of the universe or to abuse his free will and violate them. If he acts against the laws of nature, he is responsible and has to accept the consequences.

Cosmic law is also known as dharma, the subtle divine law that establishes the values and norms that govern human society. The Vedas inform us, dharmam tu saksad bhagavat-pranitam. “Dharma is the law given by Bhagavan, the Supreme Personality of Godhead.” We cannot invent our own moral and ethical laws, just as we cannot invent our own natural laws. Dharma determines which acts are “legal” and which are “illegal,” and it is man’s responsibility to study these laws and act in harmony with them.

What really elevates the human form above all other life forms is a higher consciousness that enables a person to understand dharma and other spiritual concepts. If we put these principles into practice and follow a process of selfrealization, we can purify our existence to such a degree that we will be able to leave the cycle of repeated birth and death for good.

## Chapter 4. The End of Reincarnation

## Where are we going?

Ending the cycle of repeated births and deaths is the goal of any self-actualization process. Authentic human life begins the moment we seek answers to such essential questions as “Who am I? Why do we die? Why are we born? What do we do in this world? What is the purpose of life?

When Krsna explains in the Bhagavad-gita (13.8-12) the symptoms of true knowledge, he mentions, among others, janma-mrtyu-jara-vyadhi-duhkha-dosanu-darsanam:    “Always bear in mind the miseries of birth, death, old age and disease.” These four defects form an intrinsic part of material nature, and all living entities throughout the universe, without exception, are subject to them: a-brahma-bhuvanal lokah punar avartino ‘rjuna. “O Arjuna, from the highest planet to the lowest, all are places of suffering where birth and death are repeated” (Bg. 8.16). Naturally, the question comes to mind: Is there a solution to these fundamental problems?

Human life offers the conditioned soul the opportunity to wake up from its drowsy state in the lap of Maya (illusion) and seek a way out of its pitiful situation. Tamasi ma, jyotir gama, the Vedas exhort him: “Do not stay in the darkness, come out into the light.” Liberation (mukti) from material conditioning is the ultimate goal of all Eastern traditions. They offer us three main answers.

## 1. Nirvana

Buddhism proposes the concept of nirvana as the final state of liberation. Nirvana means “to end the sensations of pain and pleasure.” Prabhupada explains in a lecture given in New York in 1967:

Those who follow the nirvana philosophy, the Buddha philosophy, are liberated also, but not in the spiritual world, but in a marginal place called nirvana. Their material existence is finished, but their spiritual development is not there. So finishing material existence is not all. Just like a man is suffering from fever, and the fever subsides. That is not health. Fever subsides. That’s all right. Fever has subsided. But healthy life is when he will work as a healthy man. Simply saying, “No, no more fever,” no more fever, lying down on the bed, is like the nirvana stage. There is no fever, but he is not able to get up from the bed and work. So that is called nirvana. The fever is finished. That is called nirvana. So when material existence is finished, that is nirvana. But you have to go further. You have to develop further. Then your real, constitutional life as spirit soul will be manifested. So that is bhakti-marga, the path of devotional service.

Another term to describe Buddhist philosophy is sunyavada. Sunya means “nothing,” “void,” “zero,” and vada means “doctrine.” Many people think Buddhism is a religion, but it is not. For the Buddhist, there is no Supreme Being with whom to establish a relationship (lat. religere). Neither is there an individual soul who is spiritual and eternal. At its core, Buddhist philosophy is atheistic and resembles the materialistic concept of existence. Science teaches that life comes from a combination of material elements and that we cease to exist when the body decomposes, becoming nothing. The Buddhists go a step further because they understand that after the disappearance of the gross body, the subtle body remains; therefore, Buddhists recognize karma and samsara and strive to overcome all material desires to dissolve the mental system and with it existence itself. The result will be: the void. Thus, the Buddhist process aims to dismantle the material body in all its aspects and return the ingredients to their original state: earth to earth, water to water, fire to fire, etc. They aim at dissolving body and mind until there is nothing left. In other words, the ultimate goal of Buddhism is the extinction of one’s existence.

## 2. Brahman

A philosophy similar to Buddhism is Mayavada, or Advaita, the doctrine of monism, or impersonalism. The only difference is that Mayavadis recognize that something remains after the dissolution of the body: consciousness. All impersonalist philosophers propose, with slight nuances, the same philosophy: “The Absolute Truth is undifferentiated consciousness, an impersonal, non-dual, indivisible and non-transformable entity, without form, without name, without attributes, and without qualities. Some call it “the Cosmic Consciousness”, “the Unmanifested”, “the Self”, “the One Life”, “the Eternal Light” or “Brahman”.

For the Mayavadis, the concept of a personal God and the manifestation of individual consciousness in the form of individual souls are mere reflections of the Absolute Consciousness. they do not have their own and eternal existence. “Ultimately,” the impersonalist says, “There is no difference between Brahman and jiva. They are one. The apparent differences are only external and illusory, they are not essential.”

The impersonalist philosophy maintains that everything manifested, including our individuality as persons, is a product of maya, illusion, and does not really exist. The only reality is Brahman. As an answer to the question why there are different manifestations, if everything is one, the impersonalists offer the example of the sky and a vessel. They compare Brahman with the infinite sky and the individual with the limited space within a vessel. As long as the individual is covered by the material body, he appears to have a separate existence, but when the vessel is broken, its content, the self, which is actually a part of the outer sky, merges with the whole of the sky.

To refute this concept, the personalists ask: ‘If Brahman is non-dual, non-transformable and indivisible, where does illusion come from and how can it cover part of Brahman which is indivisible? The example of the sky and the vessel is not valid, because if something other than the non-dual One is accepted, then Brahman does not remain without transformation. And if it is argued, to maintain the non-transformable character of Brahman, that it is only maya, illusion, which acts, then a second, active principle is introduced, which faces the inactive One. In any case, maya covers, divides and creates a shadow representation of a desire-free Brahman—a concept absolutely incompatible with the non-dual character of Brahman.

The theories of the Mayavadis do not agree with the conclusions of the Vedic scriptures that affirm that the ultimate concept of the Absolute Truth is personal. The Absolute Truth is simultaneously personal and impersonal, but the origin of the impersonal aspect is the Supreme Person who possesses an infinite variety of potencies, among which there are three main ones: the internal energy (spiritual), the marginal energy (spiritual sparks), and the external energy (material nature). In the revealed scriptures such as the Srimad-Bhagavatam we find a more detailed explanation about these three energies.

In the Srimad-Bhagavatam (1.2.11) we also find the following verse on the nature of the Absolute Truth:

*vadanti tat tattva-vidas tattvam yaj jnanam advayam*  
*brahmeti paramatmeti bhagavan iti sabdyate*

**“Learned transcendentalists who know the Absolute Truth call this non-dual substance Brahman, Paramatma or Bhagavan.”**

The Absolute Truth is one. There cannot be two absolute truths. But depending on how we look at it, this single truth has different aspects. The example of a city may clarify the idea. From a distance we can only see a diffuse mass, from closer we can see that the mass is composed of several elements, and when we enter the city, we see that there are buildings, parks, streets, markets, inhabitants and a great variety of activities. The impersonalist is blinded by the effulgence emanating from the Supreme Person, and sees only the diffuse light of Brahman; the yogi penetrates Brahman and distinguishes the omnipresent Supersoul, the Paramatma, in the heart of every living entity and in every atom. The bhakta, the devotee, understands that the origin of Brahman and Paramatma is Bhagavan, the Supreme Person and that there is a bond of love between the infinite Supreme Soul and the infinitesimal individual soul.

## 3. Goloka

“If the finite can know the Infinite, then the Infinite would be finite,” argue the impersonalists. The reply is: “If the Infinite cannot reveal itself to the finite, then it would not be infinite.” The Absolute Truth is infinite and inconceivable, it is both personal and impersonal, and it is also allpowerful and completely independent. If He wants to reveal Himself, who can stop Him?

The revealed scriptures contain information about the Infinite and describe His nature, qualities, names, desires, etc. The Brahma-samhita (5.1) offers the following definition:

*isvarah paramah krsnah sac-cid-ananda-vigrahah*  
*anadir adir govindah sarva-karana-karanam*

**“Krsna, who is known as Govinda, is the supreme controller. He has an eternal, blissful, spiritual body. He is the origin of all. He has no other origin, for He is the prime cause of all causes.”**

In the Bhagavad-gita (10.8) Krsna says, aham sarvasya prabhavo mattah sarvam pravartate, “I am the source of all material and spiritual worlds. Everything emanates from Me.” He also affirms that He is the basis of the impersonal Brahman: brahmano hi pratisöhaham (Bg. 14.27).

All the great spiritual authorities and acaryas (teachers), such as Madhvacarya, Ramanujacarya, Vyasadeva and even Saìkaracarya, the founder of the Mayavada school, recognize Krsna as the Supreme Being. In his Gita-bhasya, Saìkara comments that Bhagavan Sri Krsna is transcendental to the material cosmos (narayanah paro ‘vyaktat), and at the end of his life, Sankaracarya said to his disciples: bhaja govindam bhaja govindam bhaja govindam muòha-mate, prapte sannihite karana, hita kala-marane na hi na hi raksati òukrn-karane. “You fools, worship Govinda, worship Govinda, worship Govinda because you are talking about philosophy and grammar and many other things, but when death captures you, your grammar and your juggling will not save you.”

When Krsna speaks in the Bhagavadgita, the author, Vyasadeva, always uses the words sri-bhagavan uvaca: “Sri Bhagavan, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, says.” He could have said sri-krsna uvaca, but to emphasize that it is the Supreme Person who is speaking, not an ordinary person, he uses the term bhagavan. Bhaga means “opulence” and van means “one who possesses”, indicating that Krsna is the person who possesses all opulences, called vibhutis (wealth, strength, fame, beauty, knowledge and renunciation), in fullness:

aisvaryasya samagrasya viryasya yasasah sriyah (Visnu Purana 6.5.47).

Anyone who owns one or more of these opulences is attractive. The Supreme Lord possesses these opulences fully, and therefore the name Krsna is the most appropriate name for Bhagavan, the possessor of all opulences and potencies because Krsna means “all-attractive.”

Krsna explains in the Bhagavad-gita (15.7) that all souls come from Him: mamaivamso jiva-loke jiva-bhutah sanatanah: “All jivas are My eternal fragmental parts.” And as parts, they have a natural relationship with the whole, and that relationship is one of love and devotion. The Sri Caitanya-caritamrta, another Vedic scripture, says: jivera ‘svarupa’ haya—krsnera ‘nitya-dasa’, (Madhya 20.108).. “The jiva is by constitution an eternal servant of Krsna.” But being a servant of God is not the same as being a servant of a master of this world.

The wonderful thing about devotional service is that it provides the servant with the same unlimited bliss as the master—one of the mysteries of the transcendental plane.

The nature of the soul is to be attracted to the Supreme Soul, its origin, but when the soul is covered by the gross and subtle bodies, the influence of illusion makes it forget its eternal love relationship with the Supreme. Srila Prabhupada explains in the Srimad-Bhagavatam (3.26.8):

If he so desires, the spirit soul can change this conditional life of dualities by choosing to serve Krsna. The living entity is the cause of his own suffering, but he can also be the cause of his eternal happiness. When he wants to engage in Krsna consciousness, a suitable body is offered to him by the internal potency, the spiritual energy of the Lord, and when he wants to satisfy his senses, a material body is offered. Thus it is his free choice to accept a spiritual body or a material body, but once the body is accepted, he has to enjoy or suffer the consequences.

The process of bhakti-yoga, the yoga of devotional love, aims to awaken the natural inclination of the soul towards feeling divine love and help develop its spiritual body to serve Krsna in Goloka, the spiritual world.

Love is the real religion of the soul. Actually, the soul, in its pure state, is a luminous being, an inhabitant of the spiritual world, where life is eternal, full of knowledge and full of bliss thanks to the loving relationship with the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Krsna, or Govinda.

The love we know in this world is a mere reflection of that divine love. It is fragile and temporary, and if not broken during one’s life, it will break at the time of death. Even so, being a shadow of the original substance, it is so powerful and unique that humans celebrate it as the most wonderful thing in the world. Millions of songs praise love, countless novels describe love, and zillions of movies have love at the center of their plot. Love is beyond calculation. It exists on a plane that eludes all control of external forces. We can observe the affection of a mother even among birds, wild animals and insects. Love is present everywhere in the creation, and its intuitive force makes the world go round. Nobody knows where love comes from, but it appears whenever and wherever it wants. Love is free and independent. It is an inexplicable force that even overcomes reason and intellectual power.

Divine love for the personification of love, Krishna, is the ultimate goal of life.

## Concluding Words

Bhakti, divine devotional love, is not an imaginary sentimental state of delusional adepts. The bhakti-yoga process is a true science of spiritual development that is based on the teachings of great self-realized souls who have come and continue to come to this world to help conditioned souls free themselves from their entanglement in the material energy.

Even God Himself comes from time to time to spread spiritual knowledge. The most prominent example is the Bhagavadgita, spoken by Krsna Himself over 5,000 years ago. The most recent avatara of Krsna is Sri Caitanya Mahaprabhu, who appeared about 500 years ago in Bengal to teach the process of bhakti by chanting the holy name of the Lord.

His devotees and followers have written many books on the science of bhakti-yoga and they continue to spread this same knowledge through the guru-parampara, a chain of teachers and disciples descending from Sri Caitanya.

Today there are hundreds of thousands of followers of Sri Caitanya Mahaprabhu all over the world who teach the path of bhakti through their example. The most prominent follower in recent times was the founder of the Krsna consciousness movement, His Divine Grace A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada.

Srila Prabhupada came to the West in 1965, at the age of 69, and propagated the process of bhaktiyoga, teaching it with his exemplary life as a pure devotee of Krishna.

Until    his departure from this world in 1977, he traveled around the world 14 times on lecture tours, bringing the teachings of Lord Krsna to hundreds and thousands of people on six continents. Men and women of all walks of life accepted his message and, with theirs help, Prabhupada established ISKCON centers and projects all over the world.

Under his inspiration, Krsna devotees established temples, rural communities, and educational institutions. Prabhupada returned to India on several occasions, wanting to nurture the roots of Krsna consciousness in his home country, where he caused a revival of the bhakti tradition. In India, he opened dozens of temples, including large centers at the holy sites of Vrndavana and Mayapura.

However, Prabhupada’s most significant contribution is undoubtedly his books. He authored more than 70 volumes of philosophical works in the krsna-bhakti tradition, translations of revealed scriptures highly respected by scholars for their authority, depth, fidelity to tradition, and clarity of meaning. Several of his works are used as textbooks in numerous university courses. His writings have been translated into more than 100 languages.

The most prominent titles are: The Bhagavad-gita As It Is, the Srimad-Bhagavatam (published in 30 volumes), and the Sri Caitanya-caritamrta (published in 17 volumes).

Today, the International Society for Krishna Consciousness has over 400 centers around the world, where anyone is welcome to hear about Krsna and take part in the spiritual practices of bhakti-yoga.

We invite the reader to contact the nearest ISKCON center to deepen the knowledge presented in this essay.
