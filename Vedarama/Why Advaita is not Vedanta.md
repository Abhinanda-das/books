# Why Advaita is not Vedanta?

## Why Advaita is not Vedanta

The reasons for that are mentioned below. At first, I’d like to express my gratitude for the fact that, like many so-called Vedantists, I was happily brainwashed by Advaita philosophy for many years. Indeed, in the early going, my belief that the world is mithya strengthened my sense of renunciation of the world and helped me achieve success during my six-month stay in a meditation retreat in Canada.

But 15 years later, while enrolled as a student of Sanskrit grammar in a school in India, I witnessed the axe of wisdom chop down the Advaita tree. This happened as I listened to the morning class of a devout student during the morning routine of spiritual activities. That devotee explained to me the first of the following reasons why Advaita-Vedanta is not Vedanta:

1\. In Bhagavad-gita, Sri Krsna is said to have spoken thus: *jheyam yat tat pravaksyami yaj jhatvamrtam asnute \| anadi mat-param brahma na sat tan nasad ucyate \|\|* ([13.13](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/13.13)). Sankara, however, twists the rules of Sanskrit phonetics and makes the wording “*anadimat param brahma*” (Brahman is beginningless and supreme) instead of “*anadi mat-param brahma*” (Brahman is beginningless and is inferior to Me). Although it is true that in Sanskrit, the spoken form is given superiority over the written form, so that in this instance the wording “*anadimat*” may seem to be a valid grammatical form although all the texts of Bhagavad-gita read “*anadi mat-param*”, the wording “*anadimat*” cannot, in fact, be made, because nowhere in the canon of Sanskrit literature and nowhere in the rules of grammar has the suffix *mat\[up\]*, which is expressive of possession, ever been used in a *bahuvrihi* compound. Rather, the key word here is *mat-param* (inferior to Me), a *bahuvrihi* compound in which mat is not *mat \[up\]* but a replacement of *asmad* (I); the literal meaning of *mat-param* is “Brahman is one whose superior is I.”

The significance is that the concept of Paramesvara (God), symbolized as Sri Krsna in the Gita and as Sri Rama in Ramayana, is superior to the concept of Brahman, whereas in Advaita, the concept of Isvara (controller) is inferior to the concept of Brahman.

2\. The textual analysis of the Gita verse [13.13](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/13.13) helps us understand another crucial text said to be spoken by Sri Krsna therein: *brahmano hi pratisthaham* (14.27). Sankara interprets *brahmanah* in the ablative case (I am the foundation from Brahman), whereas, in view of text [13.13](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/13.13), only the genitive case is acceptable: “I am the foundation of Brahman.” That only the genitive applies here is further substantiated by the fact that all the other words in the verse are in the genitive.

The verse reads: *brahmano hi pratisthaham amritasyavyayasya ca \| sasvatasya ca dharmasya sukhasyaikantikasya ca \|\|* ([14.27](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/14.27)). The word hi means “because” (“because I am the founda­tion of Brahman”) and is the explanation of the previous verse, which explains that the practice of *bhakti-yoga* is necessary to achieve the goal of meditation, self-realization: *mam ca yo ’vy- abhicdrena bhakti-yogena sevate \| sa gunan samatityaitan brahma-bhuyaya kalpate \|\|* ([14.26](https://vedarama.com/en/books/bg/14.26)).

The following concepts derived from the above illustrate further differences between Advaita and the real Vedanta:

- Sakti (Maya) belongs to Paramesvara, not to Brahman.
- Sakti is not only material; the Sakti which is Paramesvara’s own power is transcendental, as evidenced in this text: *parasya saktir vividhaiva sruyate svabhaviki*, “It is heard that His tran­scendental potency is manifold and is inherent in Him” (Svetasvatara Upanisad [6.8](https://vedarama.com/en/books/sveta-bs/6.8)).

3\. The canonical texts of Vedanta philosophy are these three sets of scriptures, collectively called the Prasthana-trayi: the Bhagavad-gita, the Vedanta-sutra, and the 11 major Upanisads. This favorite mantra of Advaita: “*brahma satyam jagan mithya*”, is in Niralamba Upanisad, which is not one of the 11 major Upanishads and is, therefore, not accepted as a canonical text. The notion of “*jagan mithya*” is easily rejectable by logic too: The world is illusory, but is not an illusion like falsely seeing a rope as a snake, because even after understanding that Brahman is the sub­stratum of everything, the world doesn’t disappear, whereas after seeing the rope, the illusion of a snake disappears. Even after achieving self-realization, the jivan-muktas operate in the world. The existence of the world is called *vyavaharika-satta* (material existence).

4\. In Vedanta-sutra, Sankara rejects the entire section (*adhikarana*) starting from the aphorism: *kartā śāstrārthavattvāt* (2.3.33). Sankara writes: *yat tūktaṁ śāstrārthavattvādibhir hetubhiḥ svābhāvikam ātmanaḥ kartṛtvam iti tan na*, “It was stated with several reasons, beginning from *śāstrārthavattvāt* (VS 2.3.33), that the doership of the soul is inherent. That is wrong” (Sariraka-bhasya 2.3.40). Sankara rejects the notion that *jivatman* has any degree of doership, such as willpower. In short, Sankara doesn’t accept that the *jivatman* is both identical with and distinct from Brahman. In truth, Vedanta is a philosophy of paradox (*bhedābheda*).

No one can claim to rightfully expound Vedanta philosophy while rejecting a portion of the Prasthana-trayi.

That said, Advaita is the perfect philosophy for hardcore meditators. I have more respect for meditators in a meditation retreat than I have for anyone who merely has the perfect intellectual understanding of Vedanta.

The purpose of studying Advaita is the attainment of *moksa*, liberation. This goal may seem out of reach. Nonetheless, in my view every article discussing Advaita should incite the readers to plan to go meditate in a meditation retreat someday, because these intermediary goals too are well worth achieving:

1. Realising that the soul is the witness (*saksin*) of the mind; in other words, having the expe­rience that the soul is distinct from the mind and the body and that the mind is distinct from the body;
2. Attaining a high state of meditation;
3. Reaching the state of trance (*samadhi*); it is attained by many devout meditators.

The TM centers of the organization founded by Maharishi Mahesh Yogi are well-suited for the above and for the spiritual fun of yogic flying / frog hopping.

Buddhism is higher than Vedanta in the sense that it provides the means to achieve the experience of unconditional love for all beings. Several Buddhist centers, such as the Vipassana Meditation Centers, offer free ten-day retreats to anyone desirous of meditating many times per day according to their guidelines.

The core teaching in the real Vedanta philosophy is this: The *jivatman* has the nature of tran­scendental Rasa, just like Paramesvara, according to the canonical Upanishadic text: *raso vai sah. rasam hy evayam labdhvanand bhavati*, “Paramatman is Rasa (rapture). Upon obtaining Rasa, one becomes blissful” (Taittiriya Upanisad [2.7](https://vedarama.com/en/books/taittiriya/2.7)). Rasa is the combination of *ananda* (bliss) and *camatkara* (astonishment). The material Rasa has many names throughout the world, such as mojo, excitement, groove, enthusiasm, and aesthetic delight.
