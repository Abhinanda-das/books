## Chapter I - The Bhāgavata Background

## Early Accounts

Among the Āgama divisions devoted to the worship of Vishṇu ( also known as Nārāyaṇa and Vāsudeva),Pāñchrātra is the most popular,especially in south India and more especially after the days of Rāmānuja 1017-1137 A.D.). The precise origin of this Āgama division is difficult to discern,but it appears to have been crystallized within the framework of the Bhāgavata cult which was widespread all over the country even in the couple of centuries prior to the Chritian era.diverse are the details that have entered into the development of Vaishṇavism,and numerous are the literary and inscriotional references which suggest the antiquity of the Bhāgavata cult as also its various dimensions.

The Bhāgavata cult centres round Nārāyana as the supreme Godhead (Bhagavān), and identifies him with Vāsudeva (Krishna), of the Vṛshṇi (or Sāttvata) clan. The latter was undoubtedly a human hero,a kshttriya, who was deified probably during his own lifetime. Even Pāṇini (about 700 B.C.) refers to the Vṛshṇi tribe (4,1,114 ‘rshyandhaka-vṛshṇi-kurubhyaś cha’),and his commentator Patañjali (about 400 B.C.) mentions Vāsudeva,(Kṛshṇa), Baladeva (saṁkarshaṇa) and Vishvaksena as heroes of this tribe. Pāṇini was also aware of the divine status accorded to Vāsudeva among them,who was a kshattriya (4,3,98 ‘Vāsudevārjunābhyām vun’), for patañjali explains that the name of Vāsudeva has been mentioned earlier because of his revered character and not because he was a kshattriya hero (‘atha vā naishā kshatriyākhyā, saṁjñaishā tatra-bhavatah’).Patañjali also knew that another hero of the Vṛshṇi clan Saṁkarshaṇa was Vāsudeva’s brother (2,2,24, ‘saṁkarshaṇa-dvītīyasya balam kṛshṇasya vardhatām’). The epic Mahābhārata informs us that it was Saṁkarshaṇa who taught the doctrine of the Sāttvatas ( Sāttvata-vidhi) when the Dvāpara age was drawing to aclose and the Kali-age dawning (‘dvāparasya yugasyānte ādau Kali-yugasya cha,sākshāt saṁkarshaṇāl labdhvā sāttvatam vidhimīī.. ..’)

We learn from several inscriptions of the second and first centuries B.C. that Vāsudeva and saṁkarshaṇa received worship in temples built for them. The Nāgari (near mewār) inscription,for instance,mentions that the prince Sarva-tāta,Gājāyana,son of Pārāśarī,built a stone temple to these two divinities,who are described here as ‘unconquered masters of all beings’ and as ‘adorable’ (El,22,p.204) :

It is interesting that in this inscription the king calls himself a ‘bhāgavata’ (viz.affilliated to the cult of Bhagavān),who had also performed the ritual known as aśva-medha (prescribed for kings in the Karma-kāṇḍa of the Veda). He thus belonged to the Vedic fold,while he was also a devotee of the Sāttvata heros.

The Besnagar (Vidiśā, near Gwālior in Madhya Pradesh) inscription (of the century B.C.) in Brāhmī characters on the shaft of the raised column (garuḍa-dhvaja) that was erected to honour Vāsudeva, ‘the god of gods’ (deva-deva),also claims that heliodorus (son of Dion,an Indo-greek), the ambassador sent by the King Antiakidas to the court of the ruler of Vidiśā,Kāsīputra- bhāgabhadra,was a ‘bhāgavata’ (D.C. Sirkar. Select inscriptions, pp 90-91). The early Gupta monarchs styled themselves ‘bhāgavatas’ and ‘paramabhāgavatas’ (e.g.Samudra –gupta, Chandragupta II alias Vikramāditya, Kumāra-gupta I alias Mahendrāditya) in their gold coins. There is an inscription dated 402 A.D. at Udayagiri (near Bhilsa), which records the recontruction of a ‘Bhāgavata’ shrine.āgavata’ shrine.

It is probable that initially five heroes of the Vṛshṇi clan (the Sāttvatas,the forerunners of the bhāgavatas) were together deified and adored. The cult developed on this basis was known as ‘Vīra-vāda’. That there were also shrines to these heroes becomes evident from an inscription discovered in the Morā well 9 near Mathurā),which records that a lady toshā by name got constructed a stone temple to “five adorable and worship-worthy heroes of the Vrshni tribe” whose icons were also got made,during the reign of the great kshatrapa Śoḍāśa-svāmi : (E1,24,pp 194 f)

The ‘five heroes’ who are described here as the ‘bhagavatas’ (the adorable figures,objects of worship for the bhāgavatas) were also ‘heroes of a family’ according to Vāyu-purāna (‘pañchaite vaṁśa-vīrāh’) : saṅkarashaṇa (son of the Vṛshṇi chieftains Vasudeva by Rohiṇī), Vāsudeva (son of the same Vasudeva by Devakī), Pradyumna (son of Vāsudeva by Rukmīṇī),Sāṁba (son of Vāsudeva by Jāṁbavatī) and Aniruddha (son of Pradyumna). Omitting Sāṁba,the other ‘heroes’ have entered into the Bhāgavata ideology as ‘vyūhas’, which will be discussed later.

But among the Vṛshṇi-heroes Vāsudeva and Saṁkarshana became popular divinities,and shrines came to be erected for the two of them together,as has already been mentioned. And the importance ascribed to Vāsudeva was even more widely accepted.The celebrated lexicon Amara-kosha (1,1,18-30) mentions Vāsudeva first and then Saṁkarshaṇa,despite Vāsudeva being the younger brother. The inscription recording the erection of the Besnagar column by the Indo-Greek ambaaasdor Heliodorus mentions only Vāsudeva and describes him as ‘deva-deva’. The fragmentary record of the Śaka-satrap Śoḍāśa (son of Rājūvala), who caused a sacred shrine (mahāsthāna ) to be constructed at Mathurā,mentions that the shrine was only for the ‘worshipful Vāsudeva’. The inscription dated the first century B.C. found in Burhikhar village (in Bilāspur,Madhya Pradesh) records the gift of a four-armed image of ‘Bhagavān’ (viz. Vāsudeva) by a lady Prajāvatī : the image,two feet and a half foot tall,was known as ‘chaturbhujī-bhagavān’,carrying conch and mace in the extra hands,the normal hands being joined in the act of adoration (bhakti,añjali). Saṁkarshaṇa was thus pushed to the background ,and was made to occupy the second position in the vyūha-scheme.

## Sāttvata-Tantra

The pānchartāna as an āgama division was affiliated to the bhāgavata cult becomes evident from the decription of Pāñcharatra as ‘bhāgavata-śāstra’, ‘sāt-tvata- saṁhitā’ and ‘vasudeva-mata’. Vāmana-saṁhitā explains that Pāñcharātra is called ‘bhāgavata-śāstra’ because it was the ‘bhagavat’(viz. Vāsudava) that first propounded it.

The same taxt interprets the expression ‘bhagavat’ to mean the possesser of ‘bhaga’ which signifies the concrete manifes-tation of six divine attributes: omniscience( jñāna ), omnipotency (śakit ), unhindered energy (bala), absoulate Sovereitgnty (aiśvarya), indefatigable valour (vīrya) and inexplicable splendour (tejas). The term refers to Vāsudeva.

According to Purushottama-saṁhitā (1,10-12), the Pāñcharātra system is known as ‘sāttvata-tantra’, ‘bhagavchhāstra’, ‘ekāyana-veda’and mūla-veda’.

The doctrine of the Pāñcharātra school claims that it was propounded by Vāsudeva himself for the benfit of mankind ; hence it is ‘vāsudeva-mata’ (viz. the view of Vāsudeva). We read in Īśvara-saṁhitā (1,54-55):

This verse describes the Pāñcharātra literature, which is in the verse form (in anushtubh metre), and which is elaborate in some details and succinct in others; what isbrieflytold in some texts is elaborated in others; and what has already been dealt with in great detail in other texts is but condensed in some (samāsa-vyāsa). And what was first promulgated by Vāsudeva was later expounded by Behmā, Rudra, India and other gods and sages. But the authenticity of the doctrine is derived from Vāsudeva himself being the original spokesman. Vāsudeva is the supreme godhead, according to the Sāttvatas, and hence this doctrine is known as Vāsudeva-mata (the view of Vāsudeva) and Sāttvata-vvidhi (the injunctions of the great Sāttvata herogod, Vāsudeva).

The identification of this Sāttvata-Vāsudeva with Vishnu of the Vedic corpus helped the bhāgavata cult gain a wide appeal.

And Pārameśvara-saṁhitā (1,73-74) traces the origin of the bhāgavata doctrine to the teachnigs of the divine sages Nara and Nārāyana in the hermitage of Badarī for the welfare of all mankind, which found the sāttvata-hero Vāsudeva as its only exponent; and from Vāsudeva it spread among the people.

The same text characterizes the Sāttvata-vidhi or Vāsudeva-mata as ‘ekāyana-veda’ (viz. the Veda which is the only path salvation) (1,56). Purushottama-saṁhitā (1,12) also proclaims that this is the only way, and that there is no other.

Śrī-praśna-saṁhitā (2,38-39) explains that Pāncharātra is the path that leads to the salvation of individuals; that this path takes the form of worship of Godhead; and that this is the ageless path shown in the Vedic corpus. According to this text, the Pāñchrrātra rests on the head of the Veds (i.e. is superior to the Vedas).

This is the thrust of Vāmana-saṁjotā also, which text pointedly suggest that it is only in this Pañcharātra that salvation is the only goal aspired for, whereas the Vedic corpus seeks to promote the other three values of life lso (viz. tri-varga : dharma, artha and Kāma); hence the superior merit of the ‘ekāyana’, which teaches the worship of god who has descended on earth and among mankind, for the sake of realizing final emancipation:

This position also brings out the difference between the ekāyana and the Veda. The Vedas are meant only for the persons who are eligible to their study and who are qualified to perform the rituals presbribed therein, while the ekāyana is meant for all alike (sarveshām), for all are eligible to realize final emancipation. Indeed, it was claimed by the Pāñcharātra authorities that this ekāyana is the original veda(mūla-veda), the veda (the word of God) that existed before the divisions of Rg, Yajus and Sāman came about. Utpala’s Spanda-Kārika cities this view :

The Pāñcharātra texts proceed to describe the extant Vedas as only large branches of the huge ekāyana tree, for the ekāyana ws propounded by Vāsudeva himself who is the source of the world: it is in fact the original Veda.

(cf. Pārameśvara-saṁhitā, 1,76-77 and Purushottama-saṁhitā, 1,14-15)

Nārāyana or Vāsudeva was not a mere kshattriya, as both Pānini and Patañka;o were well aware; besides being a here of the Sāttvatas (or of the Vṛshṇi clan), he is celebrated as a wise man withmarked spiritual attainmnts. Nārāyana is the Vedic seer(ṛshi), who was responsible for the famous ‘Purusha-sūkta’ of the Ṛg-vedic corpus(10,90). The Śatapatha-brāhmaṇa refers to Purusha- Nārāyaṇa as seer-god who performed the pāñcharātra-sattra and attaind superiority over all beings and became all beings himself (13, 6,1)

He is, according to Ahirbudhnaya-saṁhitā (38,39), the one who bore the form of the sacrifice ( Yajña-rūpa-dhara) and the ‘sattra’ in person(sattra-purusha). The ‘sattra’ that he is said to have performed is a community-sacrifice extended to five days (including the night before the first day), involving no professional priests, but the Yajamānas themselved acting for the priests.

Nārāyaṇa becoming all beings and lording over all beings has an interesting aspect. The word ‘nara’ from which the word ‘nārāyaṇa has been formed, means the individual man; ‘nārāyaṇa’as the d ·elling place for all the individuals (narānāmayanam) signifies the collective tribal existence. The individual’s extension into the group, and assumption of universal form is the symbolism behind the ‘’Viśva-rūpa appearance in the Bhagavad-gītā, cha. 11. The same idea occurs in the Mahānārāyaṇopanishat.

The importance atrtached to Purusha-sūlta(Ṛg-veda, 10,90) and to the ‘sahasra-śīrsha’ section of the Mahānārāyaṇopanishat by the adherents of the Pāñcharātra school is thus underrstandable. Nārāyaṇa is for them the father-figure. His identification with Vāsudeva-krshṇa is not far-fetched. We have Kṛshṇa-āṅgirasa as a Vedic ṛshi (Ṛg-veda 8,85-87, and 10,42-44). And Kṛshṇa as the disciple of Ghora-āṅgirasa is mentioned in Chhāndogya-Upanishat(3,16). Bhāgavata-gītā, which is claim;ed to be the definitive text of the bhāgavata-cult, not only identifies Kṛshṇa with Vāsudeva (11,50 and 18,74) and Vāsudeva as a member of the Vṛshṇi clan (10,37), but in keeping with the Śatapatha-brāhmaṇa account of Nārāyaṇa becoming all beings after having performed the pāñcharātra-sattra, described Vāsudeva as all (‘vāsudevas sarvam itl’ ,7, 19).

The epic Mahābhārata (Sānti-parva, 45,1-10) also contains an account of how yudhishṭhira saw Kṛshṇa in a state of spiritual composure and meditative absorption. The senses were withdrawn, the mind was at rest, the body was still like a log of wood, a wall or a rock; he resembled a lamp that was steadily buming in a spot free from breeze. He was responding to the thoughts of dying Bhīshma who lay on the couch of arrows; his spirit had gone to the pīous and wise hero of many battles. This is a practical application of Vāsudeva being all things (‘Vāsudevas sarvam iti’).

## ‘Nārāyaṇīya

The ‘Nārāyaṇīya’ section of Mahābhārata (Śānti-parva, moksha-dharma-parva, chapters334 to348) may rightly be regarded as the springboard of the bhāgavata cult. There is a long discourse which underlines the need for having Vāsudeva as the supreme spirit and object of devotion and knowledge(339,25).

It also contains in bare outline in bare outline the vyūha-theory, which forms the bedrock of bhāgavta(or pāñcharātra outlook (339,32-41, 340,28-33;343,14-18) and enumeratesthe ten incarmatory forms(prādurbhāvas) of Vishṇu (339, 78-790;

The three rāmas mentioned here are Paraśu-rāma, Dāśarathi-rāma and Bala-rāma(who substitutes for the buddha). And later in the same chapter Kṛshṇa has been described as a Sāttvata (104).

The account of the origin of pāñcharātra given in this section of the epic is briefly as follows : After visiting the sages nara and Nārāyaṇa in the holy hermitage of ‘Badarī in the Himālayan heights, and at the behest of sage Nārāyaṇa, Nārada went to Śveta-dvīpa which was situated on the northern side of the mythical Milky Ocean (Kshīrābdhi)). The denizens of this unusual island were equally unusual. Although human beings (“narānśvetān’, ‘mānavān’), they were invisible to mortal eye. They were free from physical urges like hunger, thirst and sleep and devoid of sense-organs. They were absolutely bereft of all sins and stains. They had bodies which were strong like diamond and minds that were perfectly tranquil and serene. They shone like moonlight and were ever iln a blissful state.

They were in complete and uninterrupted communion with Godhead, (nārāyaṇa), and their devotion was exclusive and absolute (ekānta). They are frequently described as ‘ekāntin’, ‘ekānta-bhāvoopagatā’, ‘ekānta-gata-buddhi’, ‘ekānteshu samāhitā’ and ‘eka-bhāvam-anuvratā’ ; and also as ‘bhakta’, ‘bhāgavata’, ‘pāñcharātrī, pañcha-kālajña’ and ‘nārāyṇa-para’. There can be little doubt they were the model pāñcharātradevotees.

Nārada, however, was able to sight them because he himself was an ‘ekāntin’ (339, 13, ‘na cha drakshyati’ kaśchana, ṛtehyaikāntika-śreshṭhāt, tvam chaivānkantikottamah’). Nārada desired an exculsive vision of Godhead (tvām didṛkshuh ekānta-darśanāya’), Nārāyana manifested his form as encompassing the entire universe (‘viśva-rūpa-dhṛk’). in Nārada’s eulogy of Godhead with two hundred names (ch. 337), we come across expressions like ‘pañcha- yajña- (65), ‘pañcha-kāla-kartṛ-pati’ (66), ‘pañcha-rātrika’ (67), ‘sāṁkhya-yoga’ (78), ‘pañcha-mahā-kalpa’ (114), ‘Vaikhānasa’(117), and ‘Vāsudeva’(147). There is curiously no mentionhere of Lakshmī as Nārāyaṇa’s consort (although there is a name ‘Lakshmyāvāsa’, 142, but thisdoes not mena anything more than prosperity in the context), of his vyūhas or of his prādurbhāvas.

Nārada obtains from Nārāyaṇa the pāñcjrātra teachings on this occasion. In the words of Bhīshma (339,111-112):

Thus the pāñcjarātra doctrines were first propounded by Nārāyaṇa himself, to Nārada, and were later communicated by Nārada to the sages in the world of Brahmā, before they descended to mankind in a successive lne (‘pāraṁparya-gatam hyetat’, 339, 141).

There is an interlude here about the monarch Uparichara (chap.336), who was also a great devotee of Nārāyaṇa (nārāyaṇa- bhakta). He lived according to ‘ Sāttvata-vidhi’ (‘sarvah sāttvatam-āsthāya vidhim chakre samāhitah’, 335,24); and in his residence gathered the eminent knowere of the ‘pāñcharātra (‘pāñcharātra-vido mukhyās gehemahātmanah’, 25). There is also a mention here of how the pāñcharātra system was crystallized (28) by the seven sages (called collectively ‘chitra- shikhaṇḍinah’) for the welfare of all mankind (‘loklān saṁchintya manasā, hitam anuttaman’): Marīchi, Atri, Āṅgirasa, Pulastya, Pulaha, Kratu and Vaśishṭha.

The account in the epic closes with an eulogy of the ekāntins as the best among the devotees, for they worship no other god and have no other interest; their devotion to god is altogether selfless.

God accepts the worship done by them and delights all of them; they reach the highest (viz. emancipation, which is god himself). If only the world were filled by these ekāntins, who are nonviolent in their approach and who cherish the good of all beings, there would indeed be the dawn of Kṛra-yuga (even while the kali-yuga is on). But few are the ekāntins.

And the section known as Nārāyaṇīya ends with identifying the doctrine of the ekāntins as Pāñcharātra which comprehends the Sāṁkhya-yoga, the vedas and the āraṇyakas as interpenetrating branches.

## Sāmkhya-Yoga

The intimate association of the outlook of Pāñcharātra-bhāgavata with the doctrinal position of Sāṁljua-yoga is also emphasized in the epic. At the court of the monarch Janaka of Mithilā arrives the celebrated Sāṁkhyan teacher Pañcha-śikha (Śānti-parva, chapter 218). The first exponent of the sāṁkhya teachings was Kapila, an incarmation of Nārāyaṇa. His successor was Āṣuri, and Pañcha-śikha was Āsuri’s discple. He is described in the epic as an expert in the five-moded sacrifice (‘pañcha-srotasi yas sattram āste’ which reminds us of the pāñcharātra-sattra of the Śatapatha-brāhmana), and as proficient in Pāñchaṛātra  (218, 11-12)

We will deal in a later chapter with the Sāṁkhya background of the vyūha-theory of the Pāncharātras. We may mention here that the account of the ‘ekāntin’ denizens of Śveta-dvīpa who are invisible (avyakta) and to whom Vāsudeva (viz.Purusha) is immediately in sight (ch.338-339) also has Sāṁkhyan involvements. In fact, the sages Nara and Nārāyaṇa explain to Nārada that what he beheld in Śveta-dvīpa was in fact the dual nature of ultimate reality: nara (prkṛti,) and nārāyaṇa (viz. purusha in prakṛti) (343, 46-46).

The denizens of the Śveta-dvīpa answer to the teaching of the Sāṁkhyan master Pañchaśikha about the ultimate reality of the unmanifest abiding in the Purusha (‘purushāvastham avyaktam paramārtham nyavedayet’, 218, 12): and this is the symbolism of Nara (prakṛti) and Nārāyaṇa (purusha-in-prakrti) as being the manifest aspect of the highest Godhead, which is in reality unmanifest, but provides the support and spirit for all creation. Nārada beheld this highest Purusha even when looking at Nara and Nārāyaṇa. We will have occasion to return to this thought later, while dealing with the philosophy of the of the Pāñcharātra texts, especially Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā.

The original bhāgavata-pāñcharātra teaching has found its best expression in the Nārāyaṇīya section of the epic as well as in the bhagavad-gītā which is also regarded as a part of the epic (Bhishma-parva, chapters 25 to 42) . The latter text claims that this teaching was first communicated by Kṛshṇa (viz. Nārāyaṇa or Vāsudeva) to Vivasvān (the solar deity), who passed it on to Manu, who in turns gave it to lkshvāku. Thus the teaching was handed down in a long line of succession (Bhagavad-gītā, 4, 1-2, ‘ evam paraṁparā-prāptam idam’)

The Nārāyaṇīya section also describes this teaching as ‘pāraṁparya-gatam hy etat’ (339, 141) but provides some additive details. According to this text, Vivasvān received the teaching from Āditya (the Sun-god viz. Nārāyaṇa) at the beginning of Tretā-yuga; Vivasvān handed it over to Manu, and Manu, for the sake of prosperity in the world, gave it to his son lkshvāku; and when lkshvāku taught the doctrine, it spread in all the worlds. When the worlds are dissolved in the pralaya, the teaching returns to Nārāyaṇa (348, 50-53). The text also mentions that this has been told in Hari-gītā (viz. Bhagvad-gītā) in brief (“kathito hari- gītāsu samāsa-vidhi-klpitam”)

And the text in another context clearly spells out that what was originally communicated by the Sun-god (viz. Nārāyaṇa) was the Sāttvata doctrine (viz. Bhāgavata-pañcharātra) (335, 19 ‘sāttvatam vidhim āsthaya parāk sūrya-mukha-nissrtāt’). And this makes it beyond doubt that the ‘yoga’ which is mentiioned in Bhagavad-gītā as first communicated to Vivasvān is nothing other than the Bhāgavata-pāñcharātra doctrine,which was closely associated with Sāṁkhya-yoga both in the Nārāyaṇīya section and in Bhagavad-gītā. that the Bhagavad-gītā was the original document of the bhagavata-cult appears amply justified.

It is not without reason that devotion (bhakti) assumes an important role in the Bhagavad-gītā. The bhāgavta cult is distinguished by its insistence on bhakti which is absolute and exclusive (ekānta), towards a concrete inconic manifestation of Bhagavān. the expression ‘bhakti’ occurs even in the Rg-vedic corpus, as for instance in 1,127,5:

‘The three-fold fires (āhavanīya, gārhapatya and dakshiṇāgni) protect alike those mortals who share their food with them (viz. those who offer oblations) and those who do not do so. But when pleased with the oblations offered to them , they becomeunaging (viz.immortal), and make those who hae offered them oblations also unaging’. Bhakti in the context of Vedic rituals signifield a share, a portion that was assigned or alloted. And bhakta, in its extended tsense, was not only the wealth that was thus distributed but the person for whom the share of wealth is assigned. In its original meaning, it meant boiled rice and the owner of that form of material wealth. The term ‘bhagavat’ meant the possessor of wealth.

The relation between bhakta and bhagavat is akin to the relation that obtains between ‘nara’ and nārāyaṇa’. The final refuge for the individual share-holder (nara, bhakta) is the owner of all the shares or wealth (nārāyaṇa, bhagavat). It is but a short step for the word bhakta to acquire the meaning ofa devotee, and for bhakti to mean belongingness or devotion. One who understands the importance of Bhagavat (or Nārāyaṇa) and aligns his life entirely to the will of Bhagavat is a bhagavata (viz. one who belongs to Bhagavat).

Bhāgavata is thus the cult which recognizes the all-importance of the Bhagavat (Nārāyaṇa or Vāsudeva); in the words of the Bhagavad- gītā, ‘Vāsudevas sarvam iti’. It is such recognition that is known as ‘Sāṁkhya’ in the epic. And the cult also prescribes the procedure by means of which the bhakta communicates effectively with Bhagavat; it is ‘yoga’. It is this yoga that was taught by Kṛshṇa to Vivasvān (4, 1-2 ‘ imam vivasvate yogam’), and it is this that is being taught again to Arjuna.

## Chapter II - The Pāñcharātra Literature

## The Meaning Of Pāñcharātra

The exact meaning of the word ‘ pāñcharātra’ when used to identify an Āgama division is rather uncertain. The Pāñcharātra texts themselves give a variety of meanings, some of which are fanciful. The earliest employment of which are fanciful. The earliest employment of this expression was in the Śatapatha-brāhmaṇa (12,3,4,1 and 13,6,1),and there is referred to a sacrifice (sattra) involving man (purusha), which stretched tofive nights. This was of course the most obvious and the simplest of meaning that the word may have.

The epic Mahābhārata makes use of the word in the sense of a cult, a doctrine, a way of life, with no reference whatever to ‘the ritual offive nights’. Pāñchaṣikha who visited the court of Janaka in Mithilā was an expert in Pāñcjarātra (Śānti-parva, 218--,12). Pāñcharātra was another word for the doctrine of the ekāntins (viz. bhāgavatas), who were devoted exclusively to the worship of Nārāyaṇa (op.cit.348.81-82 ‘esha ekāntinām dharmo nārāyaṇa-parātmakaāh’). it maybe recalled that the pāñcharātrasattra of the Śatapatha-brāhmana also involved Narayaṇa: It was this sattra that made him supreme and pervasive.

The epic also brings out the assimilation of Sāṁkhya-yoga with the Vedic corpus as the distinguishing feature of Pāñcharātra or the doctrine of the ekāntins (bid., ‘evem ekam sāṁkhya-yogam vedāraṇyakam eva cha; parasparaāṅgāny etāni pāñcharātram tu kathyate). But there is here no explanation offered for the choice of the word ‘pāñcharātra’ with its two necessary implications ‘five’ and ‘nights’. This may, however, suggest that the actual word-meaning of Pāñcha-ratra as ‘fivenights’ (as it ws the case when it rferred to the pāñcharātrasattra in Śatapatha-brāhmana) had already become obsolete, the word having come to be used in its extended sense of a ritualistic doctrine centering round Nārāyaṇa (viz. Vāsudeva).

The smṛti texts reveal that Pāñcharātra was the procedure by which Vāsudeva was ritualistically worshipped, but make no attempt to justify the use of the expression. We read, for instance, in Hārīta-sma Pāñcharātra was still a ‘yajan’, sacrificial act, even as it was in Śatapatha-brāhmana; but it was no longer a ritual limited to five nights or in the nature of a vedic sacrifice. This was the ritual adopted by the bhāgavatas. And who is a bhāgavata? According to Mahābharata (āśvamedhika-parva, 118,33) he was the devotee who understood the significance of the four-fold forms that narāyaṇa assumes 9viz. vyūhas) and the importance of the twelve-lettered mantra of Vāsudeva (om mamo bhagavate vāsudevāya),and who followed the scheme ofdividing hisdys meticulously in five segments for total dedication to godhead (pañcha-kāla).

Pādma-saṁhitā (2,87-88) calls the bhāgavata a ‘pāñcharātrika’ (viz. one who adopted the Pāñcharātra way of life).

Among the several words that, according to this text, could be used synonymously with pāñcharātrika (or bhāgavata), only one (viz. ‘pañch-kālavit’, the knower of the significance of the five –fold division of time) involved the number ‘five’ and therefore bears any reasonable and real relationship with the word ‘pāñcharātra (pāñcharatra-rakshā) was meant to highlight this aspect of the pāñcharātra doctrine and practice:

It is an accepted view that one who is absolutely and exclusively devoted to Godhead should order his life so that each day is spent in the service of God. The day is divided into five phases (pañcha-kāla):

(1) abhigamana, the first prahara (beginning with the brāhmamuhūrta about 4.30 a.m.) devoted to the preparatory sequences in the morning (like śaucha, snāna, sandhyā, japa and dhyāna);

(2) upādāna, the second prahara (from about 9 in the morning till 12 noon) spent in collecting the articles necessary for formal worship (like flowers, tulasīfruits and so on) and making preparations for worship;

(3) ijyā, the third prahara(beginningwith nootime), devoted to the formal iconic worship elaborately conducted, and partaking of food as naivedya with other devotees;

(4) svādhyāya,the fourth prahara(afternoon and evening), spent in the study of scriptures, contemplating on their import, teaching scriptures to others and such activities; and

(5) yoga, the last prahara (night), devoted to the repitition of the formal iconic worshipbut briefly conducted, yoga practice (eight-fold) practice, ashṭāga-yoga) and deep meditation on godhead (dhyāna) before finally resting for the day.

It maybe noticed that the most important sequence here is called ‘ijyā’, which word originally ment a sacrifice, but later used in the sense of offering any worship. The first two sequences are but preparations for this, and the last two actually evolve out of this. All the five phases aree intended to be one uninterrupted worship-ritual. It strongly reminds us of the spirit of the pāñcharātra-sattra ritual (of the Śatapatha-brāhmna), with five nights of the sattra being altered to five phases of the day.

Jayākhya-saṁhitā (1,14) tells us that Pāñcharātra is so called because it involves the five actions in these five phases oftime (‘pañcha-karmānusthāna-pañcha-kāla-nirdeśanena pāñcharātra iti saṁjña’). This is explained:

There are other meanings given for the word Pāncharātra, but most of them appear like imaginative exercises, and do not refer to the unique outlook of the doctrine or practice. The word ‘rātra’ has sometimes been taken in the sense of ‘system of thought’, or discipline. Paushkara-saṁhitā (38, 307-8) interprets the word as meaning a collection of five systems of thought: purāṇa, veda, vedāntra, sāṁkhya and yoga, the collection calculated to produce great benefit:

The Mahābhārata view that the four vedas conjoined with Sāṁkhya-yoga are to be known as Pāñcharātra has already been mentioned.

Śāṇḍilya-saṁhitā (4,80) sees in Pāncharātra a concatenation of five details: sāṁkhya, yoga, dispassion (vairāgya), austerity (tapas) and devotion to godhead (bhakti).

Here, along with the two disciplines, sāṁkhya and Yoga, some ways of life have also been ilncluded in the expression ‘rāta’.

Nārada-pāñcharātra says that the word ‘rāta’ signifies knowledge or understanding, and thatPājcharātra speaks of five modes of relevant knowledge: viz. concerning reality (tattva), salvation (mukti), devotion (bhakti), spiritual exercises (yoga) and worldly life (vishaya).

Some texts curiously interpret the word ‘rāta’ as suggesting ‘overcoming’ or ‘eradicating’. According to Padma-purāna, Pāñcharātrais so called because it dismisses (rātrīyante, adrśyāni bhavant) five different systems of thought: Sāṁkhya, Yoga, Vaiśeshika, Nyāya and Mīmāṁsā,’even as the sun destroys darkness’.

While Pāñchrātra far form dismissing, actually incorporates Sāṁkhya and Yoga as most of the authorities concede, there is nothing in Pāñcharātra which attacks the other three systems of thought mentioned in the above verse. The interpretation is obviously fancifuol and untrue. Śāṇḍilya-saṁhitā while bringing out the special merit of Pāñcharātra,mentions that this teaching is for all human beings who aspire for salvation, unlike the Vedas which are meant only for the higher classes, and that therefore the teaching is explailned by sāṁkhya and yoga:

Some texts take another rather remarkable view, and interpret ‘rāta’ as ignorance’ and ‘pañcha’ as elimination thereof. We read in Vishṇu-saṁhitā.

The five ‘ignorances’ (rātrayah), according to this text, are the five objects of sense-experience, (form, sound, smell, taste and touch), or ‘false knowledge’ obtained through the senses; the five ‘rātris’ may also refere to the fundamental elemental principles (mahābhūta: earth, water, fire, air and ākāśa) which constitute the world; Pāñcharātra will undo this ignorance or false knowledge.

Viśvāmitra-saṁhitā (2,3-7) has a curious explanation to offer. According to this text ‘pañcha-ra’ means human individuals who get to know (ra) about the world through their five senses (pañcha); and pāñcharātra signifies those who are eager to protect (tra0 the five sense. Alternately, the five systems sāṁkhya, Yoga and others are involved in Pāñcharātra; but the text also concedes that pāñcharātra is so called because it clears away the other five disciplines.

Parama-saṁhitā (1, 39-41) has another explanation to offer. For an individual endowed with the body, the qualities of the five fundamental elements (mentioned above) are the ‘rātris’; and the word rātri may signify the conjoint operation of these five details (Yoga), or their withdrawal (viz. nivṛtti). Because five are the details which combine or separate, the system is called Pāñcharātra. It is obvious thatthis interpretation does not carry conviction; it did not to the author himself, for he proceeds to provide an alternate interpretation, however equally unconvincing. The ‘rātris’ for the lindividual are the filve tanmātras (the fundmental elements), ego (ahaṁkāra), consciousness (buddhi) and unmanifest prakrti (avyakta).

While this reminds us of the ‘puryashṭaka’ of the Sāṁkhya thought, it is difficult to see how this bears any relevance to the ideology of Pāñcharātra. And the text does not provide an unequivocal meaning for the expression ‘rātri’. However, it is possible to see some sense in this interpretation. Normal life is lthe com;bination (yoga) of these five details (whether they are merely the attirbutes of lthe five lmanā-bhūtas or the five aspects of lindividual consitution lwhich is psychophysical). This may be taken to represent the pravṛtti-dharma. Pāñcharātra as a system of thought prescribes that worldly involvement must be eliminated (nivṛtt-dharma) in order to engage oneself in absolute and exclusive devotion to Godhead (ekānta-bhakti)

## The Original Teaching

Frequently, the texts take the expression to mean simply ‘five nights’, and explain that the system was so called because this was taught by Vāsudeva in five ‘nights’. In the traditional context, ‘rātra’ (night) means the day and night together (ahorātra). Vāmana-saṁhitā and Īśvara-saṁhitā (21) mention that five sges received this teaching in the five ‘nights’ so that what was one veda (meant only to secure salvation) was now made to flow in five streams, at the behest of Vāsudeva himself:

The five sages were really the human incarnations of the five weapons (pāñchāyudha) of Vishnu, and they appeared in five sacred spots. The discus (Sudarśana) appeared as the sage Śāṇḍiya in Pauṇḍra-vardhana-kshetra; the couch(pāñchajanya) as the sage Kauśika in Mṛga-kshetra; the mace(Kaumodakī) as Bhāradvāja in Śālagrāma; the sword (Nandaka) as Mauñjyāyana in Ananta-kshetra; and the bow (Śārṅga) as Aupagāyana in Totādri. Another account identifies the sagesdifferently; conch (Aupagāyana), mace (Mauñjyāyana), sword (Kauśik) and bow (Bhāradvāja). Īśvra-saṁhitā (21, 519-533) explains that because the five sages who were the incarmations of the five weapons of Vishnu received the teaching in five successive nights (each sage in a day-night peirod), the doctrine came to be known as Pāñcharātra.

Vihagendra-sṁhitā(1, 33-34) also takes Pāñcharātra to mean the teaching received by five devotes in five nights but identifies the recipients of the teaching differently: Brahmā (first night), Śiva (second night), Ananta (third night), Vishvksena (fourth night), and Garuḍa (fifth ;night ). And the text mentions that the teaching was given at the beginning of Kṛta-yuga (the first of the four yogas)

Vishṇu-tattva-saṁhitā, however, substitutes Vishvaksena and Garuḍa with Nārada and Bṛhaspati. Maha-Sanatkumāra-saṁhitā has four chapters, which are named Brahma- rātra, Śiva-rātra, Indra- rātra and Ṛshi- rātra, suggesting Brahmā, Śiva, Indra and the sages as the recipients of the Pāñcharātra teaching. Bhāradvāja-saṁhitā (2,12-13) counts the five nights in Pāñcharātra after Brahmā, Śiva, Indra, Nāga and the sages:

We also read in Mārkaṇḍeya-saṁhitā

Or it is general teaching of the doctirne for five continious nights by Vāsudeva (as in Īśvara-saṁhitā, 21,532-533)

But the early accounts including the epic Mahābhārata uniformly accept Nārada as the first recepient of the Pāñcharātra teaching, and hold that Nārada taught the other sages.

Īśvara-saṁhitā (1, 29-30) makes Nārada communicate the teaching that he got in Śveta-dvīpa, to seven sages who are therefore regarded as the masters and first exponents of the teaching; Sana, Sanatsujāta, Sanaka, Sanandana, Sanatkumāra, Kapila and Sanātana:

Śāṇḍilya-saṁhitā also ascribes the human origin of Bhāgavata-Śāstra (viz. Pāñcharātra) to the great sages headed by Nārada:

## Pāñcharātra Texts

There is method of classifying thePāñcharātra texts into three groups (e.g. Pārameśvara-saṁhitā, chapter 30): (1) divya(of divine origin), (2) muni-prokta (given by the sages), and (3) paurusha (of human origin).

The first group of texts (like Sāttvata-saṁhitā, Paushkara-saṁhitā and Jayākhya-saṁhitā) are claimed to have come directly from Vāsudeva himself and spread in the worlds (terrestrial as well as celestial) by the gods (like Brahmā, Rudra and Indra) who obtained them from Vāsudeva. They are in the anushtup metre and they explain the teachings in brief as well as in great details:

The divya texts are crystal-clear in their import, and altogetherunambiguous while being full of meaning; they are rooted in the Vedas, and the meant tosecure salvation. They are brief in nature, land involve the description of godhead in its four major forms (vyūh, vibhava, antaryāmi and archā). They are authoritative in character:

The temples where worship is conducted according to these texts are said to be ‘divya-deśes’ (most sacred among the Vaishṇava places of worship ), and the Tamiḷhymns by; the celebrated Āḷvārs which eulogize these temples answer to the name of ‘divya- prabandha’.

(2) the second group of texts include not only those composed by the gods (like Brahmā and Rudra) but also those compiled by the great sages (hence muni-prokta or muni- bhāshita), out of their own understanding intuition and reason. Such texts occur in three-divisions; (a) sāttivka, (b) rājasa, and (c) tāmasa.

(a) The sāttvika texts are composed by the sages or gods who have procured the original teaching from Vāsudeva himself, but have relied on their own light for explaining the details thereof in order to set the human devotee on the right path. Such texts contain guidance and prescriptions, and are authoritative in their own right. They are consistent in their delilneation of topics, and in perfect conformity with the divy;a-texts upon which they seek to expatiate. They also appeal to the tastes of the lay folk, while leading them on spiritual path.

To this sub-division belong texts like Īśvara-saṁhitā, Pārameśvara-saṁhitā, the saṁhita ascribed to the seven great sages (Marīchim Atri, Aṅgirāh, Pulaha, Pulastya, Krtu and Vaśishṭha) and Jayottara-saṁhitā.

(a) the rājasa class of texts is distinguished by; the greater role of reason than that of intuition. The author’s own intelligence helps him to understand the meaning of the teaching, given by Vāsudeva and other gods like Brahmā and Rudra, and great sages, and also to e xplain it to others. He relies on some part of the t eaching and expatitates upon it; he also summarises the teaching for the edification of ordinary people And while doing so, he arranges the matter in his own way, and chooses to describes the topics according to his own understanding.

These texts are more intellectual in nature: they discuss the philosophical issues involved and also the details of worship rituals like iconography, installation and consecration of images, the specific mantras to be employed in vrious sequences of worship, thefive-fold activity in installation and consecration (stāpanā, āsthāpanā, saṁsthāpanā, prasthāpanāand pratishṭhā), worship accorded to the moveable images (Karmārchā), daily worship (nitya-saṁpūjanā), iconographical and other details of minor icons in the Vishnu shrine (like Brahmā and Rudra), worship done on behalf of the common people (‘pūjanam tu samuddishṭam prākṛrānām jaḍātmanām’), the eulogy of the benefits derived from worship, from the construction of temples and installation of images or from making grants and endowments to shrines (‘praśaṁsakam yat siddhīnām), rules of good conduct (saṁpravartaka), varieties of fire rituals(yāga, mahāyāga, sava, kratu, adhvara, haristoma and somayajña), and details of initiation and the officiants (samayī, dīkshita, charavarti abhishikta, guru, āchārya and bhagavān).

Among the texts that answer tothis description are Sanat-Kumāra- saṁhitā, Pādmodbhava-saṁhitā, maya-vaibhava-samhitā, Tejo-draviṇa-samhitā and Satya-saṁhitā (according to jayākhya and Pārameśvara-saṁhitās) (see Appendix for a complete list)

(a) The tāmasa texts are composed by the sages after studying the divya and sāttvika-muni-prokta texts, but altogether based on their own understanding and imagination; they may even be called their independent and original compositions, which however are not entirely authorittive:

Such texts do not give due consideration to the fact that all deities other than Vishṇu which are worshipped in the Vishnu-temple (like Brahmā, Rudra, other gods, Durgā and the seven mother-goddesses) are merely aspects of Vishṇu (aṅga-bhāva); and therefore they prescribe independent worship for these deities (svātantryeṅa): mantrs, contemplations, iconographic deities and so on.

Among the texts included in this division are Pāñcha-prśna-samhitā, Śuka-praśna-saṁhitā and Tattva-sāgara-saṁhitā.

(3) The last group of Pāñcharātra texts are ‘paurusha’ or mānusha viz. the works composed entirely by human effort, viz. without the benefit of intuition of perfect understanding of the ‘divine’ texts. Unlike the other two groups, this group is characterized by numerous defects, like meaningless explanations, irrelevant discussions, excessive verb iage and inconsistencies:

It is needless to point out that these are the least authoritative, and occupy an inferior status. They must not be followed when other groups of texts are available. Indeed, when it is the tradition of a shrine to conduct worship according to the ‘divine’ group of texts it is improper to follow the texts belonging to the group, ‘given by the sages’. And when the texts of the sāttivka division of the latter group are in practice, the texts of the rājasa division must be avoided; where the texts of the rājasa division in vogue, those of the tāmasa division are not to be followed.

But if a ‘paurusha’ text is in conformity with the ‘divine’ or with the ‘sāttvika’ or ‘rājasa’ texts, such of the details as are consonant and necessary may be followed, and treated like ‘the texts gien by the sages’.

But authorities are not unanimous about the texts to be included in these groups. All the texts, howeer, concede that’the three jewels’, Sāttvata, Paushakara and Jayākhya-samhitās are to be regarded as ‘divine’ and supremely authoritative. A minor text of the fourteenth century, Siddhānta-ratnākara of Śrī-śAILA- Veṅkaṭa-Sudhi adds some other saṁhitās also in this group: Vāmana-saṁhitā, Aniruddha-saṁhitā, Pradyumna-saṁhitā, Saṁkarshaṇa-saṁhitā and varāha-saṁhitā. But this is not widely accepted.

Vedānta-deśika’s Pāñcharātra-rakshā classifies the first two groups of texts as follows:

(1) ‘divya’: Sāttvata, Paushkara and Jayākhya-saṁhitās

(2) ‘Muni-prokta’

(a) ‘sāttvika’:

Īśvara, Bharadvāja, Pārameśvara, Vaihāyasa, Jayottara, Sumantu and Chitra-śikhaṇḍI samhitās

(a) ‘rājasa’

Sanatkumāra, Māyā-vaibhva, Pādmodbhava, Śātāpada, satya, and Tejo-draviṇa saṁhitās

(a) ‘tāmasa’

Pañcha-praśna, Śuka-praśna and Tattva-sāgara saṁhitās.

The Pāñcharātra text Bhāradvāja-saṁhitā, considers Bhāradvāja, Vaśishṭha, Hārīta, Vaiyāsa and Kāśyapa samhitās as belonging to the ‘muni-prokta-sāttvika’ group. Nārāyaṇa-saṁhitā has a larger list of texts in this group: Hārīta, Vihagendra, Paushkara, Pañcha-praśna, Ananta Parama-purasha, Purushottama, Bhāgavata, Jayākhyā, Śrī-praśna, Mahīpraśna, Sanandana, Vishṇu- siddhānta, Śrī-kara Satya, Viśva, Mahā-Sanatkumāra, Bṛhan-nāradīya, Tattva-sāgara and Vishṇu-vaibhava saṁhitās, twenty in umber. It may be noticed that this text regards ‘the three jewels’ normally included in the ‘divya’ group ) as Sāttvika texts.

According to Bhāradvāja-saṁhitā, Mānava, Yājñavalkaya, Atreya, Tārkshya, Vishṇu and Kātyāyana-saṁhitās are the rājasa texts of the muni-prokta group. Nārayaṇa-saṁhitā enumerates as many as thirty-six texts in this division, like Pādmodbhava, Māyā-vaibhava, Mārkaṇḍeya, vishvaksena, Īśvara, Vaihāyasa, Bhārgava, Pādma, Saṁkarshaṇa, Ahirbudhnya, Nāradiya, Bhāradvāja and Hārīta saṁhitās.

Gautama, Bārhaspatya, sāmvarta, Śaṁkha, Yama and Auśanasa saṁhitās belong to the’tāmasa’ division of the’muni-prokta’ group, according to Bhāradvāja-saṁhitā; Kapila, Jaimini, Bṛhaspati, Paulastya, Dattātreya, Paiṅgala and Mārīcha saṁhitās are also included in this division by some texts.

However, this classification of texts according to guṇas (sāttvika, rājasa amd tāmasa) appears arbitrary and untenable. And many of the saṁhitās included in these divisions are not no longer available, so that it is difficult to ascertain the precise criterion on which the classification was based. A broad frame-work has thus been indicated; the ‘divya’ is what is directly taught by the Lord, and promulgated by the gods; the ‘muni-bhāshita’ of the ‘sāttvika’ division confines its attention to what has thus been taught by the gods. While that of lthe ‘rājasa’ division is but a partial reception of the divine teaching but augmented by ones own spiritual attainments, and that of the ‘tāmasa’ division is more heavily dependent upon ones own attainments, having the divine teaching only in the background; and the’ mānusha’ is what is composed by human authors, quite differently from the above two.

## The Extent Of Pāñcharātra Literature

Like the other Āgamas, Pāñcharātra also claims that the original extent of this āgama is endless and beyond count (‘akshayyam asaṁkhuam tantram’ 2,1); but when Brahmā obtained it from Nārāyaṇa, it was limited to one and a half crore of granthas, a grantha being a metrical group of thirty-two syllables (‘sṣrddha-koti- pramāṇena jagrāha bhagavān vidhih’ , ibid, also Pādma-saṁhitā, jñāna-pada, 1f). Īśvara-saṁhitā, which closely follows Sāttvata-saṁhitā, is also a work in twenty-five chapters, but consisting of more than 8,000 verses. Sixteen of the chapters deal with the details of worship, the other chapters being devoted to topics like iconography, initiation, contemplation, mantras, expiation and yogic practices.

The Pauskhara-saṁhitā is of considerable antiquity, as it is cited both by Rāmānuja and Vedānta-deśika, It is a work of 5,900 verses in forty-three chapters. Pārameśvara-saṁhitā which follows this work is a work of 8,700 verses in twenty-six chapters. It has a commentary by Nṛsiṁha-yajvā. Jayākhya-saṁhitā was probably so called because the original Mahābhārata was called ‘Jayā’, and this work was devoted to the glorification of the bhāgavata (viz.Pāñcharātra) cult. In any case, it is a work of 4,500 verses in thirty-three chapters, and is encyclopaedic in character. The Pādma-saṁhitā which follows Jayākhya is the work which is widely used and best known. It has 9,000 verses and eighty -two chapters. Peddaṇṇa has prepared a gloss on this work, entitled Pāñcharātra-pradīpikā.

## Subject-Matter Of Pāñcharātra

We learn form this work that ekāyana-veda, which existed even prior to the four vedas, originated from Vāsudeva and dealt with devotion as the only approach available to reach the highest. This was indeed the original and pure veda, while the other vedas are modifications thereof (vikāra-veda). But the latter appeal to the people better, because of the worldly welfare that they envisge. Vāsudeva thereupon withdrew the ekāyana-veda from the popular gaze, and taugt it to a group of select ‘entins’: Sana, Sanatsujāta, Sanaka, Sanandana, Sanat-Kumāra, Sanātana and Kapila. Other sages like MaŽichi, Atri, Pulaha, Pulalstya, Dratu, Vaśishṭha and Svāyaṁbhuva also learnt it from Nārāyaṇa. The Pāñcharātra literature has been based upon the teachings that all of them thus receilved. Sāṇḍilya learnt ‘ekāyana’ from Saṁkarshaṇa, the first official master of the Sāttvata creed, and taught it to other sages. It ws saṁ-Karshaṇa that ordained that all the saṁhitās, especially the Sāttvata, Paushkara and jayākhya samhitās, should be in perfect accord with the sāttvata-vidhi.

A word must be said about a curious text known as Nārada-pāñcharātra (different from Nāradīya-saṁhitā), which was at one time imagined as the earliest of the Pāñcharātra texts, and which is the only Vaishṇava āgama work to be unearthed in the northern parts of the country. Published by the Asiatic Society of Bengal as early as 1865, this poetical work claims to be a dialogue between Śilva (who is represented here as a devotee of Kṛshṇa) and the sage Nārada(who was a disciple of Śiva) on the Śataśṛṅga peak in Goloka; and this dialogue was recalled by Nārada in Pushkara during sūrya-parva. The work is undoubtedly a late composition, involving as it does the Rādhā cult alongside the doctrine of devotion to Kṛshṇa(especially Bāla-Kṛshṇa). This work has none of the doctrinal details of Pāñcharātra, nor does it concern itself with ritualistic worship. The thought structure found here would be altogether unfamiliar to any of the Pāñcharātra texts discovered till now.

The text calls itself Pāñcharātra because it deals with five kinds of knowledge (which is meant by the word rātra) (1,1,44):

The five kinds of knowledge dealt with here are (1) the highest knowledge that Śiva received from Kṛshṇa which helps one to achileve immortality (jñanam parama-tattvam janmamṛtyu-jarāpaham; mṛtyuñjayah śaṁbhus saṁprāpa Kṛshṇa-vaktratah’);

(2)the pure knowledge that is conducive to salvation (‘mukti-pradam śuddham’):

(3)the metitorious knowledge that accomplishes devotion to Kṛshṇ (‘maṅgalam kṛshṇa-bhakti-dam’);

(3) the knowledge of the yog that brings about all attainments (‘yaugikam jñānam sarva-sidddhi-pradam);

(4) and the mundane knowledge that helps ordinary people flourish ;in the world (‘vaishyikam nŽṇām jñānam’) (1,1, 45-53).

The first two are described as ‘sāttvika’ in character, the thrid devoid of any guṇa (nairguṇyam), the fourth ‘rājasa’ and the fifth ‘tāmasa’. This work calls itself the sense of the veda, and the view of Kṛshṇa himself (‘veda-sāram idam kṛshṇa- matam’,2,31).

Quite different is the treatment of the Pāñcharātra doctrine and practice in the txts we have been discussing till now. For instance, Śrī-praśna-saṁhitā (2,42-46) mentions the following as topics dealt with in a typical Pāñcharātra-saṁhitā:

Wisdom that leads to emancipation is the focus of attention; but such wisdom is always accompanied by yoga, and therefore yoga also is dealt with. Four are the normal approaches to godhead: wisdom (jñāna), ritualistic action (karma), devotion (bhakti) and surrender (prapatti). Wisdom involves withdrawal from normal life (nivṛtti), whereas ritualistic action in joins involvement (pravṛtti). The pāñcharātra insists that the best approach to Godhead is by engaging oneself in ritualistic action (karma), devotion (bhakti) and surrender (prapatti). Wisdom involved withdrawal from normal lilfe (nivṛtti), whereas ritualistic action enjoins involvement (pravṛtti). The pāñchrātra insists that the best approach to Godhead is by engaging oneself in ritualistic action. Nārada (in Sāttvata-saṁhitā), 1,7) is asked by Paruśu-rāma to teach the sages the path of ritualistic action, which is really the Sāttvata method:

The path of Kriyā(Karma) takes the form of building temples, making of images, and worshipping them. Therefore, the Pāñcharātra texts deal with problems like the building of temples, co;nsecrating them, fashioning of icons, their peculiar charcteristics, installations off the icons, fire-rituals, expiations for the commissions and omissions, initiation of the priests, worship-sequences, varieties of festivls and the mantras that are employed during worship.

The path of ritualistic action (Kriyā-mārga) will bloom into surrender (prapatti), when it is associated with wisdom and devotion. Thus the four-fold approach to godhead is accompanied by following the Sāttvta-vidhi.

The ideal method of treating the subject-matter covered in any Āgama has been to divide the text into four sections; the first dealing with the theoretical foundations, especially nature of the supreme spirit and the nature of the soul, (Jñāna-pāda or Vidyā- pāda), the second with the higher aspects of spiritual practice by means of which the individual soul will get into communion with the supreme spirit (Yoga-pāda), the third with the ritualistic practice connected with iconic worship and temple construction (Kriyā-pāda), and the fourth with problems lilke initiation into priesthood, daily behaviour, festival occasions in the temple and so on (Charyā-pāda). Many of the Śaivāgama texts do follow this scheme, but very few Pāñcharātra texts, and none at all of the Vaikhānasa texts, do so . However, the details covered normally under these four sections are considered, sometimes in detail, by the Pāñcharātratexts, although not arranged in such sections, nor in that order. The only notable exception is Pādma-saṁhitā, which is undoubtedly the most popular among the texts of lthis Āgama-division. It has four pādas as mentioned above, and comprises of 9,000 verses in eighty-two chapters.

That this division into four sections is ideal has, however, been conceded generally by the Pāñcharātra writers. The first division should, according to them explain the nature of Vāsudeva, the four vyūha-forms, their functions, creation of the world, the individual soul, and the highest possible attainment (salvation moksha). The second division should focus attention on the methods to be adopted by the individual to approach godhead (uupāsanā-prakārāh). It follows the first division in as much as the knowledge of the nature of Godhead and of the nature of individual soul leads to the pursuit of the device by means of which the two can come together; it is a special mental effort:

The ‘Kriyā’ in the third section refers to ritualistic actions, particularly in connection with construction of temples, and fashioning if icons (‘ālayāchā-vigrahādi- nirmāṇam’) so that Godhead may more easily be approached by worship. iconography, iconometry and architectural details are to be dealt with here.

The fourth section, known as ‘Gharyā-pāda’, is to provide all the relevant details of worship-rituals (‘ārādhanā-vidhayah’) in the temple, daily and occasional worship-sequences, festivals, initiation of priests, lthe mantras, lthe maṇḍalas, and ideal priestly behaviour in general

Some only of the available texts contain information about the philosophical position of lthe Pāñcharātra: Pādma-, Ahilr-budhnya-, Jayākhya-, Paushkara- and Sāttvata-saṁhirtās may be mentioned in this connection. Accounts of yoga in the Pāñcharātra texts pertain to the eight-fold yoga (ashtāṅga-yoga) of the Pātañjala type, and include details of mediation and japa. Parama- and Vihagendra-saṁhitās provide such accounts. The problems in the Kriya-yoga are to be found discussed in texts like Pādma-Kāpiñjala-, Mārkaṇḍeya-, Sāttvata-,Śrī-praśna- and Nāradīya- saṁhitās. Most of the pāñcharātra texts are devoted (and many exclusively so) to the actual worship rituals, daily and occasional utsavas, dīkshā,vidhi and suchilssues (charyā). Īśvara-saṁhitā devotes as many as seventeen of its twenty four chapters to this aspect of the Āgama. Vishṇu-tattva-, Pārāśara-, Mahāsanatkumāra-, Anirudha- and Hayaśīrsha-saṁhitās are among the texts which address them-selves especially to this theme.

## Chapter III - The Pāñcharātra Outlook

## Authenticity Of Pāñcharātra

The very fact that the great Śrī-vaishnava teacher of the ninth century A.D., Yāmuna-muni, (917-1041, A.D., predecessor of the Rāmanuja) undertook to prepare a tretise defending the authoritative character of Pāñcharātra (Āgama-prāmāṇya) suggest that Pāñcharātra was being treated as an outcast by the adherents of Vedic tradition. That this prejudice against followers of Pāñcharātra was strong even centuries later is evidenced by the elaborate defence of that system by Vedānta-deśika (Veṅkatanātha, 1268-1369 A.D.) in his Pāñcharātra-rakshā. Indeed, the differences between Vedic traditionalilsts and the āgama enthusiasts (of any division, Śiva or Vaishṇava have not entirely died down even now.

The strife between the two groups is a long-standing one, going back to a distant past. It was merely academic, nor even simply doctrinal, but it has social and economic overtones. The Vedic insistence on collective and elaborate rituals and strtict conformity with the injunctions of the Brāhmaṇa texts provoked a resistence form those who placed, superior value on individual experience of the spirit and loving devotion for the Godhead. It was natural that the Āgama approach of personal ldeities and iconic worship (which however are also to be seen in the Vedic hy;mns) had a more popular appeal, and became widespread. In this, lthere was often rivalry and sometimes rapproachment. When the two approaches, the Vedic and the Āgamic, developed belligerant stances, a work like Yāmuna-muni’s Āgama-prāmāṇya was helpful.

The vedic adherents of Smṛti condemened the Āgama as unorthodox, and described the Āgama as sinners and outcasts. Several smrtis (like Āśvalāyana), some purāṇas (like Kūrma, Vāyu, Liṅga, Āgni and Bṛhannāradīya) and even some saṁhitās (like Vishṇu, Sūta, Śātātapa, Hārīta and Bodhāyana) warned the ‘decent’ folk from having anything to do with the pāñchrātrins, who were outcsts (‘sarva-dharama-bahiskrtā’) and had lost their rights for performing vedic rituals. Kūrma-Purāṇa prohibited inviting them to households for partaking of the sraddha-meals. Bṛhan-nārādīya-purāṇa insisted that one must not even converse with the pañcharātrins. But there were other smrits (like Vṛddha-manu, Hārīta, Vāśishṭha, Vyāsa and Parāśara), other purāṇas (like Vishṇu, Nāradīya, Bhāgavata, Gāruḍa, Pādma, Brahmāṇḍa and Varāha), other saṁhitās (like Śāṇḍilya) and texts like Vishṇu-dharmottara which espoused the cause of Pāñcharātra, and defended the pāñcharātrins against the accusations of the ritualists. The major support for Pāñcharātra came from the epic Mahābhārata and from Bhagavad-gītā.

There was also a counter-attack form the Pāñcharātra camp, and the Vedas were dismissed as ‘pervented’ texts (vikāra-veda) and as incapable of fulfilling the human values (purushār-Vedic approach as the Vaidikas were to the pāñcharātrins.

It was in a sitution like this that Yāmuna-muni thought of driving some sense into both the groups and thus bring about a reconciliation. His Āgama- prāmāṇya is a valiant effort in this direction; but it had to be a delilcate undertaking, as it touched the sensitivity of both the parties. Added to it, the ideology of temple worship involved a class of professional priests who were hired to worship for other in the temple (parārtha-yajana), and the idea of worshipping for the sake of monetary reward was no doubt abnoxious to all right thinking people. A social stigma had thus been attached to this group, which was branded as heretic (avaidika).

Steering clear of social prejudices, Yāmuna-muni attempted to establish theauthoritative character of the Pāñcharātra system ( (Sāttvata-vidhi) on the basis of its having originated in Vudeva himself, even as the Veda was. Veda and Pāñcharātra are according to him, complementary to each other. The purpose of the Veda is not merely to enjoin theperformance of rituals (Smṛti), but to inform us about the nature of theSupreme Spirit, viz. Vāsudeva. The latter objective is fulfilled by the Pāñcharātratexts. Pāñcharātra thus is as authoritative as the Veda, as the Smṛti and as the Kalpa. The rituals that Pāñcharātra suggest are only meant to augment the prescriptions of the Smṛtis; and are not opposed to them.

A significant argument, lhowever, was that while the Vedas (and the Smṛtis) were meant for a small section of the people, who a re intellectually and culturally competent to understand and observe the lrituals of lthe Śrauta and Smārta varieties, Pāñcharātra was given by the same Godhead who gave the Vedas, for lthe sake of large groups of people who were unable or impatient to follow the elaborate and emticulous Smrti in junctions. Further, Pāñcharātra emphasized devotion (bhakti) as an easier and more effective instrument than the Vedic wisdom and rituals. Thus we filnd in our classics instances of sages like Śāṇḍilya who came to prefer the sāttvara mode to the Vedic methods which they had been following till this realization dawned on them.

Yāmuna-muni strove to accord equal validity to the smṛti (viz. Veda) and Pāñcharātra, in the eyes of the discerning people. But the strife took a new turn, when the Pāñcharātrins claimed that their own ekāyana-veda was the earlier word of God, and the four vedas with which people are acquainted having come out of it later and with some obscuracies and errors (‘mahatoveda- vṛkshasya mūla-bhuto mahan ayam’). Vedānta-deśika’s defence, however, accepted that Pāñcharātra was rooted in the Veda, as much as the Kalpa (a vedāṅga) is (‘sruti-mūlam idam tantram pramāṇam kalpa-sūtra-vat’) And he pointed out that while Jamadagni-smṛti prescribes that worship must be preferably conducted with vedic mantras, the text provides that hymns from pāñcharātratexts may be used, when the vedic mantras are not available.

Veda and Pāñcharātra were equal in validity, but somehow the Veda was more equal! Vedānta –desika quotes in another contex a passage from a Pāñcharātra text (Lakshmī-tantra) which says that the wise man must never transgress even in his thoughts the conduct prescribed in the Veda (Nikshapa-rakshā, p.21’ tad ukam Lakshmī- tantrepi “manīshī vaidikāchāram manasā pi na laṅghayet”’).

An attempt was thus made to bring within the Vedic fold the Pāñcharātra school, which in common with Kāpālika, Kālāmukha, Śākta, Bhairava and Pāśupata, had a heretic flavour (cf. Kūma-purāṇa and Skananda-purāṇa which describes all of them as ‘garihitāh’ condemenable). It was successful when the norms of good conduct were derived not entirely form the smṛti texts but form the epics and the purānas also; and when iconic worship in temples became an integral part of indian culture. We read, for instance, in Brahmāṇḍa-purāna, that acceptable traditional lore comprises of the four Vedas, the two epics (Mahabhārata and Rāmāyaṇa), the pāñcharātra texts and the purānas.

The involvement of Pāñchrātra in the Vedic tradition also led to a broad-based philosophical outlook. Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā (12,4- 54) speaks offive disciplines which are fundamental (‘etāni pañcha-śāstrāṇi mūla-bhūtāni’ 49) and which are all alike settled in Godhead as five different approaches (‘nishṭhā tvekaiva śāstrāṇām eteshām pañcha-vartmanām, ‘ 54): (1) Trayi or the three vedas, with their branches (śākhās), limbs (aṅgas), secondary limbs (upāṇga) and secondary vedas (upaveda);(2) the Sāṁkhya system which was crystallized by sage Kapila in ShasṭI- tantra; (3) the yoga (or yogānuśāsana), which was originated from Hiraṇya-garbha and which involves the methods or restraint 9nirodha) and methods of right action (karma); (4) the Pāśupata school of thought which was originated from Ahirbudhnay and which lwas meant form releasing the lindividual souls from bondage (paśu-paśa- pramochana); and (5) the Sāttvata (or Pāñcharātra) system which was originated lform sudarśana and which exists in ten aspects.

There can be little doubt that Sāṁkhya and Yoga are very much the philosophical outlook of the Pāñcharātra; and that the affiliation with the Vedic tradition was always sought after. But it is not so evident how the Pāśupata school is integrated with Pāñcharātra. Probably there was an attempt to include the sectrian devotees of siva within the Pāñcharātra fold; for we do have a reference to the ‘Śiva-bhāgavatas’ in Pataṇjali’s Mahābhāshya (Keilhorn’s edition,Vol II pp 387-388 ‘śivasya bhāgavatā iti śiva-bhāgavatāh).

## Siddhānta Divisions

It is clear that the Pāñcharātrins did not constitute a homogenous group; there were divisions among them. And this has been recognized in Paushkara-saṁhitā (38,303-304) when the text says that although one in essential import the Pāñcharātra or ‘siddhānta’ occurs in several divisions right form lthe beginning.

Four main divisions of Pāñcharātra, however, are recognized in some texts (like Pādma, Pārameśvara-, and Paushkara- Saṁhitās), and they are likened to the four divisions of the one Veda into ṚG, Yajus, Sāman and Atharva (Pādma-saṁhitā, jñāna-pāda, 1,76).

The divisions are called ‘siddhāntas’, because they are the final conclusions arrived at by the adepts at the end of innumerable births, and when they have no more necessity to be born again (pāraseśvara-saṁhitā, 19,544, ‘ye janam-koṭbhissiddhās teshām anto ‘tra janamanah’). And they are also called great Upanishads or secret teachings (Pādma-saṁhitā, jñāna-pāda, ‘1,110, ‘mahopanishadam nama siddhānta-pada-śaabditam’). The final teaching is that Vāsudeva alone is lthe supreme spirit and that salvation is to be got by worshipping him. But different divisions advocate different methods of worship, and they also differ in the benefits that they bestow.

Each of these divisions appears to have been regarded as a disticnt ‘siddhānta’ by virtue of the fact that it has come down in famililes through generations (pāramparya-gata). There is an insistence that the lsiddhānta that a person in born into is the most proper one for him and that he must on no account change over to another siddhānta. The mix-up (sāṅkarya) in the matter of siddhāntas is frowned upon (Pādma-saṁhitā, 19):

The pohibition against mix-up also applies with regard to the rituals pertaining to consecration of ground (karshaṇa), building of the ltemple (prāsāda-nirmāna), fashioning of the icons, installations, and worship,. All the rituals must be according to any; one of the siddhāntas; otherwise it calls for purifications (saṁprokshaṇa) and expiations (prāyaśchitta). And the priest or master of ceremonies (āchārya) who has received initiation in one siddhānta must not carry out the rituals according to any other siddhānta. This is comparable to the Vedic sacrifice where the hotr will chant only Ṛg-vedic mantras, the adhvaryu only the passages of Yajurveda, and the Udgātṛ only the chants from the Sāma-veda.

The four siddhāntas are named mantra-siddhānta, āgama-siddhānta, tantra-siddhānta and tantrāntara-siddhānta. This is the order followed in Pādma-saṁhitā, where we find an elaborate account of the siddhantas. Paushkara-, Īśvara- and Pārameśvara-saṁhitās make āgama-siddhānta precede mantra-siddhānta, and describe āgama-siddhānta as having appeared in the kṛta-yuga, land mantra-siddhānta in the next (viz. Tretā) yuga. According to these texts āgama-siddhānta is the best (‘eshakārtayuga-dharmah sarva- dharmottamah smṛtah’). Kālottara-saṁhitā describes the four siddhāntas in terms of ‘divya’ (of divine origin), ‘svayaṁ- vyakta’ (self-manifest), ‘saiddh’ (promulgated by the a depts) and ‘ārsha’ (taught by the great seers) respectively. Nārāyaṇa-saṁhitā clubs the first two divisions and calls it ‘nāgara’ (viz. of the sāttvika type), and calls the third’ drāviḍa’ (rājasa) and the fourth ‘vesara’ (tāmasa); it also equates the ‘nāgara’ type with Vishṇu, the ‘drāviḍa’ with Brahmā and the ‘vesara’ with Śiva. Hayagrīva-saṁhitā (as cited in Vedānta-diśika’s Pāñcharātra-raksha) distinguishes between the four siddhāntas in terms of the benefits they produce: āgama-sid-siddhāntas is meant to accomplish salvation alone (mokshaika-phala); tantra-siddhānta is calculated to accomplish all the four values of life (chaturvarga-phala: wealth, virtue, pleasures and salvation), and tantrāntara-siddhānta will fulfill all that one desires(vāñchhitārtha-phala).

Pādma-saṁhitā(jñāna-pāda, ‘80-82) distinguishes between the four siddnāntas mainly and simply on the basis of the deity worshipped. In the first siddhānta (mantra), only one iconic representation of the deity (eka-mūrti) is principally worshipped. However, what diety this should be is not specified. In the second (āgama), four forms of the deity (the vyūha-forms; Vāsudeva, Saṁkarshaṇa, Pradyumna and Aniruddha) are worshipped. In the third, nine forms (in addition to the above four, Nārrashiṁha, and Varāha) are worshipped. In the fourth, the deity that is worshipped has three or four faces.

Instead of worship of only one deity, this text includes not only all the forms of Vishṇu (like the vyūhas, mūtyantaras, prādurbhāvas, prādurbhāvāntara-gaṇa) but consorts (like Lakshmī and Pushti, twelve of them), personified weapons (like conch and discus), attendants (like Garuḍa) and guardians of directions for worship (For the forms of vishṇu, see later)

## Chapter IV - Pāñcharātra Ideology

## Practical Approach

It is generally supposed that Pāncharātra system has no philosophy other than the Śrī-Vaishnava philosophy of Viśishṭādvaita. This, however, is an incorrect notion. The two thought-streams have stemmed from independent and different soruces, and their courses too have been independent and different. It is true that several msters of the Viśishṭādvaita school of thought, like Yāmuna-muni (Āgama-prāmāṇya), Rāmānuja, Vedānta-deśika (Sacchāritra-raksha and Pāñcharātra-rakshā), Piḷḷai- Lokāchārya (Tattva- traya), Vara-vara-muni (his commentator and other have attempted to integrate Viśish-ṭādvaita ideas with those of Pāñcharātra, chief attractions in Pāñcharātra being the concept of Nārāyaṇa (or Vāsudeva) as the personal God s well as the supreme spirit, the recognition of Lakshmi as the energy (śakti) of Nārāyaṇa, and the importantce attached to worship and devotion as means of salvation. Some of the later Pāñcharātra works like Vishvaksena- and Vihagendra-saṁhitās and Lakshmī-tantra are also found to follow the lead of Viśishṭādvaita. But the position of pāñcharātra as may be gleaned by the early texts like Sāttvata-saṁhitā, Jayāk-hya-saṁhitā and Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā is a distinct one.

Indeed, the Pāñcharātra system is not so much an independent philosophy as a thought – system justifying a compendium of practical prescriptions (hence the name sāttvata-vidhi), which relate to worship. Viśishṭādvaita, on the other hand, is an academic philosophical exercise, and is by; no means confined to matters of worship: it has its own logic, epistemology and ontology. And it seeks to justify its position entirely on Vedic authority. Pāñcharātra, however, claims not only its priorexistence as ekāyana-veda (or mūla-veda) to the four vedas (which were called vikāra- vedas) but greater validity to itself. Although Yāmuna-muni conferred on Pāñcharātra the distinction of being ‘the fifth Veda’ and Vedānta-deśika referred to Pāñcharātra as ‘ekāyana-śurti’, the early Pāñcharātra texts them salved were indifferent to the Vedic claims. Śāṇdillya is held out as an instance of those who were dissatisfied and disillusioned with the Vedic tradition, and found fullfilment in the Sāttvata-vidhi.

It must be noted that the Pāñcharātra ideology had its beginnings even the epic Mahābhārata (Nārāyṇīya section), and was prevalent long before Viśishṭādvaita was crystallilzed as a philosophical discilpline. Even during the daays of Śaṁkara (632-664), Pāñcharātra was a well-defined system of thought, for he refers to pāñcharātra-siddhānta’ in his comments upon Vedānta-sūtra, 2,2,44 and discusses its chief tenets. Utpala-Vaishṇava (not to be confused with an other Kāshmīri writer Utpala-deva, the author of Pratyabhijḥñā-hṛdaya, and disciple of somānaandra, the author of Śiva-dṛshṭi), who was a contemporary of Abhinava-gupta (993-1015 A.D. ), knew of Pāñcharātra-sruti, Pāñcharātropanishat and Pāñcharātra-sāstra (cf. Spanda-pradīpikā); he also lcities from Pāñcharātra works like Sāttvra-, Jayākhya-, Pārameśvara-, Paushkara, and Vaihāyasa-saṁhitās).

Yāmuna-muni, who was an early architect of Viśishṭādvaita school of thought, himself quote iln his Āgama-prāmāṇya several Pāñcharātra works like Īśvara-, Śāṇḍilya-, Parama-, and Sanatkumāra-saṁhitās. Likewise Rāmanuja quoted form Sāttvata-, Paushkara- and Parama-saṁhitās and from Lakshmī-tantra. Ilt does obvious therefore that the Pāñcharātra idelogy was already well-defined before Viśishṭādvaita philosophy gained ground. And it had been by then regarded as lof lequal status with the orthodox Vedic tradition. Hence Utpala-vaishṇava’s description of Pāñcharātra as ‘śruti’ (which was the scripture that enables lus to understand the maker of that scripture, ‘śāstreṇa śāstā avangantavyah’ nd it was like kl;the stairs to climb on to the terrace, or like the raft that takes us across the river, (‘sopānena prāsādam ārohet, plavena vā nadīm taret’), as ‘upanishad’ (in the sense of secret and original teaching which existed prior to the divvision of one Veda into three or four Vedas, ‘ekāyana śākhaiva mūla-veda iit’) and as ‘śāstra’ (brought into being by Lord himself, ‘bhagavatā svayam praṇītam’)

We get frequent references in the’Nārāyaṇīa’ section of the epic Mahābhārata that Sāṁkhya-yoga was principally involved in Pāñcharātra texts themselves align their thought-content to the Sāṁkhya-yoga complex. It is also said that the Sāṁkhya system recognizes these three kinds of knowledge. The first is the knowledge about the eight- fold material prakṛti, whereas the second is the knowledge of the purusha as the higher and spiritual principle; the third is the wisdom that the two are in essence one only.

It is interesting that the Kāshmīri work of the eleventh century, Kshemarāja’s Pratyabhijñā-hṛdaya makes a mention of the Pāñcharātra ideology in terms of the transcendental prakṛti being Vāsudeva, and of the evolutionary modifications of prakrti presupposing the ‘unmanifest’ state as well as the spark-like emanations which are individual souls.

In Ahirbudhynya-saṁhitā, the ‘unmanifest’ (vyakta) means ‘ātrā’, ‘paramātmā’ or Vāsudeva. And we read Jayākhya-saṁhitā about the transcendental prakṛti being of the nature of consciousness and being undifferentiated from Brahman (3,14)

The Sāṁkhya involvement in Pāñcharātra ideology can be seen in several details like the evolutionary processes based on prakṛti and consisting of the play of guṇas (brahma-sarga, śuddha-sṛshṭI as guṇonmesha and miśra-sṛshṭi etc) the primary and secondary emanations on the basis of guṇas (vyūha, vyūhāntara), glorious and purposeful manifestations (vibhava), differential conglomerations of guṇas, the concept of ‘sudarśana’ as the initial divine spurt of dynamic consciousness, the role of Lakshmī as the energy inherent in and inseparable form the purusha, lthe rationale of nityodita and śāntodita phases and the pradyumna stage marking the cleavage between purusha and prakṛti.

It may be recalled that Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā (12,4-54) speaks of five disciplines which ;are of primary significance (śāstraṇi mūla-bhūni): the Vedic trad, the Sāṁkhya, the Yoga, the Pāśupata and the Sāttvata. The role of the first discipline in Pāñcharātrra is more formal than actual. The second provides the theoretical foundation, and the thrid the practical approch to the highest objective. The importance of the fourth however is not sufficiently recognized. The Pāñcharātra ideology has borrowed numerous concepts which a re pecular to theŚaiva āgama, like the ‘pañcha-kṛtyas’ (viz. creation, preservation, dissolution, obscuration and grace, cf. Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā, 14,15-16) and the’mala-traya’ (the three stains: āṇava, kiṁchit-kara and kiṁchij-jñatva, cf. Ibid., 14,17-20)

The fifth discipline, known to the text as Sāttvata (12,3) or Pāñchrātra is mentio;ned at the end, probably as the culmination of the series, for the Vedic triad (trayī) which figures at the beginning of the series is expressly stated as the ‘first form’ (‘trayī-mayam rūpam ādyam’, 12,5). The order of enumeration sugests a progressive unfoldment of the Pāñcharātra system. The Sāṁkhya school which is the second incorporates the first, viz. Vedic triadm and deveops into a more effective discipline. The third, viz. the Yoga, borrows its theoretical framework from the second vviz. ## The Ten Branches

The comprehensive character of the last system is supposed to be indicated by the “ten branches” into which it ramifies (‘bhedo daśa-vidhas tasya’, 12,45). Each of these branches is called a ‘saṁhitā’, which expression does not in this context refer to text 9tantra) but to a connected account of a specific topic. The following are the ten topics that the text enumerates; but the mention is all too brief, as the text itself acknowledges (‘saṁkshepeṇa prakīritah’, op.cit.) and it is difficult to ascertain precisely what the names signify. But accquaintance with the text in its entirety as well as with the Sāttvata-saṁhitā on which this text appears to be based, will suggest what the ten areas most probably are.

(1) The first theme of the Sāttvata system is the ‘Bhagavat-saṁhitā’. It is obviously the discussion about the nature of Godhead , which is known to the bhāgavata cult as the ‘bhagavat’ (possessor of ‘bhaga’ which denotes the manifested configuration of six abstract aspects of divinity: jñāna, śakti, bala, aiśvrya, vīrya and tejas, lwhich will be explained later ). (cf. Vāmana-saṁhitā, 33):

The topic is of foremost importance, as its relevance is with the very secret and purpose of life in g eneral, and with the goal of a Sāttvta devotee in particular. It merits an elaborate consideration and will be dealt with in the next section. It suffices to note here that the bhagavat is considered here not only as an absolute, indescribable, and transcendental Brahman (of the Vedāntins) but also as a personal god, worthy of adoration and worship. The ideas of ‘nityoditya’ and śāntodita’ refer to these two contexts; and we also have interesting formulations concerning the forms assumed by the personl god (vyūhas, prā-durbhāvas, vibhavas, archā-mūrits etc)

(2) The second topic is styled ‘Karma-saṁhitā’. The text later (15,7) points out that thirteen are the types of action (karma), according to the gunas, which are resorted to by the adherents of ‘pañcha-kāla’ procedure:

The topic covers supposedly the entire conduct of the Sāttvata as a person and as a devotee. It is, however, difficult to identify what the thirteen types of conduct mentio;ned here are.

Alternately, the topic may be the processes of creation (sarga),. Which re responsible for the world, in the sense of the work of the Godhead.

(3) The next topic is ‘Vidya-mayī-saṁhitā’. Vidyā is systematized and spiritual knowledge, and although it is frequently used as a synonym of ‘jñana’, there is a technical difference. We read, for instance, in Vishṇu-tattva-saṁhitā (chap. 32, ‘kuṁbhārchanā-vidhi’, in the MSS in my possession):

Vidyā is identified with the energy of the Godhead, which works within the individual, for producing knowledge. Among the vyūhas, the Saṁkarshaṇa aspect of Godhead bestows the knowledge of the scriptures (śāstra-jñāna), the Pradyumna aspect the knowledge of the practice (anushṭhāna-mārga), and the Anirudha aspect the benefits of such knowledge and practice (kriyā-phala). Sāttvata-saṁhitā (3,3) also speak of jñāna and vidya as different: jñāna-vidyā-chatushkeṇa’. Hence the difinition of ‘jñāna’ as vidyā-maya’, and not as mere ‘vidyā’.

The topic may probably refer to the three vyūhas which are collectively responsible for the Sāttvata discipline. We will consider this topic while discussing about the Bhagavat in the text chapter.

Otto Schrader, however, takes the word vidyā to mean the knowledge of prakṛti or of the seven categories of knowledge (Intorduction to the Pāñcharātra, Adyār, Madras, 1916, reprinted 1973,p.128). He appears to take the cue form a statement in the text (15, 12-13) which speaks of the knowers of the seven categories (sapta-padārtha-jñāh) who ae also endowed with the knowledge of prakrti (prakṛti-jñaāna-saṁpannāh). The seven well-known categories of the Vviśeshika system (dravya, guṇa, karma, sāmānya, samavāya and abhāva, cf. Bhāsha-parichheda, 2,14) have relevance in this context. Besides, Ahirbudhynya-saṁhitā(7,45-46) speaks of the seven modifications which result form the will of God (‘vidyā-vipariṇāmino-yam saptadhā vīkshayā hareh’) as the ‘great elements’ (‘mahābhūtāni tāny āhuh’). It may, therefore, be supposed that vidyā here refers to the knowledge of prakṛti, which is the source of the world (7,1 ‘tat sāṁkhyair jagato mūlam prakṛtiś cheti kathyate’). But the’prakṛti’ here must be taken as the five ‘mahābhūtas’ (or tanmātras of earth, water, fire, air, and ākāśa) together with prāṇa and buddhi. Altermately, it may refer to the four vyūhas which are productive, and three products, buddhi (sāttvika), prāṇa (rājasika) and kāla (tāmasika)(cf. Lakshmī-tantra, 16,2-4).

It may also be noted that in Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā (7.8) Vidyā is a synonoym of the principle of mahat (of the Sāṁkhyans) which evolves from prakṛti and is of the nature of the triad: time, consciousness and life (‘kālo buddhis thatā prāṇa iti tredhā sāgīyate’,7.1). Further, it has four forms: adharma, ajñāna, avairagya and anaiśvarya. These four forms and the three aspects together make seven details of vidyā or mahat.

(4) ‘Kāla-saṁhitā’ refers to the jlprinciple of time. Otto Schrader appears to have erred in construing this topic as related to ‘pañcha-kāla-vidhi’ (cf. Op.cit., p.128). Kāla as the principle of time is an important topic, and has been dealt with in many pāñcharātra texts. This text itself looks upon time as that which cuses the disturbance in the guṇas (6,51-52), which as the power of Vishṇu ‘Sudarśana’ (to be explained later) makes prakṛti and purusha evolve together (7,6, ‘kālah pachati tattve dve prakṛtim purusham cha’): it originates from Niyati (all-regulating subtle power of Vishṇu ) and is the origin; of guṇas (4,48); prompted by; the will of Vishṇu, it is the one principle which brings about everything in the universe (6,50 ‘kālas sa kalayaty eko vishṇu-saṁkalpa-choditah’), in an orderly fashion (‘sopāna-kramatah’, 6,52).

(5) The fifth topic is ‘karatvya-saṁhitā’ referring to what ought to be done as a duty. This obviously is worship (‘kartavyatvena hareh pūjā’), along with mediation (sāttvata-saṁhitā, 17,148 ‘kartvyam sajapam dhyānam nityam ārādhakena tu’) and it must be carried out as a duty (bid., 7,10 ‘dartavvyam iti vai karma tvaiśvaryam yas samācharet’). This worship is described as daily and imperative (nitya), and must be distinguished from what follows (viz. the special and occasional worship rituals in a temple). All pāñcharātra texts contain elaborate accounts of how the daily worship is to be conducted, either as a detail of kriyā-pada or of charyā-pāda in a standard āgama text.

(6) ‘Vaiśeshika-kriyā is the topic relating to special ceremonials conducted in a temple like pratishṭhā, yāga, bālālaya, the various kālotsavas, pavitrotsava, adhyayanotsava, mṛgayā-yātrā, prāyaśchitta, jīrṇoddhāra, saṁmārjana and saṁprokshaṇa.

Schrader has again gone wrong in taking the term ‘vaiśeshika to mean ‘special duties connected with the several castes and stages of life (op.cit.p.129). Most of the Pāñcharātra texts do not deal with what describes as ‘special duties’: but all of them contain details of the ceremonials that are special and occasional. Schrader is wrong also when he attempts to r ead the meaning of five ceremonies or sacraments in the e xpression ‘kartavya’ (the previous topic).

(7) ‘Saṁyama-saṁhitā’ and (8) ‘Chintā-saṁhitā’ are the topics that pertain to the yogic practices that are prescribed in Pāñcharātra tezxts. That the yoga system has been accepted by the Pāñcharātrins as one of the five basic disciplines (12,3) has already been mentioned. The great prescriptions of yoga (mahad- yogānuśāsanam) are of two kinds: the yo;ga of restraints (nirodha-yoga) and the yoga of action (karma-yoga)(12,31-33). Saṁyama relates to the former division. Bhagavad-gītā (especially chapter 6) emphasizes the value of yoga: and Arjuna is told to become a yogi (‘tasmād yogībhavārjuna’). Several Pāñcharātra texts contain elaborate accounts of the eight fold yoga practices, usually while dealing with ‘pañcha-kāla-vidhi’.

‘Chintā-saṁhitā’ relates to contemplation (dhyāna), visualizations (chintana), ritualistic placements (nyāsa), and projection of the object of devotion upon the devotee himself, as for instance, in Vishṇu-tattva-saṁhitā (chap.32)

This is also called ‘antar-yāga’ (‘internal or mental sacrifices’), as distinguished from the usual sacrificial rituals conducted extermally and with materials gathered for the purpose (bahir-yajña). It may be recalled that Bhagavad-gītā also distinguishes ‘jñāna-yajña’ (actual physical events).

This topic is the burden of several important Pāñcharātra texts, like Sāttvata- (4,6,9,13,21 etc), Jayākhya- (Chap.33), Īśvara- (Chap.21), Parama- (Chap.10), Śrī-praśna-(Chap.17), Ahirbudhnya- (Chap.31-32) and Nāradīya-(chap.30) saṁhitās.

This text distinguishes between ‘jñana-yoga’ (where it is knowledge tht steadies the mind) and ‘karma- yoga (actions bringing steadiness to the mind); and oth are said to lead to ‘the well-known highest foothold of Vishṇu (‘sa tat-padam āpnotiyad- vishṇoh prathitam pdam’, op.cit.10,9). Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā, however, identifies the Sāttvata-yoga with the ashṭāṅga-yoga, (31,15 ‘ashṭāṅga esha kathito’), and names all the eight limbsthat we find in; Patañjali’s Yoga-sūtra (op.cit. 16-17). Yoga in Pāñcharātra texts generally follows this approach.

## Bondage And Liberation

Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā(chap. 14) explains at great length the Sāttvata outlook on bondage and liberation. The individual soul by its nature is immortal, ilndivisible, blissful and of the nature of consciousness; it is largely divine in nature (‘bhagavan-maya evāyam’, op.cit.14,6) and its existence, nature and course are entirely and always dependent upon God (‘bhagavad-bhāvitās-sadā-, ibid.). Its form (ākāra), potency (aiśvarya) and knowledge (vijñāna) become attenuated and obscured owing to the ‘nigraha’ power of God (one of the five powers, others being creation, preservation and destruction of the phenomenal world, and compassionate succour offered to the individual souls, anugraha, see later). The soul thereupon becomes attenuated in size (aṇutva), impotent (akiñchit-karatvv) and ignorant (akiñchij- jñatva) (bid.,16-17). These three lilmitations are the three fetters which bind the soul to the transactional world (‘mala- trayam idam proktam bandha-trayam idam budhaih’, 20). It is God’s māyā-śakti and kalā which serve to introduce lilmitations in soul’s nture, and involve it in bondage.

But there is another power of God (anugrha-śakti), which prompts God to look with compassion upon the deserving soul and accept it (14, 31-32):

The descent of this power (‘śakti-pāta’) uplifts the soul and frees it from the transmigrational fetters (‘jīvam uttāryati saṁsṛthe’, 14,33). When the descent of God’s power upon the soul occurs, the soul longs for liberation, becomes, dispassionate and sets abo;ut to discren the real from the unreal; reflecting upon the scriptures, serving a master, practising Sāṁkhya-yoga, engaged in doing good deeds, perfectly disciplined and becoming steady in the knowledge of Vedānta, he attains the foothold of Vishṇu after great effort as a result of wisdom or jñāna (14,36-40)

The Pāñcharātra theory holds that at the time of the great dissolution (prralaya) all the individual souls merge with Godhead, but do not become one with it. They retain their indentity in potentiality, and when the new phase of creation begins, they all come out separately. But the souls which; have been liberated by; the methods detailed above, also merge with God, but never again come out form this source. They do not, however, become one with God; they remain distinct from each other and from God. Their lib;eration is spoken of as entry into godhead (‘viśet padam’); and godhead is actually a plane of divine presence and divine experience (bhagavat-sānnidhya, bhagavad-anubhava). Actually, it is an entrance into the power of God known as Saudarśana (an aspect of lakshmi) (cf. Ibid. 51,69-78). But jayākhay-saṁhitā holds tht the consciousness (jñāna) aspect of the liberated soul will actually become one with the Brahman.

Merging of the soul with the absolute (Godhead, Vāsudeva) is the meaning of liberation. A verse quoted without, however, specifying the source (yathāhuh) by Vedānta-deśika in his Sacchāritra- rakshā (op.cit.p.52, 1stadhikāra) speaks of sālokya (residence in the same plane as Godhead), sāmīpya (abiding in close proxmity to Godhead), sādṛśya (attaining the same form as Godhead) and sāyuja(union with Godhead) forms of liberation; but the vese in definite about the last form being real liberation.

According to Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā (14,41), liberation is consequent upon correct knowledge and purification of the heart.

## The Three Principal Modes

The Pāñcharātra philosophy is distinguished by its conception of the Deity assuming, according to different texts, three, four or five ‘modes of being’ (called prakāras) or principal varieties of forms. The three modes, recognized in early; texts (like Sāttvata-saṁhitā and Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā, are the transcendental (para), the emanatory (vyūja) and the incarnatory (vibhava), to which are added (in other texts like Śrī-praśna-saṁhitā) the immanent (antaryāmi or hārda, the inner controller residing in the heart) as the fourth mode and the iconic (archā) as the fifth.

(Śrī-praśna-saṁhitā, 2,54)

(Vishvaksena-saṁhitā)

The explanation offered in the former text is brief; the transcendental and the emanatory forms are in the high heavens, known directly only to the immutable (nitya-sūri) and emancipated souls (mukta) (‘vaikuṇṭhesminpara-vyūhosasā paśyanti sūrayah’). The incamations are forms on earth , seen by those who lilved contemporatneously (‘avatāreshu tat-kāla paśyantopi janā bhuvi’); the Deity as the inner controller abides in the heart of all beings, but visualized only by sages in meditational spells (‘yoga-tattvena munayas sadā māmdhyāna-gocharam, paśyanti hṛn-madhye’).

The enunciation of the nature and functions of these modes of the Deity is said to be what the Pāñcharātra system is concerned with.

## The Transcendental Mode

In the para or transcendental mode, the Deity is like an ocean of nectar altogether devoid of waves (nis-tarṅgāmṛtāṁ-bodhi-kalpam), quiet, profound, still, limitless and unfathomable (sarvatas śānta evāsau nir-vikārah sanātanah); it is a mass of consciousness (chik-ghana), unfragmented by space or time (deśa-kālādi-paricchheda-varjita). It is figuratively spoken of as the transcendental reaim (paramam dhāma), the supreme foothold of the Deity (vishṇoh paramam padam), the high; heavens (śrī-vaikuṇṭha), or the g reat void (paramaa-vyoma). It is the plane where knowledge and bliss abound lunobstructed (jñānānanda-maya-loka), and which is peopled by; the leternal ‘masters’ (nitya-sūris, like Ananta, Garuḍa and Vishvaksena) who are never involved lat lany time transmigration and are untouched by this world of transaction (‘kadāpi saṁsāramaprāptāh’, aspṛshṭa-saṁsāra-gandhāh’) and the emancipated souls (muktas), who were once mortals lbut who have now acquired divine bodies, mote-like in size but dazzling with brilliance of a million ray (‘trasa-renu-pramānās te raśmi-koṭI-vibhūshitāh’). These denizens of the transcendental realm are free from all physical and mental ailments and enjoy blilss which is unadulterated; they see the Deity all the time.

(Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā, 9,30)

He is verily the brahman (tad-brahma), one and secondless, and his energy; (śakti) which is responsible lfor lthe lorgin, maintenance and dissolution of the world is altogether indistinguishable from him in this state, (‘apṛhag-bhūta-śakti-tvāt’). The energy that brings about the phenonenal appearance and events is described as Lakshmī or Śrī (‘para-brahma-śakti-bhūtā śrīh); and his creative energy is to the Deity as moonlight is to the moon (‘jyotsneva chandramasah’). The Pāñcharātra texts speak of Lakshmī who ils perceived only as this world (jagattayālakshyamāṇā) as the consort of Nārāyaṇa, and as one who ils inseparable from him; hence the expression Lakshmī-nārāyaṇa for the Deity in the transcendental plane

Lakshmī in the early texts is little more than the energy of brahman, which when the Brahman wills to create appears like a flash of lightning lin the sky (vidyud iva vyomni kvachid udyotate tu sā). It is movement (kriyā) and it becomes and assumes all forms in existence (bhūti). Movement is like thread which runs through the beads and holds them together. The will of the Deity which is free and unconditioned express, iltself as the conscious urge to create and become many and this is known to the Pāñcharātra texts as Su-darśana (‘prekshaṇātmā sa saṁkalpas tat sudarśanam uchyate’), the first aspect of Lakshmī which provides impetus and movement (‘lakshmyās saudarśanī kalā’). It is this which makes all existence possible, even as lthe wind lprompts the flame to rise or the lwinds to gather (‘samīryate yathsā vahnir megho vā pi saīraṇāt). The second aspect of Lakshmī (viz. bhūti-śaktri) is the material cause for all phenomenal existence; indeed, the worlds ils only a transformation of the energy that is Lakshṁi.

While the early texts (like Ahirbudhnya-saṁhitā and Sāttvata-saṁhitā) mention only Lakshmī as the consort (viz. Śakti, energy or power) of the Deity in the transcendental state, later texts (like Pādma-saṁhitā and Pārameśvara-saṁhitā) provide both Lakshmī and Bhū (or PushṭI) as consorts, land still later, Nīlā was added (in texts like Vihagendra- saṁhitā). ## The Vyūha Modes

The second mode of the Deity’s being, viz. Vyuha, emphasizes the differential manifesttions nd functions of the six attributes. The expression ‘vyūha’ suggests the creative configurations of the attributes (guṇa-vyūha) for the sake of creation and evolution of the world, for maintaining the world protecting the devotees, and for leading the devotees to salvation. The sixth attributes are in two functionally ldistinct planes: (1) jñāna, (2) aiśvarya and(3) śakti constituting the planes of rest (viśrāma-bhūmayah), connected with the bhūti-śakti aspect of Lakshmī, and (4) bala , (5) vīrya and (6) tejas constituting the planes of activity (śrama-bhūmayah), connected with the kriyā-śakti aspect of Lakshmī. Even when all six attributes are operative, there is a tendency for the attributes characteristic of the two planes to pair off. Thus we have three configurtions, and Vāsudeva appears as three sequential emanations from these configurations (guṇa-vyatikarodbhava) as follows:

(1) jñna + (4) bala -: Saṁkarshaṇa  
(2) aiśvarya + (5) vīrya -: Prayumna  
(3) śakti + (6) tejas -: Aniruddha

These are known as vyūha-rūpas. And usually Vāsudeva, who has all the six attributes in their fulness and equal dominance, and who brings into being Saṁkrshaṇa directly and the other two forms indirectly, is also counted as a vyūha, thus mking the number of vyūhas four. Some texts distinguish between Para-Vāsudeva (viz. Vāsudeva in the transcedental mode) and Vyūha-Vāsudeva (Vāsudeva in the vyūha mode). They are two stages (daśā-dvaya) of the same Deity, the former being beyond the vyūha configurations (turya) while the latter is involved in them. In both stages, the six attributes are present in their fulness, but in the first stage they are still (samita) whereas in the second they are active (prabuddha).

Vāsudeva in the transcendental mode is described as the seed of all the subsequent forms, emanations and incarmations (‘mūrtīnām bījam avyayam’, Vihagendra-saṁhitā); he is devoid of aspects (nishkala) and without descernible form (nirākārra, niraṅga). He is static like an ocean which is absolutely still (aspanda- lakshaṇa, alolībhūta). The vyūha-Vāsudeva is dynamic and cuses the differential configurations of the six attributes. He it is that asumes the three vyūha-forms.

But some texts like Vishvaksena-saṁhitā distinguish between Parātpara-Vāsudeva who is formless and beyond the Vyūha-configurations and para-Vāsudev who is involved in the Vyūha configurations as ‘turya-vyūha’ (the vyūha beyond the other three). The context is the identification of the three vyūha-forms: Aniruddha, Pradyumna and Saṁkrshaṇa, with the three states: wakefulness (jāgrat), dream (svapna) and deep sleep (sushupti) (cf. Sāttvta-saṁhitā, chap. 2ff). The distinction between the two stages, whether it is between Parātpara-Vāsudeva and Para- Vāsudeva or between Para-Vāsudeva and Vyūha-Vāsudeva, is actually between the ‘nityodita’ (ever manifest’) and the ‘śāntodit’ (periodiclly manifest) conditions, the former characterized by the Deity’s experience of its own glory (sva-vibhūtyanubhava) while the latter by the experience only of the Deity’s own self (svātma-mātrānubhava), the former being the source (or cause) of the latter.

## Chapter VI - Pāñcharātra Practice

## The Idea Of Yāga

The recognition of the three modes of the Deity (para, vyūha and vibhava) becomes important in the context of iconic worship and for purpose of initiation into Pāñcharātra practice (dīkshā). In common with other Āgamas, pāñcharātra concedes the need for worshipping the Deity in concrete forms, and the need for o;btaining proper eligibility for such worship, especially for the ske of others (parārtha).

Worship may possibly be conducted in one of four ‘seates’ (chatus-sthānārchanā): in an icon (mūrtau, biṁbe), in the fire on the altar (vedyām agnau), in a maṇḍala (maṇḍala-madhye) or in water (viz. in a ater-stretch. Jalāśaye or tīrtha-madhye). Alternately, the four ‘seats’ are: ones own heart (sva-hṛt- padme), the sacred water-pot (kalaśe), the sun (sūrye), and the icon. Details of worship differ according to the ‘seat chosen. And the choice of the ‘seat’ depends upon the intellectual and spiritual equipment of the devotees. Ritualistic worship of the fire klpresupposes eligibility in accordance with varṇa and āśrama; worship of the Deity in ones heart is impossible for those who are spiritually not advanced. Worship in a maṇḍala required proper consecration. But iconic worship is the easiest and the safest method of worship for all. We read in vishnū-tattva-saṁhitā (charyā, 4,5):

The expression ‘worship’ (archanā) is strictly not applicable in cases where Godhead is viewed as devoid of form. Approaches like adoration, eulogy, contemplation and formal sequence of worship become relevant only when Godhead is conceived of as having a discernible form. This emphasizes the importance of iconic worship among different forms of spirituals practices.

The word ‘yāga’ in Āgama refers to worship of the Deity in any of the four ‘seats’ mentioned above (‘biṁbādishu bhagavad- archanam’). But two broad vrieties of yāga are recognized; (1) bahir-yāga, and (2) antar-yāga. The former is external worship, involving extraneous and material objects the approach is physical (karmaṇā), verbal (girā) and mental (manasā). It is mainly meant to eradicate the defects born out of lthe klplay of ltwo gunas, rajas and tamas. Iconic worship in the temple or at home belongs to this variety. Godhead is represented in visible and gross form, fashioned in stone, metal or clay, or drawn on paper. Elaborate rituals involving action are resorted to. This is the path of actions (Kriyā-yoga), but of actions that lead not to bondage but to salvation (karmaṇām eva mochakatvam)

(Bhāgvata, 1,318)

However, antar-yāga is distinguished by an inward orientation. ## The Six Planes And Initiation

The procedures and rationale of initiation (dīkshā) and consecrrtion (abhisheka) into Pānchrātra worship also involve, as said earlier, the conception of the three modes of the Deity (para, vyūha and vibhava). In t common with Śaiva-āgama, pāñcharātra relies heavily on formal initiation for passage through the ‘six plnes or paths’ (adhava-shaṭka) and for the eligibility to engage in inner worwship (antaryāga) and external worship (bahir-yāga). The concept of six ‘paths’ or ‘planes of existence’ is also fmiliar to the Śaiva-āgama. The lowest plane is the ordinary world with its objects and enjoyments, and for one to rise above it, linitiation is indispensable. And unless one rises above this plane, the obtainment of salvation is impossible.

The six planes are regarded as the transformation of Lakshmī or energy of Para-vāsudeva (viz.param brahma) (‘prabhavāmi shaḍ- adhvanā’, Lakshmī-tantra, 22,10): varṇa, Kalāmaya, tāttvika, māntrika, pādika and bhauvana (bid.,10-11).

(1) Varṇādhvā is the first arousal (unmesha) from the primal and undifferentiated consciousness. Sāttvata-saṁhitā (19,128-129) explains that the great lord (parameśvara, viz. param brahma) assumes this form for the sake of bringing about salvation for all beings (‘muktaye-khila-jīvānām’). This form is ‘śabda- brahma’, initially unmanifest (avyakta) but later manifest as the letters of the alphabet (varṇa, from ).

(2) Kalādhvā is the subsequent stage of evolution. The expression ‘kalā’ refers to the six divine attributes (guṇas headed by; jñāna) already explailned.

(3) With these two planes as basis, the next stage of transformation (vivarta) is tattvādhavā, the realm of categories, which here refer to the forms assumed by the Deity: Vāsudeva and other vyūhas, Padmanābha and other vibhavas, and whatever other forms that represent the Deity:

(4) From the tattvas evolve the mantras (‘tattvebhyo nirgatā mantrāh’, Sāttvata-saṁhitā), which are without count. According to Lakshmī-tantra, this has the prior tow paths as its basis and comes into being for rescuing the beings from the ocean of transmigration. The path of mantra helps ordinary people enjoy the world as well as develop the feeling of dispassion; it provides the sure foundatio (mental crutch, mānasālaṁbana) for successful worship by the devotees. All the mantras are alive and alert, effective and pervasive, and become trasnformed into the forms of the Deity. They are in fact of the nature of Lakshmī, the energy of godhead.

The text (22,21) derives the word mantra from the two roots’man’ and ‘trai’ and e xplains that mantras protect the person who thinks about them (‘trāyamāṇaś cha mantāram’), and that they are meant to secure happiness here and salvation hereafter (‘bhogāpavargadā hy ete’). They are the very forms of the Deity.

(5) From the plane of mantra evolves the plane of the normal states of human experience (pādādhvā), to wit wakefulness, dream and deep sleep (excluding the turya or the state beyond deep sleep)

(6) The final evolute is the plane of worldly existence (Bhuvanādhavā) consisting of fourteen worlds and composed of the three gunas (sattva, rajas and tamas); comprehending all objects of enjoyment.

(Sāttvata-saṁhitā, 19,136)

The six planes of existence are characterized by the quality of r ejection-and-acceptance (‘adhva-shaṭkam heyopādeya lakshaṇam’). The last two are to be rejected (heya) for they are impure (aśuddha) and filled with stin (mala-paṅkila). They are described as products of delusions (māyīyādhava-dvaya); they attract the ordinary beings to the world of senseory experience and sensual pleasure. The other four planes are to be accepted, for they lead one to salvation in gradual measures.

The role of initiation consists in strengthening the candidate’s aversion to the first two planes and pursuance of the other four planes. One who has developed deep dispassion for normal pleasures of life is drawn into the planeof mantras; and the mantras by their own power (sva-śaktyā) lead him to the plane of categories (tattvādhva), viz. the various forms of Vāsudeva. In due course he moves on to the plans of divine attributes (kalādhvā), and finally to the plane of sabda-brahman (varnādhvā). By understnding the nature of sadba-brahman (who in reality is the para-vāsudeva orparam brahma) he realilses the supreme and tranquil state of vishṇu, viz. salvtion (‘prāpnoti tat-parijñānan, suśātam paramam padam’, Sāttvata-saṁhitā, 19,150).

The cleansing of the plane of worldly existence (bhuvanādhva- pariśodhana) marks the intital equipment for initiation. It is likened to the release of the caged bird, which will then fly upwards

Initiation is of three varieties (cf.ibid., 19,4), in accordance with what one looks for.

(1) Para-dīkshā (also called sūkshma-dīkshā or brahma-dīkshā) which will lultimately lead to salvation (kaivalya-phla-dā);

(2) Vyūha-dīskshā (also called chaturātmīya), calculated to accomplish worldly welfare as well as salvation (bhogakaivalyadā); and

(3) Vaibhava-dīkshā (or vibhava-dīkshā), which will accomplish only worldly welfare (bhogadā).

Worldly welfare and salvation are both normal aspirations and the Āgama recognizes the valildity of lthis position. It is therefore that Sāttvata-saṁhitā(16,40) suggest that the three initiations must be resorted to by all those who seek prosperity here and emancipation hereafter (‘nihśreyasa-vibhūtyarthārtham grāhyam dīkshā-trayam’). And the same text makes all people without any discrimination or reservation eligible for initiation of whatever variety: devotees, advanced souls, old people, women, children and ignorant folk (19,5-6)

While the ritualistic sequences are elaborate and involve considerable expense, there are three procedures suggested: Those who are wealthy are to be initiated by employing the procedure of mahā-maṇḍala-yāga, which is both elaborate and expensive. But those who are not wealthy my be initiated only by a simple fire-ritual. And those who are poor and indigent may be initiated by a mere word of mouth.

All inititions, however, are of equal merit, and they cleanse the candidate of all the sins accumulated in all the previous births. However, a prior period ofpractice spent in sense restraint and mind-control will be an asset, as this practice will remove the stains of body, mind and speech relevant to this birth, and thus prepare him to receive initiation which will remove the stains acquired in previous births.

The initial initiation is into the mantra specific to one of the vibhava-forms of the Deity, preferably but not necessarily Narrsimha: whatever among thethirty-eight forms appeals to the candidate may be selected, and the appropriate mantra communicated to the candidate (‘vaibhava-mantreshu yasya yasmin abhiruchis tenaiva tat kāryam’, comm. On above). The next higher initiation employs the vyūha-forms, and thefinal initiation the transcendal form (paarā).

Initiatory rites involve five consecrations (pañcha-saṁskāra):

(1) branding of heated metallic emblems of Vishṇu, viz. discus and conch, on the arms of the devotee (tāpa, taptamudrāṅkana); (2) the wearing of marks on the forehead signifying devotion of vishṇu (puṇḍra); (3) the bestowal of one of the names of Vishṇu (nāma); (4) the formal communication of the selected mantra (mantra for recitation and contempltion); and (5) fire-rituals (yāga).

Of these, the ritualistic branding of the metallic emblem of discus on the arms is of great importance, forone devoid of this ceremony is incapable of seeking refuge in Vishṇu:

The emblem is made in an alloy of five metls (‘pañcha-loha-mayam chakram’), and is heated on sacred fire for purposes of consecration with appropriate mantras and duly worshipped. The branding of this on the devotee will transform him into a ‘Vaishṇava’ (a devotee belonging entirely to Vishṇu)

Only when this ceremony is gone through, the candidate becomes fit for receiving initiation.

It is to be noted that in later Pāñcharātra texts, the explanations of the six planes (shaḍ-adhva) and the prescriptions for initiations are given quite differently. For instance, we find that the six planes mentioned in Śrī-prraśna-saṁhitā (16,97-98) take this order and context: Kālādhava (referring to time, the fourteen Manus, the four yugas, samvatsara, ayana, ṛtu, māsa etc.), Pādāhvā (the seven worlds below satya-lokam, tapo-loka, jano-loka ect), Tattvādhvā (twenty –five categories or existence), Varṇādhvā (the four cstes, Brāhmaṇa and so on ) and Mantrādhvā (the varieties of mantra like udgītha, ashṭārṇa, manu and shaḍarṇa).

The same text (16, 17-20) explains initiation (dīkshā) as that which destroys the transmigratory world and makes for the perception of the supreme foothold of Vishṇu:

And classifies it ilnto three types: gorss (sthūla), subtle (sūkshma) and transcendental (parā) (16,23) relating them to the four grades of priests: samayī (eligible only for household worship, sthūla), putraka and sādhaka (sūkshma and madhyama) and āchārya (parā, qualified to take lup worship in a temple, and competent to provide initiation for others). Further, the initiation is related to the tantra, tantrāntara, and mantra siddhānta divisions. Initiations also differ according to the castes: bhāga (for brāhmaṇas, and kshattriyas), vardhanī (for vaiśyas) and maudaglī (for sūdras).

It may be noted the Tantra-sārāgama relies entirely on the religious philosophy crystalized by Ānanda-tīrtha (Madhvāchārya), and adopted by the Haridāsas of Karṇātaka (wandering ministreal who were also saints and lpoets). Worship (pūjā) in this Āgama takes two forms: (1) the worship of the material and inert icons mde by man to represent a form or attribue of the Deity (āhita) or of the natural objects which are divene in character (sahaja, like śālagrāma and gaṅgā); and (2) Worship of the incrnatory forms (like Aniruddha) by contemplating on their significance. Iconic worship at home or in a temple involves several rituals (mostlyin common with other Āgama practics) but more important than the rituals is the mental exercise that accompanies the rituals.

For instance, when an icon is worshipped, it is not taken s a mere icon. It is regrded as the ground (golaka) for three for five projections of the Deity which are mainly attitudinal. When employing śālagrāma for worship. It is not regarded as a ground for any projection, for it is itself a complete representtion of Godhead. When an icon instlled by gods or great sages in worshipped, then it is imagined to be the ground for three successive projections (golaka-traya): (1) that it is first of all a mterial object, visible to the eye; (2) that it is the seat of Mukhya-prāṇa, the chief prinicple of vitality, who is active in it and (3) that it is as the seat of Mukhya-prāṇa that this material object ispervaded by Lakshmī, who is the energy of the Deity. Ten icon is thus a material object, but not inert; it is alive and capable of bestwoing on the worshipper spiritual benefits.

When the icon that is sought to be worshipped is an ordinary one (viz. made by a human sculptor and instlled by; human beings) then the visualilzation of the ground (golaka-chintana) involves five projections: (1) that it is a material object, visible to the eye; (2) that it signifies the tradition of making worshipful icons instituted by the celestial craftsman Viśvakarmā, and that thus it represents in effect the handiwork of Visvakarmā himself; (3) that being fashioned by Viśvakarmā it provides the seat for Mukhya-prāṇa, theprinciple of life, and that thusitis alive and active; (4) that, inasmuch as it is the seat of Mukhya-prāṇa, it is pervded by Vāyu; the divinity empowered by Vishṇu to bestwo grace on the devotees; and (5) that, as pervded by; Vāyu, it represents the seat of the energy of Vishṇu, viz. Lakshmī; and that it signifies the presence of himself who is inseparably immanent in lakshmī.

This procedure is based on the doctrine of sequential immnence (anataryāmi); the material object is pervaded and governed by; Mukhya-prāṇa, who isimmagnnt it it; Lakshmī pervades and governs Mukhya-parāṇa, being immanent in the latter; and Vishṇu pervades and governs lakshmī as the ultimate, and independently immanent in all details.

The devotee beings by worshipping the inert icon (jaḍaprtimā) which is external (bāhya), and consecrated by the master (guru- datta), but which is also excellent in aspect (su-lakshāṇā). He sits before the icon, and gazes at it intently; and carries out the various sequences of the worship-ritual with the firm belief that it represents a form of the deity (pratimānttaragatarūpa). At length, the reflex image of the Icon gets fixed in his mind, and he will be able to see the form of the icon even in the absence of the icon (Vāsanāparoksha). And worship is now conducted in the mind only, installing the iconic form upon the heart-lotusand employing the mentalimages of the several articles that are normally used (like flowers, tulasi leaves, sandl paste, and unbroken rice). This practice enables lthe devotee to transform the mental imge of the external icon into the representtion of the form of his own bimba (biṁba-mūrti).

What follows is the deliberation that the bimba, which comprehends all forms of the Deity is the principle and active factor in the devotee’s own soul: that thus the biṁba it is that r eally worships and that the biṁba again is the seat of the Deity who is worshipped. The devotee loses thereupon the notion that he is feee and indepenent as a worshipper, and lays down what he till now considered his own resposbilility at the feet of the Deity. This is the culmintion of worhip.
